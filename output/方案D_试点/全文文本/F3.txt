A B S T R A C T  
Lithium‑sulfur batteries (LSBs) show a five times higher energy density of 2600 W h kg−1 than the conventional 
Li-ion battery cathodes, thus satisfying the growing energy demand. The main challenge in LSBs is the polysulfide 
shuttle effect, causing severe capacity fade. Combining physical and chemical interactions to capture polysulfides 
can improve the long-term cycling performance and high sulfur utilization in LSBs. This work represents a 
rational design of FeS2-reduced graphene oxide (RGO) with high surface area carbon and sulfur composite 
(FRHS) onto a 3-dimensional (3D)‑carbon fiber (CF) electrode as an efficient electrocatalyst and the conductive 
network for LSBs. The FRHS@CF electrodes deliver an excellent initial discharge capacity of 1165 mAh g−1 at 
100 mA g−1, and even at 500 mA g−1, it exhibits an initial discharge capacity of 740 mAh g−1 and maintains a 
stable capacity of 400 mAh g−1 with 73 % capacity retention at 325 cycles. The RGO and CF effectively shorten 
the path of Li-ion migration, improve electron transfer, and accommodate the volume effect of polysulfides with 
good electrolyte penetration. FeS2 effectively catalyzes the reaction kinetics of lithium polysulfides, thereby 
improving the reversibility with sulfur utilization.   
1. Introduction 
Rechargeable Li-ion batteries (LIBs) have gravimetric energy den­
sities of ~250 Wh kg−1. They are the choice of energy storage systems 
for various applications, including consumer electronics, telecommuni­
cations, grids, and even electric vehicles [1,2]. Conventional Li-ion 
batteries work by Li+ intercalation mechanisms. There is a demand in 
LIBs to force energy densities as high as ⁓ 500 Wh kg−1 and > 1000 Wh 
L−1, which is essential for portable electronics and electric vehicles 
[3,4]. The energy requirement in the LIB technology has been regained 
in the lithium‑sulfur battery (LSB), which could be a promising alter­
native to LIBs [5]. LSBs show a five times higher energy density (2600 
Wh kg−1 and 2800 Wh L−1) than conventional LIBs, satisfying the 
growing energy demand at a lower cost [6]. Sulfur is the most available 
element on the earth’s crust and is more environmentally friendly and 
cost-effective than conventional LIBs, delivering 1675 mAh g−1 theo­
retical capacity [7,8]. A lithium‑sulfur battery consists of sulfur as a 
cathode and Li metal as an anode. LSB can gain three times the energy 
density of 400–600 Wh kg−1 than the current LIBs [9,10]. Even though 
LSBs have a high theoretical capacity and energy density, their practical 
applications are still hindered due to their several challenges. The main 
issues that arise during the charge-discharge cycling of LSBs are pri­
marily due to the low electronic conductivity of sulfur (5 × 10−30 S 
cm−1) and its discharged products Li2S2/Li2S, which need the incorpo­
ration of conductive carbon additives to improve its conductivity. Be­
sides, the intermediate solubilized polysulfides formed during the 
conversion reaction account for the polysulfide shuttle effect, which 
results in the severe loss of active materials from the cathode, which 
corrodes the lithium metal. Finally, these structural changes cause ⁓ 80 
% volume expansion of the cathode upon cycling, resulting in poor 
mechanical stability and rapid capacity fading. These issues mentioned 
above gradually make LSB incapable, shortening the cycle life and 
reducing the coulombic efficiency and the overall energy density of the 
battery [11–13]. 
To uplift the practical application of LSBs, several research studies 
have been developed on improving the conductivity of sulfur by syn­
thesizing a composite of sulfur with conductive carbon additives [14], 
such as graphene, carbon nanotubes, and porous carbons. Nevertheless, 
carbon materials are nonpolar and have feeble interactions with the 
highly polar lithium polysulfides [15]. Therefore, polysulfides diffuse 
into the electrolyte due to the weak confinement of carbon-based ma­
terials, giving rise to more capacity decay and less capacity retention.

Recently, polar materials have been introduced into the carbon ad­
ditive to improve the interaction of polysulfide. In general, polar mol­
ecules interact through dipole-dipole interactions. The chemical 
interaction between polar-polar is much stronger than the polar­
–nonpolar interactions [16]. The formation of insoluble Li2S/Li2S2 from 
the soluble polysulfides forms solid particles on the surface cathode/Li 
anode [17], and the transformation of solid Li2S/Li2S2 requires signifi­
cant activation energy, leading to slow reaction kinetics and less energy 
efficiency. Intending to suppress the shuttling of lithium polysulfides, 
polar molecules make strong chemical interactions with the polysulfides 
by decreasing the energy barriers of redox reactions, which assists in the 
catalytic conversion of lithium sulfides [18,19]. Polar materials such as 
metal oxides, sulfides, carbides, and nitrides provide strong polysulfide 
anchoring sites. Besides, according to the hard-soft acid-base theory, 
transition metal sulfides (TMSs) have soft basic S2−/S2
2−instead of hard 
basic O2−ions in the metal oxides showing more covalent properties, 
making better interaction between TM atom and the soft basic S2−/S2
2−
[20]. Over other materials, TMSs gained interest as an excellent sulfur 
host due to their high electrical conductivity (more conductive than 
oxides) and strong chemical affinity towards the adsorptions of poly­
sulfide (enhanced polysulfide redox reaction) and work in low lithiation 
voltages (vs. Li/Li+) which doesn’t overlap in the voltage window of 
LSBs. This increases the overall tap density and volumetric energy 
density of LSBs [21–23]. 
Among metal sulfides, pyrite - FeS2 (iron disulfide), also known as 
fools gold, is an efficient catalyst in LSBs. Since FeS2 is a naturally 
available mineral, environmentally friendly and cheaper material has 
many applications in energy storage, photocatalytic, and photovoltaics 
[24]. FeS2 has a 3D network of metal with discrete S2
2−units and ⁓1.5 V 
lithiation potential [6,25]. Since FeS2 is semi-conductive and sulfiphilic 
in nature, it can form a chemical bond with lithium polysulfides (FeS2- 
Li2Sn) through S–S covalent bonds, which reduces the dissolution of 
polysulfide and also as a buffer upon volume change [26]. For example, 
it has been reported that the 3D- flower-like FeS/carbon assembly ma­
terial (FeS/C-S) shows 600 mAh g−1 with 66 % capacity retention after 
100 cycles at a 0.1C rate [27]. It is also reported that the monodispersed 
FeS2 electrocatalyst anchored nitrogen-doped carbon (S/CPC@FeS2) 
exhibits excellent initial discharge capacity of 1459 mAh g−1 at 0.1C 
with 60 % capacity retention till 900 cycles [28]. Similarly, the FeS2- 
decorated carbon nanofibers display 448 mAh g−1 initial discharge ca­
pacity at a 2C rate with 87 % capacity retention till 300 cycles [29]. 
In this work, a nanocomposite of metal sulfide (FeS2) decorated with 
carbon materials and sulfur is designed to mitigate the low conductivity 
of sulfur and to immobilize the polysulfide shuttling. Further, metal 
sulfide‑carbon‑sulfur composite is coated onto the 3D- carbon fiber (CF) 
electrode architecture as a current collector by replacing the conven­
tional 2D- aluminum current collector. This 3D‑carbon framework ac­
commodates volume expansion during polysulfide conversion, 
providing enough electrolytes into its matrix, high active material 
loading, and a short path for charge transport [30]. Thereby, the ver­
satile approach of sulfur-embedded carbon with metal sulfide composite 
onto the 3D-CF network as a promising cathode material provides a high 
surface area with a 3D-network structure, and metal sulfides provide the 
active sites, which improves the overall electrochemical performance in 
LSBs. 
Hereby, we synthesized FeS2-decorated-reduced graphene oxide 
(FeS2-RGO) by in situ hydrothermal method, followed by the synthesis 
of FeS2-RGO with high surface area carbon and sulfur composite (FRHS) 
by melt diffusion process. The FRHS slurry is coated onto the 3D‑carbon 
fiber (FRHS@CF) electrode to study the electrochemical performance of 
the electrode. The RGO and CF ensure fast electron transport by 
improving the conductivity and accommodating volume changes of 
sulfur during charge-discharge cycling. The decorated polar FeS2 
nanoparticles benefit from good chemical adsorption with sulfur, alle­
viating the dissolution of polysulfides. The FRHS@CF electrodes show 
excellent electrochemical performances (Cycling, C-rate, different 
loading). The detailed study of this cathode work denotes the symbiotic 
effect of FeS2 decorated-conductive RGO with sulfur and 3D electrode 
architecture, which is an impressive path for modifying catalytic cath­
odes in LSBs. 
2. Experimental 
2.1. Materials 
Iron III nitrate nonahydrate and Sulfur (Sigma Aldrich), thiourea and 
NMP(SRL), ethanol (99.9 % purity), High surface area carbon (2500 
m2g−1 from china steel, Taiwan), Carbon fiber (surface area ⁓1 m2g−1; 
TFP Global, UK; Optimat@20301 A; 80 g m−2 carbon felt), were used as 
received. 
2.2. Synthesis of FeS2-RGO hybrid and FRHS composite 
The graphene oxide is synthesized by Hummer’s method, followed 
by thermal heating at 500 ◦C in a tubular furnace under a nitrogen at­
mosphere for 30 min to synthesize reduced graphene oxide (RGO). 
To synthesize the FeS2- RGO (40:60) hybrid, 1 mmol iron III nitrate 
nonahydrate (Fe (NO3)3.9H2O) and 2 mmol thiourea (CH4N2S) pre­
cursors are dissolved in 100 ml ethanol. Then, 400 mg of RGO is added 
to the dissolved solution and sonicated. The resultant solution is trans­
ferred to the hydrothermal jar and heated at 150 ◦C for 12 h. Further, it 
is then dried overnight to get the powdered FeS2-RGO hybrid (Supple­
mentary information Fig. S1a). 
The FeS2-RGO-high surface area carbon (HSAC)-S (FRHS) composite 
is synthesized by the melt-diffusion method. For instance, 60 % sulfur, 
20 % FeS2- RGO, and 10 % HSAC are ground in a mortar pestle, then 
transferred to the Ar gas-filled Schlenk tube and heated at 150 ◦C for 6 h 
to get the FRHS composite (Supplementary information Fig. S1b and 
Scheme 1). 
2.3. Fabrication of FRHS@CF electrodes 
The slurry is prepared by mixing 90 % FRHS composite with 10 % 
PVDF binder dissolved in an NMP solvent. The prepared slurry is coated 
onto a CF mat and then dried at 70 ◦C in a vacuum oven overnight [31]. 
12 mm FRHS@CF electrodes are punched and calendered for the elec­
trochemical study. The average active material loading of the electrode 
is 2.5 mg cm−2. Besides, a high sulfur loading of 4 mg cm−2 has been 
tested. 
2.4. Physical and morphological characterizations 
X-ray diffraction (XRD), Raman spectroscopy, SEM, TEM, and X-ray 
photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) were analyzed for the FRHS material 
and FRHS@CF electrode. The XRDs were performed in the 2θ range of 
100–800, Cukα λ = 1.5406 Å using (40 mA, 40 kV) by X’Pert Pro 
diffractometer (Netherlands). Raman analysis was analyzed using a 
Witec Raman confocal spectrometer. XPS was examined using ESCA+
(Kratos AXIS Nova, Shimadzu, Japan) combined with monochromic 
AlKα (1486.6 eV) and analyzed using Gaussian functions in Origin 
software (9.0). The morphologies of the samples were analyzed by SEM 
and TEM using JEOL-2011 (200 kV) and JEM-F200 microscopes. Ther­
mogravimetric analysis was conducted using an SDT Q600, Germany 
instrument to calculate the weight loss under nitrogen atmosphere at a 
heating rate of 10 ◦C min−1. 
2.5. Electrochemical characterizations 
The CR2032 coin cells were fabricated inside the Ar-filled glove box 
(mBraun, Germany) to test the electrochemical performance of the 
FRHS@CF electrodes. The H2O and O2 levels in the glove box are 
maintained at ≤1 ppm. The cells were fabricated using FRHS@CF as a

Fig. 1c, d depicts the Raman spectra of FeS2-RGO, FRHS, and sulfur.

the FRHS composite show D and G bands similar to FeS2-RGO, and no

decorated reduced graphene oxide (FeS2-RGO), which is an ion- 
electronic network and the composite of sulfur upon melt diffusion. 
The FRHS onto 3D-CF network (FRHS@CF) displays an internal space of 
effective sulfur utilization, high sulfur loading, and good infiltration of 
electrolyte into its matrices, which could effectively benefit the elec­
trochemical performance of FRHS@CF electrodes in LSBs. 
The redox processes of the FRHS@CF composite electrodes in CV 
curves of Fig. 4a show two cathodic peaks at 2.3 V and 2.0 V for the 
reduction of higher-order polysulfides (Li2S8) to soluble polysulfides 
(Li2S6/Li2S4) and insoluble lower polysulfides (Li2S2/Li2S). Similarly, 
the anodic peak at 2.46 V corresponds to the oxidation of Li2S to sulfur. 
Thus, the higher peak current, sharp peaks, and overlapping of the CV 
curves reveal lower polarization, rapid polysulfide conversion kinetics, 
and higher reversibility of the FRHS@CF electrode in LSBs. Therefore, 
the FeS2 relieves the stress of polysulfide shuttling by promoting the 
nucleation ability of Li2S, which enhances the reaction kinetics for more 
conversion of soluble polysulfides to insoluble lithium polysulfides 
[35,36]. The 3D carbon fiber enhances active material utilization, thus 
making the FRHS@CF cathode a more stable electrode, consistent with 
the galvanostatic charge-discharge (GCD) cycling discussed below. 
Similar to the CV curves, the GCD cycling curves show a multistep 
mechanism of sulfur. The reduction at an upper plateau at 2.4 V is 
assigned for the conversion of Li2S8/Li2S6 to Li2S4 and the lower plateau 
at 2.15 V for the conversion of Li2S4 to Li2S2/Li2S. The cycling profile of 
the 3D-CF-based FRHS electrode (FRHS@CF) delivers a remarkable 
capacity of 1165, 1134, and 1074 mAh g−1 in their initial three cycles at 
100 mA g−1 (Fig. 4b) compared to the conventional carbon‑sulfur 
cathodes (Supplementary information Fig. S4) [14]. From Fig. 4c, it is 
observed that the voltage gap (ΔV) between the charge and discharge 
cycle of 1st cycle is 200 mV; thus, the lower potential gap between the 
charge-discharge cycling shows the lower polarization, therefore man­
ifesting the effective electrolyte penetration and the stability of the 
FRHS@CF electrode. The integration of the area under the discharge 
curve of the initial cycle shows an energy density of 2414 Wh kg−1 
(Fig. 4d) for the LSBs (considering the cathode mass alone). 
The long-term cycling (Fig. 5a–d) of the FRHS@CF is carried out at 
different current densities. In Fig. 5a, FRHS@CF at 300 mA g−1 delivers 
an initial discharge capacity of 886 mAh g−1, 662 mAh g−1 at the 25th 
cycle, and maintains 520 mAh g−1 until 200 cycles. The polarization 
effect (Supplementary information Fig. S5a) has been analyzed from the 
initial and the 200th cycle of charge curves, revealing that the hump in 
the 1st cycle at 2.4 V is reduced to 2.2 V at the 200th cycle, showing the 
decrease in polarization effect upon cycling. The voltage gap (Supple­
mentary information Fig. S5b) in the first cycle is 0.23 V and is reduced 
to 0.17 V at the 200th cycle. This shows that the more reductive sulfur 
species formed initially enhance the conversion of Li2S [28]. The 
excellent electrocatalysis of polar FeS2 helps significantly to reduce 
electrochemical polarization. 
Similarly, at a high current density of 500 mA g−1, it delivers the 
initial discharge capacity of 740 mAh g−1, 547 mAh g−1 at its 25th cycle, 
and retains 420 mAh g−1 even at 200 cycles. The capacity retention is 73 
% at the end of 325 cycles (considering 25th cycle capacity) with 99 % 
coulombic efficiency. The capacity decline in the initial cycles at 300 
and 500 mA g−1 (Fig. 5b, d), followed by stable cycle stability after 25 
cycles. The early capacity fading in the first 25 cycles is due to the 
incomplete electronic contact of sulfur, resulting in less sulfur utilization 
[27]. However, the FeS2 could improve the cycling stability by chemi­
cally adsorbing lithium polysulfides, accelerating the cleavage of long- 
chain polysulfides more effectively [37]. This enhances the electronic 
conductivity and exhibits a stable capacity even after 300 cycles. In 
contrast, the cyclic stability plot of the RGO-S@CF electrode at 500 mA 
g−1 with 2.5 mg cm−2 loading of sulfur delivers an initial capacity of 504 
mAh g−1 with 65 % capacity retention at the end of 130 cycles (Sup­
plementary information Fig. S6). 
The rate capability plot of FRHS@CF (Fig. 5e, f) from low to high 
current densities shows a discharge capacity of 1300, 1165, 973, 857, 
750, 654, and 529 mAh g−1 at 50, 100, 200, 300, 500, 1000, and 1500 
mA g−1 current densities. The FRHS@CF displays stable rate capability 
with improved capacities at all current densities by enhancing the re­
action kinetics [38], depicting good stability of the FRHS@CF electrode. 
At high sulfur loading, the concentration of sulfur increases, blocking 
the transition of soluble sulfides. Therefore, the resistance of the battery 
is increased. To utilize the full capacity of sulfur, FeS2-RGO is imple­
mented to improve faster redox kinetics [20,39]. Even at a higher sulfur 
loading of 4 mg cm−2 (Fig. 6a), the cyclic stability plot reveals that the 
electrode performs well and delivers a stable capacity of 550 mAh g−1 
till 120 cycles at 400 mA g−1. The area-specific capacity plot is provided 
in Supplementary information. Fig. S7 indicates an average capacity of 
1.8 mAh cm−2 for 120 cycles. 
To understand the advantage of the FRHS@CF cells, a self-discharge 
study was performed after a fully charged condition followed by 170 h in 
open circuit condition at 25 ◦C (Supplementary information Fig. S8a).

electrochemical performance and cycling stability of FRHS@CF elec­

infiltration, and productive utilization of sulfur, simultaneously elevated

Visual adsorption studies (Supplementary information Fig. S10a) are 
conducted to understand the role of the FeS2-containing sulfur com­
posite cathode in the FRHS@CF electrode. Li2S6 solution is prepared by 
mixing sulfur and Li2S powders by dissolving in Li–S electrolyte under 
50 ◦C continuous stirring for 12 h. Subsequently, the Li2S6 solution (dark 
yellow color) is diluted in the electrolyte and taken in the vials con­
taining the FRHS@CF electrode. With time, the visible change in the 
Li2S6 solution from dark yellow color to pale yellow to colorless is 
observed, and its corresponding UV–visible absorption spectra (Sup­
plementary information Fig. S10b) show the strong peak for Li2S6 so­
lution and the faded solution shows less peak intensity. This depicts the 
strong chemical interaction of FeS2 with the lithium polysulfides. 
XPS of the postmortem cycled (after 200 cycles) FRHS@CF elec­
trodes (Fig. 7a–e) were carried out to understand the surface adsorption 
of lithium polysulfides at discharged state. The survey scan (Fig. 7a) of 
the cycled FRHS@CF electrode confirms the presence of S 2p, S 2 s, C 1 s, 
O 1 s, Fe 2p, and Li 1 s elements. Fig. 7b, Fe 2p shows the shift in peak for 
Fe 2p3/2 and Fe 2p1/2. Compared to the pristine Fe 2p (FRHS@CF), the 
cycled electrode manifests a strong interaction and chemical affinity 
between polar FeS2 and polysulfides. This has been further supported by 
the S2p XPS spectrum in Fig. 7c. The S2p (Fig. 7c) spectrum shows the 
peak at 160.2, 161.7, 162.9, 168, and 169 eV for the corresponding Li2S, 
FeS-S/S2−, Fe–S, sulfates and TFSI−bonds. Fig. 7d, C 1 s spectra show 
that the peaks at 284.6, 285.4, and 289.8 eV correspond to the C–C, 
C–S, and O-C=O bonds. As the C–S physical force is weaker than the 
strong chemical bond between Fe–S, it helps to improve the reduction 
of the polysulfides. Fig. 7e shows that the Li1s spectrum shows the Li–S 
bond at 54.6 eV and Li2S. The presence of the Li–S bond is attributed to 
the elemental sulfur and Li2S deposition; the sulfate (SO3
2−) and S–O 
bonds are responsible for the impregnation of electrolytes in the elec­
trode during the charge-discharge cycles. 
SEM analysis is carried out to understand the morphology of the 
cycled postmortem FRHS@CF electrode. Fig. 7f–h manifests that the 
morphology of the cycled FRHS@CF electrode maintains its integrity. 
The sulfur particles are uniformly deposited on the electrode’s surface, 
improving sulfur utilization. It keeps its structural morphology un­
changed after 200 cycles [43,44]. Thereby, FeS2 provided a selective site 
for the chemical interaction with polysulfides. The RGO offers a good 
network structure, thereby preventing lithium polysulfide from 
collapsing, and no aggregates are seen on the CF matrix. Further, it is 
confirmed by EDX mapping (Fig. 7i–l), representing a uniform distri­
bution of C, Fe, and S elements on the surface of the CF matrix, which 
confirms the structural stability and integrity of the FRHS@CF elec­
trode. The SEM images of the cycled high sulfur loading electrodes in 
Supplementary information Fig. S11 show the Li2S deposition under 
high mass loading of cycled electrodes is quite more on the surface of the 
CF (Supplementary information Fig. S11a–d); the agglomeration of 
sulfur particles is observed in the EDAX images (Supplementary infor­
mation Fig. S11g). A potentiostatic discharge plot of Li2S deposition is 
provided in Supplementary information Fig. S12. For this purpose, the 
cell was galvanostatically charged to 2.8 V and then potentiostatically 
discharged at 2.05 V till the current reached 30 μA (5 h).The capacity of 
Li2S precipitation for FRHS@CF is high, up to 1011 mAh g−1, which 
indicates the fast conversion of lithium polysulfides to Li2S [45].

4. Conclusion 
This work presents FeS2 decorated RGO - HSAC (high surface area 
carbon) with sulfur-infused (FRHS) composite onto the 3D‑carbon fiber 
(FRHS@CF) electrode as a cathode for LSBs. The FRHS@CF electrode 
delivers the initial high discharge capacity of 1165 mAh g−1 at 100 mA 
g−1. At 500 mA g−1, it exhibits an initial discharge capacity of 740 mAh 
g−1 and maintains a stable capacity of 400 mAh g−1 with 73 % capacity 
retention after 300 cycles. Even at a high current density of 1 and 1.5 A 
g−1 shows a better capacity of 654 and 529 mAh g−1. The FRHS@CF 
electrode displays a stable electrochemical performance attributed to 
the rational design of the ion-electron conductive network [46]. The 
RGO and CF effectively shorten the path of ion migration (fast Li+
transport, reduce the cell impedance upon cycling) and provide space
