Suppressing argyrodite oxidation by tuning 
the host structure for high-areal-capacity 
all-solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries
 
Zhuo Yu 
  , Baltej Singh, Yue Yu & Linda F. Nazar 
   
Argyrodite (Li6PS5Cl) is a promising electrolyte for high-performance 
solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries (SSSBs), which operate on the 
reversible conversion of S↔Li2S. However, argyrodite is electrochemically 
decomposed above 2.5 V versus Li+/Li on charge, because free S2− ions 
in the lattice are oxidized to sulfur at a similar potential as Li2S. Here we 
demonstrate that creating a strong interaction between the Li ions in 
argyrodite and the sulfur host synergistically suppresses the oxidation of 
argyrodite by inhibiting the extraction of Li+ in the initial step. A carbon 
nitride/N-doped graphene host serves as a proof of concept to demonstrate 
this effect. Additionally, its moderate electron transport enables SSSB 
operation and constrains electron mobility at the argyrodite interface. 
Consequently, SSSBs utilizing this host deliver excellent rate capability 
and stable long-term cycling compared with non-polar carbon materials. 
An areal capacity of 2 mAh cm−2 was achieved over 230 cycles at room 
temperature, whereas a high capacity of 11.3 mAh cm−2 was obtained with 
90% retention at 60 °C. The descriptors presented here could enrich the 
understanding of solid electrolyte redox activities and guide interface and 
materials design in SSSBs.
Solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries (SSSBs) can meet the require-
ments for e-mobility owing to their high theoretical energy density 
(2,600 Wh kg−1) and the low cost of sulfur1,2, as well as circumventing 
organic electrolyte flammability in liquid electrolyte lithium–sulfur 
(Li–S) batteries. Importantly, solid-state cells eliminate the negative 
impact of the shuttle effect on cycle lifespan caused by dissolved poly-
sulfides, thereby potentially improving both safety and stability3–6. 
Argyrodites (AGs)—Li6PS5Cl (LPSC) and the more ionically conductive 
Cl-rich Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 (LPSCl1.5) (ref. 7)—are commonly used solid-state 
electrolytes (SEs) in SSSBs due to their high ionic conductivity—up to 
10 mS cm−1 in LPSCl1.5—and good ductility8. However, they exhibit a low 
oxidation potential of ~2.5 V versus Li+/Li, which overlaps the operat-
ing voltage window of the S–Li2S redox couple that extends to 3.2 V 
(refs. 9–11). The insulating nature of both sulfur and Li2S also requires 
a conductive agent in the cathode, accelerating AG oxidation at the 
interface10–13. This causes SE consumption at the cathode interface dur-
ing the charging of the SSSB, deteriorating battery performance9. Elec-
tronic transport is a controlling factor at the sulfur host–AG interfaces 
in which the oxidation is initialized, and is dependent on the electronic 
conductivity of the host and nature of the contact14–16. For a highly 
conductive host, AG can decompose with only a small overpotential; 
to suppress oxidation, local electron transport at the interfaces needs 
to be restricted. Although the SE will not decompose if ion/electron 
transport is totally blocked, the SSSBs also cannot function. Achieving 
a balance of ion and electron transport is, thus, vital.
Deciphering the redox activity of AG can help find ways to sup-
press it. It was recently proved that this fast-ion conductor favours 
an indirect decomposition pathway on oxidation, via delithiated 
states of the SE. These states then convert to thermodynamically 
stable, redox-active decomposition products, explaining the wider

Suppressing electrochemical oxidation of LPSCl1.5 
with CNG
SSSBs were fabricated using either laboratory-prepared LPSCl1.5 or LPS 
as an electrolyte (the X-ray diffraction patterns are shown in Supple-
mentary Fig. 3) and cycled at room temperature under a stack pressure 
of 50 MPa. We utilized Cl-rich Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 AG to lower the fraction 
of free S2− in the AG and to benefit from its high ionic conductivity 
(10 mS cm−1; Supplementary Fig. 4). LPS, with a typical Li-ion conduc-
tivity of 0.23 mS cm−1, served as a comparison6. CNG–S, commercial 
Vulcan carbon–sulfur (VC–S) or NG–S (40 wt% S) were mixed with 
these two SEs (50:50 by weight) to evaluate the role of N-doped carbon.
Cells with baseline Vulcan carbon were first discharged. The cell 
with LPSCl1.5 exhibits slightly lower polarization and higher discharge 
capacity than LPS (1,210 mAh g−1 versus 1,130 mAh g−1; Fig. 2a), indi-
cating that the much higher ionic conductivity of LPSCl1.5 modestly 
improves the SSSB performance. On charge, both cells achieve addi-
tional ‘overcharge’ capacity at ~2.9 V (dashed line region); a plateau 
ascribed to the oxidation of the SEs. By contrast, the cell with CNG–S 
as the cathode and LPSCl1.5 as the electrolyte did not show an additional 
plateau (Fig. 2b), indicating negligible decomposition of the SE.
To show that CNG suppresses LPSCl1.5 oxidation, the cathodes 
(host–sulfur–LPSCl1.5) were directly charged to 4.1 V to compare 
LPSCl1.5 degradation (Fig. 2c). AG is oxidized above 2.5–2.7 V versus 
Li+/Li (refs. 28–30), but since sulfur cannot be electrochemically 
oxidized, any charge capacity must result from LPSCl1.5 oxidation 
alone. VC–LPSCl1.5 attained almost full capacity (277 mAh g−1 versus 
280 mAh g−1; Supplementary Fig. 5) expected for the complete oxida-
tion of AG to S, LiCl and P2S7
4−, followed by NG–LPSCl1.5 with 3.1 at.% N 
(200 mAh g−1). In sharp contrast, the CNG–LPSCl1.5 composite with 
15 at.% N showed a capacity of only ~46 mAh g−1. CNG has a higher 
surface area than VC (450 m2 g–1 versus 260 m2 g–1)27 and should, thus, 
favour charge transfer if the oxidation capacity of AG is simply based 
on contact with the carbon host. This demonstrates the positive effect 
of the carbon’s N content.
Differential capacity analysis (dQ/dV) of the three SSSBs (Fig. 2d) 
shows that NG–LPSCl1.5 and VC–LPSCl1.5 obey a two-step oxidation pro-
cess, as reported elsewhere17. However, CNG–LPSCl1.5 shows a critical 
difference. We propose that the first peak centred at 2.4 V for VC (2.5 V 
for NG) corresponds to the oxidation of Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 to transiently form 
metastable Li4.5PS4.5Cl1.5 (confirmed in an LPSCl1.5-only cell (Supplemen-
tary Fig. 6)). This is followed by its immediate conversion into LPS, LiCl 
and S, at 2.94 V and 2.95 V for VC and NG, respectively. The Coulombic 
capacity on oxidation confirms these proposed processes (Supplemen-
tary Fig. 7). The shift to a higher voltage for VC→NG may arise from a 
kinetic overpotential owing to the reduced electronic conductivity by 
two-dimensional NG, Li+ in AG binding to N3−δ in NG, or both. Remark-
ably, the first and second processes are absent for CNG, leaving only one 
broad and weak peak. This demonstrates sluggish oxidation kinetics 
and strong suppression of LPSCl1.5 degradation. We propose that the 
third process at ~3.1 V corresponds to the complete oxidization of LPS 
to S and P2S7
4− (ref. 17). An aging test at 4.1 V for 30 h also demonstrates 
the suppressed oxidation of LPSCl1.5 by CNG (Supplementary Fig. 8).
Multiple probes of AG–CNG interaction
To determine the underlying origin of the observed behaviour, DFT was 
used to calculate the interfacial binding energies between LPSCl1.5 and 
the VC and CNG host materials (approximated as graphene and carbon 
nitride, respectively). Among the calculated low-Miller-index surfaces 
of LPSCl1.5, the one with the lowest surface energy (010, ~0.4 J m–2; Sup-
plementary Table 1) was investigated for interaction with various host 
frameworks. DFT-relaxed configurations of graphene, carbon nitride 
electrochemical stability window versus that expected for direct 
decomposition17. The density functional theory (DFT) simulations 
predicted that Li6PS5Cl will first be oxidized to ‘Li4PS5Cl’ (via the extrac-
tion of 2Li+ and oxidation of the free S2− ions in the lattice to S0), but this 
transient intermediate, at a higher voltage, immediately decomposes 
into sulfur, Li3PS4 (LPS) and LiCl, which are poorly ion conductive17. 
We rationalize that if the initial oxidation process could be inhibited 
at the interface between the solid electrolyte and the carbon host, 
the stabilization of AG could result. This would extend deep into the 
solid electrolyte given its low partial electronic conductivity. A cen-
tral factor is AG’s interaction with the sulfur cathode’s host, which we 
consider for the first time.
Here we demonstrate that generating a strong interaction by 
altering the charge density at the interface suppresses AG oxidation 
by slowing the kinetics of the indirect pathway, and can shift the oxi-
dation potential for S2−↔S0. Polar moieties, namely, N-containing18–21, 
O-containing22–24 and F-containing surface groups25, are used in 
liquid-electrolyte Li–S batteries to bind lithium polysulfides. Using 
these design principles, the sulfur host–AG interface was stabilized by 
regulating both the local electron transport and chemical binding envi-
ronment of LPSCl1.5. We limit electron transport at the interface by uti-
lizing a wide-bandgap carbonitride (C3N4)–nitrogen-doped graphene 
(CNG) composite as the sulfur host. CNG also serves a second critical 
function: it invokes strong interaction of its electron-dense pyridinic 
nitrogen groups with Li+ ions in AG at the interface, as confirmed by our 
DFT and spectroscopic studies. The suppression of AG oxidation on 
charge in CNG–S cells was demonstrated by a large onset overpotential 
and very low charge transfer compared with other carbons. Further-
more, although the initial 2e−/2Li+ oxidation step is evident with carbon 
hosts, it is effectively eliminated with CNG–AG. Shutting down this step 
inhibits further decomposition of AG to insulating by-products such 
as P2S7
4−. Accordingly, CNG–S cells exhibit little internal resistance 
increase during cycling, resulting in SSSBs that exhibit an areal capac-
ity of 2 mAh cm−2 over 250 cycles at 25 °C. At higher sulfur loading, an 
unprecedentedly high areal capacity of 11.3 mAh cm−2 was achieved, 
although over limited cycling. This work provides fresh perspectives 
into the design of sulfur host–AG interfaces in SSSBs, and sheds light 
on electrolyte oxidation suppression in other all-solid-state batteries 
using sulfide electrolytes.
Design of the composite sulfur host material
The heavily nitrogenated porous carbon–sulfur host—CNG—is light-
weight and accommodates a high fraction (70 wt%) of sulfur–Li2S. Our 
previous DFT studies using Li2S2 as a probe molecule demonstrated that 
Li+ strongly binds to the pyridinic Nδ− groups in CNG26, making it a good 
choice in the solid state. In the AG structure adopted by Li6PS5Cl and 
Cl-rich Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 (LPSCl1.5; Fig. 1a), isolated S2− ions exist in the cubic 
lattice along with PS4
3− tetrahedra. Li+ ions at the carbon–AG interfaces 
are first removed, accompanied by oxidation of the S2− ions to S0 via 
electron transfer to the host17. This corresponds to the first oxidation 
peak at ~2.5 V in the dQ/dV profile (Fig. 1b). The incorporation of a high 
fraction of nitrogen in the carbon host (Fig. 1b) inhibits initial Li+ extrac-
tion because of the strong polar Nδ−–Li+ interaction at the AG interface, 
thereby stabilizing the AG to oxidation.
CNG is formed by the stacked combination of g-C3N4 and N-doped 
graphene (NG), both of which exhibit a two-dimensional sheet structure 
(Fig. 1c) to form a continuous three-dimensional conductive network. 
The graphene sheet morphology of CNG is evident in the scanning 
electron microscopy (SEM) (Fig. 1d) and transmission electron micros-
copy (TEM; Fig. 1e,f) images. Nitrogen is evenly distributed within the 
CNG at a concentration of 15 at.%, whereas NG contains only 3.1 at.% 
(Supplementary Fig. 1). X-ray diffraction patterns (Supplementary 
Fig. 2) exhibit a broadened (002) peak in CNG compared with a sharp 
peak in g-C3N4. The semiconductor g-C3N4 makes the local g-C3N4–AG 
interface less electronically conductive than for NG or Vulcan carbon

regardless of which termination of LPSCl1.5 binds with C3N4. However, 
this is not the case for the graphene–LPSCl1.5 interface. The simulations 
of surface bandgaps also indicated much less favourable electronic

Overall, comparing the TOF-SIMS and XPS spectra of CNG at OCV 
and CNG charged to 4.1 V, we found only small changes corresponding 
to minor degradation. This is consistent with the low degree of LPSCl1.5 
oxidation on charging the CNG–LPSCl1.5 cell (Fig. 2c). Since LPSCl1.5 oxi-
dation creates insulating LiCl and Li4P2S7 interphases near the active 
materials and electron-conducting phases, it hinders ion transport in 
the cathode composite and deteriorates the SSSB performance9. We, 
therefore, monitored the resistance of the three cells after charging 
(Fig. 2c) using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and the 
corresponding distribution of relaxation times. All the EIS spectra showed 
a charge transfer resistance due to a passivated interface (Supplementary 
Fig. 16). However, the CNG cell exhibited the lowest value, indicating much 
less decomposition of LPSCl1.5 at the LPSCl1.5–CNG interface. The distribu-
tion of relaxation times analysis (Supplementary Fig. 17) demonstrated 
the same trends. Hence, utilizing CNG as a sulfur host for SSSBs greatly 
improves the electrochemical performance, as shown below.

For practical applications, high sulfur loading is important for 
SSSBs to achieve high energy densities and meet practical requirements. 
Li–S cells at a sulfur loading of 6.8 mg cm−2 (Fig. 6a,b) were assembled 
to assess high-sulfur-loading compatibility and was cycled at C/20. The 
cells were able to deliver 4.1 mAh cm−2 at initial discharge with good 
capacity retention over >40 cycles at room temperature. At 60 °C, the 
cell showed improved electrochemical performance due to enhanced Li+ 
diffusion and reaction kinetics, demonstrating smaller polarization and 
a higher initial conversion efficiency of 96.7% (Fig. 6c). The cell delivered 
11.3 mAh cm−2 at the initial cycle and a capacity retention of 91% over 40 
cycles (Fig. 6d). The anode/cathode areal capacity ratio (N/P ratio) was 
set at 1.2, which is close to practical application requirements. To the 
best of our knowledge, this is superior to recently reported solid-state 
Li–S batteries (Supplementary Fig. 27 and Supplementary Table 2 for 
comparison). The NG–S cell exhibited an areal capacity of 4.1 mAh cm−2 
only at 60 °C, accompanied by low Coulombic efficiency (~92%) and fast 
capacity decay (Supplementary Fig. 28). Although electrolyte oxidation 
is usually more extensive at higher temperatures, the profile at 60 °C still 
exhibited only one plateau and the oxidation capacity was smaller than 
those of NG–S and VC–S at room temperature (Supplementary Fig. 29). 
Therefore, CNG is still able to mitigate AG degradation at elevated tem-
peratures, favouring improved cycling.
Although we have tackled the instability of AGs during oxidation, 
dendrite growth and the stability of SEs against a lithium metal anode is 
still an issue to be resolved. There are some effective strategies recently 
reported to address those problems, including the use of alloys42,43. 
Also, an alternative approach would be to use Li2S as the cathode to 
house the lithium inventory, in an ‘anodeless’-type configuration.
Conclusions
A continuous ion- and electron-conductive network and the suppres-
sion of electrolyte degradation has a synergistic effect on achieving 
promising electrochemical performance of SSSBs. By using a sulfur 
host with a very high nitrogen content (CNG), AG decomposition is 
largely inhibited via strong Li–N binding at the solid electrolyte–sulfur 
host interface. This inhibits the initial oxidation of AG—which occurs by 
an indirect process—kinetically limiting Li-ion extraction and shifting 
the potential for sulfide-ion conversion to sulfur in the first step. When 
the first step is suppressed, so are the subsequent steps. This slows 
down the oxidation kinetics, rather than changing the thermodynamic 
oxidation potential of AG, as reflected by an elevated overpotential 
in direct charge and a broad single peak in the derivative curve. SSSB 
cycling performance is improved by diminishing the build-up of insu-
lating decomposition products at the interface, allowing the CNG host 
to accommodate high sulfur loading. The CNG cell delivered a 2.8-fold 
areal capacity (11.3 mAh cm−2 at 60 °C) compared with that of the NG 
cell. This work presents an effective approach of suppressing AG degra-
dation in SSSBs, which may be extended to other AG-based solid-state 
batteries. We believe that there will be new materials developed in the 
future inspired by this approach to comprehensively address the issue 
of electrolyte decomposition and push solid-state Li–S batteries to a 
more feasible commercialization level.

Methods
Synthesis of CNG and CNG–S
The CNG was synthesized according to our previous report27. Typi-
cally, 900-mg dicyandiamide and 9.6-g AS-30 colloid solution was 
added to 400 ml of a 1 mg ml−1 graphene oxide aqueous solution. The 
mixture was sonicated for 2 h and then stirred for 5 h. The mixture was 
subsequently heated to evaporate around 80% of the water, forming a 
thick paste that was freeze dried to obtain the precursor. The precursor 
was annealed at 550 °C for 4 h with a ramp rate of 4 °C min−1. CNG was 
obtained by etching the annealed products followed by a subsequent 
drying process. CNG was well mixed with sulfur at the target ratio and 
heated at 155 °C for 18 h to form CNG–S.
Synthesis of Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5
Lithium sulfide, phosphorus pentasulfide and lithium chloride were 
ground in a mortar at the target ratio and pressed into a pellet under 
an Ar atmosphere. The pellet was sealed in a quartz tube under vacuum 
and sintered at 550 °C for 7 h with a ramp rate of 0.5 °C min−1.
SSSB assembly
CNG–S (or NG–S; S content, 70%) and Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 at a mass ratio of 
1:1 were manually ground to obtain the CNG–S–Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 hybrid 
cathode. To assemble an SSSB, 70 mg of Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 was first pressed 
into a pellet in a 10-mm die at 2 t for 1 min. The cathode composite was 
then spread on the surface of Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 and pressed at 3 t for 5 min. 
On the opposite side of Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5, a piece of Li–In alloy was attached 
and pressed at 1 t. Finally, the die was sealed in a stainless-steel casing. 
Screws on the casing were fastened by applying a torque of 10.7 N m. 
Galvanostatic cycling was conducted in the voltage range of 1.4–3.1 V 
versus Li+/Li on a BioLogic VMP3 electrochemical workstation. CV 
studies were performed at a scan speed of 0.1 mV s−1 in the voltage 
range of 1.4–3.1 V versus Li+/Li on a BioLogic VMP3 electrochemical 
workstation. EIS studies on the SSSBs were conducted in a frequency 
range from ~1 MHz to 100 mHz with an amplitude of 100 mV using a 
BioLogic VMP3 electrochemical workstation. All the EIS spectra were 
acquired after a 15-min rest to stabilize the cell.
The electrolyte oxidation contribution was conducted by directly 
charging the host–sulfur–LPSCl1.5 cells with a sulfur content of 40% 
to 4.1 V versus Li+/Li at C/20. The LPSCl1.5 electrolyte loading was 
~4.5 mg cm−2. The capacity was calculated based on the mass of the 
electrolyte. The aging test was conducted using the same procedure, 
and using constant-voltage aging at 4.1 V for 30 h.
Ionic conductivity measurement
Ionic conductivity was measured by EIS at room temperature. Typi-
cally, ~100-mg Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 powder was placed between two titanium 
rods and pressed in a 10-mm die at 3 t for 1 min in an Ar-filled glovebox. 
EIS was conducted within a frequency range from ~1 MHz to 100 mHz 
with an amplitude of 100 mV using a BioLogic VMP3 electrochemical 
workstation.
Physical characterization
SEM studies were conducted on a ZEISS Ultra field-emission SEM 
instrument equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray attachment 
(Oxford). TEM studies were carried out on a ZEISS Libra 200MC TEM 
instrument at 200 kV. X-ray diffraction data were collected on a PANa-
lytical Empyrean instrument outfitted with a PIXcel two-dimensional 
detector operating at 45 kV and 40 mA, using Cu Kα radiation 
(λ = 1.5405 Å). XPS was conducted on a Thermo Fisher K-Alpha XPS 
instrument. All the spectra were fitted with Gaussian–Lorentzian 
functions and a Shirley-type background. The binding energy values 
were calibrated using the C1s peak at 284.8 eV. TOF-SIMS spectra were 
collected using an ION-TOF TOF-SIMS 5 machine in the negative-ion 
or positive-ion mode using a 30-keV Bi3
++ cluster primary ion gun with 
a measurement area of 200 × 200 μm2. The area was rasterized with 
256 × 256 pixel2. The cycle time was set to 100 μs. For each patch, one 
frame and one shot per pixel and frame were chosen. The primary ion 
current was about 0.3 pA. To enable a semiquantitative analysis, the 
stop condition was set with the primary ion dose of 5 × 1012 ions per 
cm2. The measurements of the OCV and 4.1-V charged cathode pel-
lets were conducted on the top surfaces. The samples were protected 
under an Ar atmosphere for the transfer process. Thermogravimetric 
analysis was performed on a TA Q500 from room temperature to 
600 °C under a N2 atmosphere.
DFT calculations
The Vienna ab initio simulation package44,45 was used for the DFT cal-
culations. The calculations utilized plane-wave pseudopotentials with 
projector augmented wave potentials to account for the core electrons, 
whereas the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof version within the generalized 
gradient approximation was used for the exchange–correlation term46. 
The DFT calculations were performed with a plane-wave energy cut-off 
of 520 eV and a k-point mesh density of 731 points per atom. To ensure 
accuracy, the total energy and forces were converged to 10−5 eV and 
0.01 eV A–1, respectively, during the geometry optimizations.
To obtain the ground-state structure of LPSCl1.5, the Li6PS5Cl 
structure provided in the Materials Project database47 was uti-
lized. Substitutions and ordering were performed, where all the 
4a sites were exclusively occupied by Cl, and a total of 32 orderings 
were generated to explore the ground-state structure of LPSCl1.5. 
Low-Miller-index surface slabs of LPSCl1.5 with various surface termi-
nations were generated using pymatgen48 and subsequently relaxed 
through DFT calculations. The slab thickness was set to be greater 
than 10 Å, accompanied by a vacuum thickness of 20 Å. Surface ener-
gies were computed for all the slabs (Supplementary Table 1). Only 
the lowest-energy (010) surface was selected to investigate its inter-
actions with the host framework. The graphitic carbon and g-C3N4 
were modelled using bilayer and monolayer configurations in the a–b 
plane. To account for the dispersion interactions between the layers 
of two-dimensional graphene and g-C3N4, the Tkatchenko–Scheffler

denotes the total energy of the slab containing LPSCl1.5(010) interfaced 
with the host slabs. Conversely, ELPSC
Slab and EHost
Slab correspond to the total 
energies of the isolated slabs of LPSCl1.5(010) and the host materials, 
respectively.
