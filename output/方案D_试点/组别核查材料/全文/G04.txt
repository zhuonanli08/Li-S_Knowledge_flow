Lithium–sulfur batteries (LSBs) are currently considered as promising 
candidates for next-generation energy storage technologies. However, their 
practical application is hindered by the critical issue of the polysulfide-
shuttle. Herein, a metal organic framework (MOF)-derived solid electrolyte 
is presented to address it. The MOF solid electrolyte is developed based on a 
Universitetet i Oslo (UIO) structure. By grafting a lithium sulfonate (-SO3Li) 
group to the UIO ligand, both the ionic conductivity and the polysulfide-sup-
pression capability of the resulting -SO3Li grafted UIO (UIOSLi) solid elec-
trolyte are greatly improved. After integrating a Li-based ionic liquid (Li-IL), 
lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)­imide in 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium 
bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide, the resulting Li-IL/UIOSLi solid electrolyte 
exhibits an ionic conductivity of 3.3 × 10−4 S cm−1 at room temperature. Based 
on its unique structure, the Li-IL/UIOSLi solid electrolyte effectively restrains 
the polysulfide shuttle and suppresses lithium dendritic growth. Lithium–
sulfur cells with the Li-IL/UIOSLi solid electrolyte and a Li2S6 catholyte 
show stable cycling performance that preserves 84% of the initial capacity  
after 250 cycles with a capacity-fade rate of 0.06% per cycle.

1. Introduction
An efficient and economical utilization of renewable energy 
sources requires high-performance energy-storage technolo-
gies. The oxide cathodes developed in the 1980s helped the 
commercialization of lithium-ion batteries,[1] but the high cost 
of the transition-metal-based cathodes hamper their use for 
large-scale energy-storage applications.[2] Among the various 
types of new energy-storage systems, lithium–sulfur batteries 
(LSBs) are promising as a next-generation technology owing to 
the high theoretical capacity of sulfur cathode.[3] However, the 
practical application of LSBs is hindered by a series of intrinsic 
problems associated with the charge and discharge processes,

Recently, a nanoporous separator based on a mixed matrix of

poor electronic and ionic conductivity of sulfur usually leads to 
an inefficient utilization of bulk sulfur cathode, especially when 
less amount of liquid electrolyte is used. The use of Li2S6 catho-
lyte could facilitate the dispersion of sulfur active material into 
the cathode matrix (carbon host) in the case of lean electrolyte 
conditions. In this regard, despite the vital drawback of shut-
tling, the use of dissolved polysulfides as a sulfur-source cathode 
can help to elevate the practical utilization of sulfur.[19] Based on 
the above facets, a new type of LSBs, named hybrid-electrolyte 
LSBs, has been proposed by integrating the merit of SSEs and 
polysulfide catholytes.[20] In such LSBs, LISICON or NASICON 
(such as LATP, LYZP) solid electrolytes have been applied and 
Li2S6 dissolved in organic solvents was used as active catho-
lytes.[21] Remarkable cell performance have been achieved with 
the use of SSEs in terms of specific capacity, cyclability, and the 
elimination of parasitic reaction on the Li anode. However, the 
LISICON or NASICON SSEs usually exhibit either low ionic 
conductivity or incompatibility with lithium metal.
In this work, we present a Li-S battery with an MOF-based 
SSE and Li2S6 catholyte. The MOF SSE is developed on the 
basis of a UIO structure but with a lithium sulfonate (-SO3Li) 
group grafted (termed as UIOSLi). The UIOSLi SSE plays 
multiple important roles in the operation of Li-S cells. First, it 
acts as an insulating separator to prevent the electrical contact 
between the anode and cathode. Second, it provides an ionic 
pathway for the transport of Li+ ions to sustain the electro-
chemical reactions at the two electrodes. Finally, it serves as an 
LPS barrier to prevent the shuttling of polysulfides. The grafted 
SO3Li functional group in the UIO enhances the conductivity 
of the SSE up to 3.3 × 10−4 S cm−1 at room temperature. Hybrid-
electrolyte LSBs with the UIOSLi SSE and the Li2S6 catholyte 
show remarkable rate capability, high coulombic efficiency, and 
long cyclability.

To evaluate the polysulfide permeability in the IL-LI/UIOSLi 
membrane, an in-house designed setup, as schematized in 
Figure S11, Supporting Information, was used to visualize the 
Li2S6 diffusion. The experimental details are as described in our 
previous publication.[27] For a comparison, the conventional Cel-
gard separator was also tested in the same setup. As illustrated in 
Figure 4a, the Li2S6 catholyte immediately infused through the 
Celgard membrane into the blank 1,3-dioxolane/dimethyl ether 
(DOL/DME) solution in the lower glass container. As a result, 
the lower beaker was filled with the brownish solution after 1 h 
of testing. In contrast, the Li-IL/UIOSLi solid electrolyte effec-
tively retained the polysulfide solution in the upper glass vessel 
throughout 7 days of the continuous experiment (Figure  4b). 
The retention of Li2S6 by the Li-IL/UIOSLi is associated with 
three key factors. First, the pore size of MOF is ≈6–12 Å,[28]  
which can suppress the penetration of polysulfide species, 
but allow the transport of Li+ ions. Second, both the sulfonate 
group (SO3−) and the polysulfides (Sn2−) are negatively charged 
species. The presence of SO3− on the ligand of UIO would 
induce a repulsion effect on the Sn2− species and maintain an 
attraction effect of boosting the Li+-ion transport. Finally, the 
Li-IL (2.0 m LITFSI in EMIM [TFSI]) exhibits a viscosity that 
is much higher than that of the catholyte solvents (DME/DOL). 
For example, the viscosity of EMIM [TFSI] at 25 °C is 32 cP, 
while the viscosity of DOL and DME are, respectively, 0.59 and 
0.23 cP at room temperature. Hence, the Li-IL could act as a 
“glue” to hold the polysulfide species from diffusion through 
the UIOSLi framework.

The electrochemical performance of the Li-IL/UIOSLi solid 
electrolyte was further investigated in Li-S cells that were prepared 
with a Li-metal anode and a Li2S6 catholyte. The cell is termed 
as Li ǁ Li-IL/UIOSLi ǁ Li2S6. The active material loading at the 
cathode was 5.6 mgsulfur cm−2 and the ratio of electrolyte to sulfur 
was 5 (5.0 µL of electrolyte per mg of sulfur). Figure 6a shows 
cyclic voltammetry (CV) profiles of the Li ǁ Li-IL/UIOSLi ǁ Li2S6  
cell that were collected at a scan rate of 0.1 mV s−1. The cathodic 
scan starting from 3.0 to 1.5 V demonstrates two broad peaks 
corresponding to the two-step reduction reactions. The first 
peak at ≈2.25 V (marked as I in Figure 6a) is associated with the 
reduction of active sulfur material to dissolved polysulfide spe-
cies (e.g., from S8 to Li2Sn, n = 4–8). The second peak at ≈1.90 V 
(marked as II in Figure  6a) corresponds to further reduction 
of the dissolved polysulfide species to Li2S2 and Li2S (solid-
phase species). The backward-scan profile includes one broad 
anodic peak (marked as I′ in Figure 6a) around 2.40 V that is 
related to the conversion of Li2S2/Li2S to Li2S8/S8. Figure S13, 
Supporting Information, presents the CV profiles of a Li-S cell 
with a conventional Celgard separator (termed as Li ǁ Celgard ǁ 
Li2S6). A comparison of Figure 6a and Figure S13, Supporting 
Information, indicates that the cathodic peak potentials of the 
solid electrolyte cell are lower than those of the liquid electro-
lyte cell, while the anodic peak potential is higher for the solid 
electrolyte cell. This phenomenon is attributed to the relatively 
sluggish transport of Li+-ions in the solid electrolyte. However, 
the Li ǁ Celgard ǁ Li2S6 cell shows a notable decline in current 
density after five cycles (Figure S13, Supporting Information), 
whereas the CV profiles of the Li ǁ Li-IL/UIOSLi ǁ Li2S6 cell 
maintain repeatable CV profiles from cycle to cycle (Figure 6a).

However, the sulfur utilization in the Li ǁ Celgard ǁ Li2S6 cell 
is lower due to the invasion of polysulfide into Li-metal anode. 
The voltage difference between the charge and discharge pro-
files at the mid-depth of charge and discharge can be used to 
evaluate the polarization behavior of the cell. At the low C rate 
(0.1C), the Li ǁ Celgard ǁ Li2S6 cell provides a lower overpotential 
(0.23 V) than the Li ǁ Li-IL/UIOSLi ǁ Li2S6 cell (0.33 V), owing 
to the sluggish Li+-ion transport in the solid electrolyte. How-
ever, at the high C rate (1C), the Li ǁ Celgard ǁ Li2S6 cell exhibits 
higher overpotential than the solid electrolyte cell, possibly due 
to the passivation at the surface of Li anode caused by the poly-
sulfide shuttle. Consequently, the overpotential of the Li ǁ Cel-
gard ǁ Li2S6 cell crucially increases by 70% when the C rate is 
switched back to 0.1C (Figure S14a, Supporting Information). 
The absence of additional (other than the redox reactions of 
sulfur, polysulfide, and sulfide species) peaks or plateaus in the 
CVs or charge–discharge profiles in Figure 6a,b indicates that 
the solid electrolyte has no electrochemical activity during the 
discharge–charge process.
Figure  6c compares the rate capabilities of the Li ǁ Li-IL/
UIOSLi ǁ Li2S6 and Li ǁ Celgard ǁ Li2S6 cells in more detail. 
The Li ǁ Li-IL/UIOSLi ǁ Li2S6 cell delivers reasonable discharge 
capacities (based on the mass of sulfur) of 1095, 985, 890, and 
749 mAh g−1, respectively, at the cycling rates of 0.1C, 0.2C, 0.5C, 
and 1C. When the cycling rate is switched back to 0.1C, the cell 
exhibits a recovery of its discharge capacity of 1033 mAh g−1, 
which is 95% of its initial discharge capacity before the high-
rate cycles. In addition, the discharge capacities within the five 
cycles at each C rate are consistent. On the other hand, the 
discharge capacity of the Li ǁ Celgard ǁ Li2S6 cell continuously 
declines in each cycle and the capacity fade becomes more dra-
matic with increasing cycling rates. The benefits of using solid 
electrolyte are obvious from the above comparison between the 
Li ǁ Li-IL/UIOSLi ǁ Li2S6 cell and the Li ǁ Celgard ǁ Li2S6 cell. 
The enhanced electrochemical performance of the solid electro-
lyte cell is majorly attributed to the effective polysulfide control 
by the IL/UIOSLi membrane.[35]
Long-term cycling performances of the Li ǁ Li-IL/UIOSLi ǁ 
Li2S6 and Li ǁ Celgard ǁ Li2S6 cells are compared in Figure 6d. 
The cells are cycled at a rate of 0.2C. The Li ǁ Celgard ǁ Li2S6 
control cell shows a rigorous decline in discharge capacity, 
ending up with a very low capacity after 100 cycles. This out-
come majorly originates from the continuous polysulfide 
migration from the cathode to the anode during prolonged 
cycling. The diffused polysulfide reacts with Li metal and 
induces a loss of active sulfur material at the cathode and the 
passivation Li-metal surface, which consequently leads to 
a low coulombic efficiency and short life span of the battery.

an important role in the utilization of active sulfur material.[36]

generated and accumulated in each cycle. These clusters could 
continuously consume active lithium like in a parasitic reac-
tion, which could continuously lower the coulombic efficiency 
over long-term cycling.

3. Conclusion
In summary, we have developed an MOF-derived solid electro-
lyte to overcome the crucial problem of polysulfide-shuttle in 
LSBs. The MOF electrolyte was developed with the UIO as the 
base structure, which selectively allows the transport of Li+-ions 
but blocks the migration of polysulfide species. By grafting a 
lithium sulfonate (-SO3Li) group onto the UIO ligand, both the 
ionic conductivity and the polysulfide-suppression capability 
of the solid electrolyte were improved. By properly integrating 
a lithium-ion conductive ionic liquid (Li-IL), LiTFSI in EMIM 
[TFSI], the resulting Li-IL/UIOSLi electrolyte exhibited an ionic 
conductivity of 3.3 × 10−4 S cm−1 at room temperature. Li-S cells 
with the Li-IL/UIOSLi MOF electrolyte showed stable cycling 
performance.

4. Experimental Section
Chemicals and Materials: Zirconium chloride (ZrCl4, >99.5%, Alfa 
Aesar), terephthalic acid (H2BDC, >99%, TCI America), monosodium 
2-sulfoterephthalate (NaSO3-H2BDC, >98%, TCI America), and acetic 
acid (99%, ACROS Organics) were used for the MOF material synthesis. 
EMIM [TFSI] (99.5%, Solvonic) was used for the synthesis of ionic liquid. 
Sublimed sulfur powder (99.5%, Acros Organics) and lithium sulfide 
(Li2S, 99.9%, Acros Organics) were used for preparing the polysulfide 
catholyte. Organic solvents and lithium salt used in this research 
included N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.5%, Acros Organics), 
dimethoxyethane (DME, 99+%, Acros Organics), DOL (99.5%, Acros 
Organics), and lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonimide) (99%, ACROS 
Organics). Carbon nanofiber powder (Fisher Scientific) was used for 
fabricating the cathode matrix.
Synthesis of UIOSx: The UIOSX (x refers to the percentage of SO3− 
group grafted to the UIO ligand, 0, 25, 50, 75, and 100) was prepared 
in a 250 mL round-bottom flask with a reflux condenser. Stoichiometric 
amounts of ZrCl4, H2BDC, and NaSO3-H2BDC were mixed and 
sonicated in a mixed solvent of DME and CH3COOH (19:1 by volume) 
for 1 h. Then, the mixture was continuously stirred and heated at 140 °C 
under reflux for 48 h. The white powder produced was collected by a 
vacuum filtration process and washed with, 250 mL of DME and 300 mL 
of acetone, respectively. The obtained UIOSX was then further dried in a 
vacuum oven for 3 days at 150 °C.
Lithiation of UIOS (UIOSLi): The UIOSx powder was first dispersed 
into a liquid electrolyte comprising 1.0 m LITFSI in a DME/DOL (1:1, 
volume ratio) mixture solvent. Then, the mixture was continuously 
stirred for 14 days at 80 °C. The resulting UIOSLi powder was washed 
with acetone and dried in a vacuum oven for 24 h at 150 °C.
Fabrication of Li-IL/UIOSLi Solid Electrolyte Membranes: The UIOSLi 
powder and Li-IL (2.0 m LITFSI in EMIM [TFSI]) were thoroughly mixed 
in an agitating mortar. The mixture was then heated at 100 °C for 48 h. 
The obtained Li-IL/UIOSLi powder was placed in a pellet pressing die 
set with a diameter of 0.5 in. and was pressed at 7 tons for 1 min to form 
a pellet. The pellet was then dried at 100 °C for 24 h.
Characterization: Ionic conductivity measurements of the MOF solid 
electrolyte pellets were carried out with an AC impedance technique on 
a Solatron 1287 potentiostat at room temperature. The applied frequency 
ranged from 0.1 to 106 Hz and the applied amplitude was 5 mV. FTIR 
of the MOF materials were collected on a Thermo Scientific Nicolet 
iS5 instrument. SEM along with EDS experiments were conducted 
on a Quanta 650 instrumentation. XRD experiments were conducted 
on MiniFlex Benchtop instrumentation with CuKα radiation. Surface 
analysis of the pristine and cycled MOF electrolytes was conducted on 
an X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS, Kratos Analytical Company).
Cell Fabrication and Testing: A blank electrolyte was prepared by 
dissolving 1.0 m LITFSI and 0.1 LiNO3 in a DOL/DME (1:1 volume ratio) 
mixture solvent. Li2S6 polysulfide catholyte was prepared through a 
reaction of sulfur and Li2S (the molar ratio of sulfur to Li2S was 5:1) in 
the blank electrolyte. The concentration of Li2S6 was controlled at 1.0 m 
(6.0 m sulfur). A carbon nanofiber (CNF) paper was used as a matrix for 
the polysulfide catholyte. The CNF paper was synthesized by following 
the same procedures as in previous publications.[40] Li ǁ Li-IL/UIOSLi ǁ 
Li2S6 and Li ǁ Celgard ǁ Li2S6 cells were prepared with a CR2032 coin-
type configuration in an Argon-filled glove box. Sulfur cathodes were
