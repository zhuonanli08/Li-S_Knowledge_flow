ORIGINAL PAPER
Electrochemical evaluation of different graphene/sulfur composite
synthesis routes in all-solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries
Abdulkadir Kızılaslan1,2 & Şükran Efe3 & Hatem Akbulut1,2
Received: 19 April 2020 /Revised: 16 June 2020 /Accepted: 17 June 2020
# Springer-Verlag GmbH Germany, part of Springer Nature 2020
Abstract
The preparation of different cathode composites with intimate contact between the components is of great importance to obtain
batteries with better electrochemical performance. Among different cathode systems, graphene-sulfur composites are widely
utilized in lithium-sulfur batteries and their electrochemical performances are directly linked to syntheses methods. In this study,
graphene-sulfur composite cathodes were prepared by three distinct routes and their performances were evaluated in all-solid-
state lithium-sulfur batteries where the lithium and Li7P3S11 were utilized as anode and solid electrolyte, respectively. Our results
indicate that cathodes prepared by solution-based techniques were found to impart higher capacity and cycling stability compared
with cathode prepared by the melt-diffusion method. Better performance was attributed to fine and homogeneously distributed
particles obtained in cathodes prepared by solution-based techniques. Besides, higher capacity was acquired in the cathodes
where the sulfur was obtained through reduction from Na2S2O3 instead of utilizing sulfur in elemental powder form. Among
three different cathodes synthesized by different routes, the best performance was gained in the cathode prepared by sulfur-amine
chemistry method where the sulfur was obtained by reduction from Na2S2O3 with 970 mAh g-1 and 795 mAh g-1 initial and after
200 cycles discharge capacity respectively. Our results indicate the significance of particle size and intimate contact between
components on the electrochemical performance of the sulfur-based cathodes and the synthesis route to follow for high-
performance all-solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries.
Keywords All-solid-state lithium-sulfur battery . Graphene/sulfur cathode . Li7P3S11
Introduction
In the last few decades, commercial lithium-ion batteries
(LIBs) utilizing graphite anode and oxide cathode have been
widely commercialized on electronic devices. However, as
getting close to the edge in the theoretical limits of Li-ion
batteries, lithium-sulfur batteries (LSBs) have attracted great
interest. With the theoretical capacity of 1672 mAh g-1, LSBs
can deliver eight times more energy compared with traditional
lithium-ion batteries. Despite their charming capacities,
traditional lithium-sulfur batteries with liquid electrolytes
have major challenges to be adopted into high energy de-
manding systems due to well-known polysulfide shuttling
[1–5], volumetric expansion of sulfur upon lithiation/
delithiation [6, 7] and lithium dendrite formation problems
[8–10]. While great strides have been achieved, LSBs with
liquid electrolytes have not yet been emerged into the market.
Lithium-sulfur batteries with solid electrolytes are consid-
ered the next-generation batteries to overcome the aforemen-
tioned problems encountered in batteries with liquid electro-
lytes. Compared with complex reduction reactions in LSBs
with liquid electrolytes [11–13], all-solid-state LSBs are
governed solely by the reaction S+2Li++2e- ↔Li2S which
practically inhibits the formation of soluble Li2Sx (x> 2)
polysulfides and lithium dendrites.
On the cathode side, an intimate contact between all cath-
ode components is vital to ensure continuous charge transfer.
Various carbonaceous materials, e.g., CNTs [14–16], carbon
black [17–19], and graphene [20–22] composited with sulfur,
have been widely used in LSBs. In preparing carbon-sulfur(C-

One way to deposit sulfur onto graphene is carried out by
impregnating melted sulfur into aggregated graphene sheets
to take advantage of the fact that sulfur interacts strongly with
carbon. In this technique, where the sulfur used in elemental
powder form, thoroughly mixed sulfur and carbonaceous ma-
terials were directly heat-treated at 155 °C. Sulfur having the
lowest viscosity at this temperature melts and decorates onto
carbonaceous material in the composite. Although the method
is facile and easy to be implemented in large scales, it suffers
the fine and homogenous distribution of sulfur particles.
Considering the insulating nature of sulfur, coarse particulates
cause very low utilization of the active material. Recently,
solution-based techniques where the sulfur reduced from dif-
ferent species (e.g., Na2S2O3, Na2S, Na2SO4, C14H14S2) and
decorated onto graphene have attracted much interests. By
carefully controlling the reaction parameters, sulfur size, and
load in the composite can be well controlled in solution-based
techniques [23]. Several studies revealed that C-S composites
prepared by solution deposition methods surpass the perfor-
mance of composites synthesized by melt deposition [24] in
LSBs with liquid electrolytes. Many studies discussed the
electrochemical performance of graphene-sulfur composite
cathodes synthesized by various techniques [4, 25–28].
Although the performance limits of the abovementioned
cathodes have been thoroughly investigated separately in
LSBs with liquid electrolytes, a careful analysis of different
cathode chemistries has not yet been investigated in all-solid-
state batteries.
In this study, three different quaternary cathode composites
with the same components, namely, reduced graphene oxide
(rGO), conductive carbon (CB), solid electrolyte (SE), and
sulfur, were synthesized by different syntheses routes. Three
cathodes differ from each other in the way rGO/S composites
were prepared and subsequently each rGO/S mixed with CB
and SE. The first rGO/S composite was synthesized through
the melt diffusion technique and denoted as cathode no. 1. The
second composite was synthesized from sulfur-ethylene
diamine(S/EDA) complex precursor where elemental sulfur
powder was utilized as sulfur source and denoted as cathode
no. 2. The last composite was also synthesized through sulfur-
amine chemistry where the sulfur obtained through reduction
from Na2S2O3 and denoted as cathode no. 3. Synthesis
methods and sulfur source of cathodes were given in Table 1.
After the synthesis of three different cathodes, their elec-
trochemical performances were evaluated in assembled all-
solid-state batteries where lithium and Li7P3S11 were utilized
as anode and solid electrolyte respectively.
Experimental
The synthesis of solid electrolyte used in this study was re-
ported in our previous study [29]. Powder XRD analysis of the
cathodes was carried out by Rigaku D/MAX 2000 type X-ray
spectrometer with Cu Kα radiation between 10° and 50° with
1°min-1 scan rate. The morphologies and elemental mapping
of the cathode composites were observed with field emission
scanning electron microscope (FESEM, FEI Quanta FEG
450). To calculate the active mass load on cathode compos-
ites, thermal gravimetry analysis was carried out using
Netzsch DSC200 F3 under a nitrogen atmosphere with a
heating rate of 10 °C min-1. Transmission electron microscopy
(TEM) analysis was performed to characterize the size and
morphology of graphene-sulfur composites. Fourier transform
infrared (FTIR) analysis was carried out in attenuated total
reflection (ATR) mode with 4 cm-1 resolutions between 400
and 4000 cm-1 wavenumbers to characterize the graphene
oxide and reduced graphene-oxide/sulfur composites.
The electrochemical measurements of cathodes were con-
ducted on all-solid-state PEEK insulated battery cell (ø10
mm) where Li and Li7P3S11 were utilized as anode and solid
electrolyte respectively. Typically, 100 mg solid electrolyte
was pressed under 340 MPa to obtain solid electrolyte pellet.
Four milligrams of cathode powder was then spread on one
side of the pellet and pressed under 240 MPa. Finally, lithium
chip was attached to the other side of the pellet and pressed
under 120 MPa to get the three-layered cell. Stainless steel
punches of the battery mold act as a pressure transmitting
media along with current collectors in the cell. Cyclic volt-
ammetry analysis was conducted in Gamry Ref1000
galvanostat between 1.2 and 2.8 V at a scan rate of 0.5 mV
s-1. The galvanostatic charge-discharge tests were carried out
between 1.4–2.8 V with a current density of 0.4 mA cm-2 at 40

Cathode no. 1: composite cathode synthesis by melt
diffusion
Graphene oxide was synthesized by the modified Hummers
method and thermally reduced at 800 °C under Ar/H2 flow to
obtain reduced graphene oxide (rGO). High-purity sublimed
sulfur and rGO was mixed in agate mortar (4/1, wt./wt.) and
subsequently heat-treated at 155 °C for 10 h in a sealed glass
tube. After heat treatment, sulfur was impregnated into rGO
and black-colored rGO/S composite was obtained. rGO/S
composite was then composited with carbon black (CB)
(3/1, wt./wt.) through ball milling at 400 rpm for 10 h.
Ternary rGO/S/CB composite was then mixed with Li7P3S11
(3/2, wt./wt.) in Ar-filled glove box and ball milled at 200 rpm
for 10 h to obtain the final composite. In order to preserve the
synthesized graphene/sulfur morphology, mild milling was
carried out.

Cathode no. 3: sulfur-amine chemistry composite

Graphene oxide of 270 mg (GO) was dispersed in 270-mL

distilled water which was subsequently added into rGO solu-

then added dropwise into the rGO/Na2S2O3 solution and
stirred for 1 h. rGO/S composite was obtained through filter-
ing and rinse with distilled water. After drying overnight at 60
°C, obtained composite was heat treated at 155 °C under Ar
flow in a sealed glass jar for 12 h. rGO/S composite was then
composited with CB and Li7P3S11 as the same procedure
mentioned above to get the quaternary cathode composition.

Results and discussion
Phase analysis of cathodes
Phase analysis of the rGO/S cathodes synthesized by different
routes was analyzed by XRD and the results are given in Fig. 2.
Main diffraction peaks located at 2θ = 15.59°, 22.16°, 23.41°,
26.16°, 26.90°, 28.07°, 29.01°, and 31.73° were indexed to or-
thorhombic sulfur peaks (JCPDS card no. 78-1889). A large
amorphous peak at around 26° testifies the existence of rGO in
the composite. XRD analysis of cathode no. 1 displays sulfur
peaks having high intensity which stands for the existence of the
crystalline sulfur particles with coarse grains decorated onto the
rGO matrix. On cathode no. 2 and cathode no. 3, diffraction

show that sulfur particles are much smaller in size. Besides, there
is no peak at 2θ = 11° on cathode no. 3 which testifies that
graphene oxide was completely reduced by EDA
functionalization at 75 °C. The effect of EDA on the reduction
of graphene oxide was evidenced by treating dispersed graphene
oxide solution with EDA at different times. As shown in Fig. 3,
the existence of strong peak at 2θ = 11° which stands for the

rGO/S composites, we have carried out FTIR analysis.
Figure 4 displays the presence of different functional groups

Hydroxyl and carboxyl functional groups are observed at ~

which was attributed to the formation of C–S bonds. [33].

Electrochemical evaluation of cathodes
Cyclic voltammetry (CV) analysis was carried out to charac-
terize electrochemical reactions and corresponding voltages in
which the reactions take place. Figure 8 displays the CV anal-
ysis of assembled all-solid-state batteries for each cathode. It
can be observed that each cell driven by the same electro-
chemical reactions and these reactions are different from typ-
ical LSBs with organic liquid electrolytes [11, 12]. On the
cathodic scan, one reduction peak at around 1.75 V was ob-
served which stands for the reduction of S8 into Li2S where
the corresponding reaction was observed in anodic scan at
around 2.4 V which is due to oxidation of Li2S reverse back
into S8. The overall reaction governing all-solid-state LSBs
was confirmed as S+2Li++2e-↔Li2S.
Figure 9 displays the galvanostatic charge-discharge anal-
ysis of cells utilizing different cathode chemistries along with
Li7P3S11 and lithium as solid electrolyte and anode respective-
ly. Cathode no. 1, cathode no. 2, and cathode no. 3 exhibited
an initial discharge capacity of 930, 1010, and 970 mAh g-1
and after 200 cycles, cells display discharge capacities of
543.9, 740, and 795 mAh g-1 respectively.
Figure 10 displays the cyclic performance of the cells as-
sembled with different cathodes. After 200 cycles, cathode no.
1, cathode no. 2, and cathode no. 3 have the capacity fade of
41.6%, 26.7%, and 18% respectively.

Cathodes prepared by solution-based techniques were
found to having higher discharge capacity and better cycling
stability which can be attributed to the smaller particulate size
of active materials and intimate contact between cathode com-
ponents. Small initial discharge capacity difference between
cathode no. 2 and cathode no. 3 may be due to the slight active
material difference in composites as verified by thermal gravi-
metric analysis. However, on cathode no. 1, in which the
composite synthesized through the melt-diffusion method,
the initial capacity is lower despite the higher sulfur load. As
displayed in Fig. 11a, large sulfur particles in the composite
were precipitated if the reaction time extended into 10 min
(instead of 2 min) in the synthesis of cathode no. 2 as in the
case of cathode no. 1. As illustrated in Fig. 11b, during dis-
charge, lithium ions react with sulfur particulates which form
an insulating Li2S layer on sulfur yields an active material loss
in cathodes. Hence, materials with smaller particulates allow
higher capacities due to the increased number of surface atoms
in active materials.
Cathode composites prepared by solution-based techniques
where sulfur-amine chemistry utilized yield higher discharge
capacities and cycling stability independent of sulfur source
e.g. elemental sulfur powder or Na2S2O3. Although sulfur
exists as octatomic (S8) form in natural powder form, it was
revealed that it resides as polysulfide ions and sulfur radicals
in amine solutions [37]. In sulfur-amine chemistry where sul-
fur in elemental powder form, sulfur–ethylenediamine com-
plex decomposes and sulfur attaches onto graphene due to
strong affinity between sulfur and graphene. On cathode no.
3, graphene oxide initially functionalized through EDA moi-
eties which were then reduced into graphene through the re-
moval of oxygen functional groups in graphene oxide at mod-
erately high temperatures. The amine groups with high reac-
tivity strongly bind polar lithium sulfide with non-polar
graphene surface which promotes the contact between rGO-
Li2S during discharge. In fact, strong interaction between Li2S
and functionalized rGO was verified by density-functional
theory-based calculations [24]. Such interaction between the
rGO and sulfur not only enhances electronic conductivity in
cathode but also suppresses the volumetric change of sulfur
during charge and discharge. Sulfur is loaded into this cathode
through the disproportionation reaction of Na2S2O3 with HCl
as a reducing agent. Although cathode no. 2 and cathode no. 3
serve the same reaction chemistry, kinetics of sulfur-amine
chemistry with elemental sulfur is hard to control due to the
rapid crystallization of sulfur on graphene. Precise control
over reaction time is required to control sulfur crystallite size
which may inhibit to scale-up the production for cathode no.
2. However, on cathode no. 3, reaction kinetics can be well-
controlled which offers feasible paths to produce such cath-
odes in large amounts.
Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measure-
ments were conducted in the cells before and after the cycling
tests as given in Fig. 12. The Nyquist plots reveal an incom-
plete semicircle in the high-frequency region due to lower
resistance with a capacitive linear spike at low frequencies
due to which is common in all-solid-state batteries with
sulfur-based solid electrolytes where the total impedance of
the cell was calculated from the intercept of linear spike and
real part of the impedance plot [22, 38–41]. Our results indi-
cate that the cell assembled with the cathode no. 3 yields the
lowest total impedance and the trend in the impedance spec-
trum of each cathode is in line with the electrochemical per-
formance of the cathodes.

Conclusions
All-solid-sate lithium-sulfur batteries were assembled with
three different graphene-sulfur composite cathodes each syn-
thesized through different routes and Li7P3S11 utilized as a
solid electrolyte. Cathodes prepared by solution-based sul-
fur-amine chemistry method were found to surpass the elec-
trochemical performance of cathode produced by melt-
diffusion in terms of capacity and cycling stability. Among
the cathodes prepared by sulfur-amine chemistry, better per-
formance was obtained in the cathode where sulfur was re-
duced from Na2S2O3 instead of utilizing an elemental sulfur
powder. Better electrochemical performance was attributed to
the slower reaction kinetics in the reduction process which
inhibits the crystallization and grain growth of the sulfur par-
ticulates in the composite cathode. Besides, coarse sulfur par-
ticles with highly crystalline nature were obtained in case
elemental sulfur powder was utilized in the cathode synthesis.
Our results put forward a strong correlation between the active
material size in cathode and the cycle stability of the
ASSLSBs, where the capacity loss was obtained as 18%,
26.7%, and 41.6% with increasing sulfur size in the cathode.
Our results indicate that the performance of all-solid-state
LSBs is highly dependent on the particle size of sulfur in the
cathode and the solution-based techniques yield finer active
materials in cathodes which promotes the electrochemical per-
formance of cathodes.
