1. Introduction
The ever-increasing demands for high-
energy-density batteries have motivated 
intensive studies on rechargeable Li-metal 
batteries in recent years because Li-metal 
anodes have an exceedingly high specific 
capacity (3860 mAh g−1) and low elec-
trode potentials (−3.04  V vs the standard 
hydrogen electrode).[1–5] However, Li-metal 
anodes suffer from several severe issues 
in practical applications,[6–10] such as high 
surface area lithium growth,[11–15] rapid 
consumption of active Li,[16–18] and accu-
mulation of solid electrolyte interphase 
(SEI),[19–22] which lead to safety concerns, 
short cycle life, and low Coulombic effi-
ciency (CE). Owing to the high activity, Li 
metal spontaneously reacts with most elec-
trolytes if it is not passivated or protected. 
Thus, a viable Li-metal anode should main-
tain the stability of Li/electrolyte inter-
faces where SEI plays a crucial role.[23–25] 
Integrating solid-state electrolyte (SSE) into Li-metal anodes has demon-
strated great promise to unleash the high energy density of rechargeable 
Li-metal batteries. However, fabricating a highly cyclable SSE/Li-metal anode 
remains a major challenge because the densification of the SSE is usually 
incompatible with the reactive Li metal. Here, a liquid-metal-derived hybrid 
solid electrolyte (HSE) is proposed, and a facile transfer technology to 
construct an artificial HSE on the Li metal is reported. By tuning the wetta-
bility of the transfer substrates, electron- and ion-conductive liquid metal is 
sandwiched between electron-insulating and ion-conductive LiF and oxides to 
form the HSE. The transfer technology renders the HSE continuous, dense, 
and uniform. The HSE, having high ion transport, electron shut-off, and 
mechanical strength, makes the composite anode deliver excellent cyclability 
for over 4000 h at 0.5 mA cm−2 and 1 mAh cm−2 in a symmetrical cell. When 
pairing with LiFePO4 and sulfur cathodes, the HSE-coated Li metal dramati-
cally enhances the performance of full cells. Therefore, this work demon-
strates that tuning the interfacial wetting properties provides an alternate 
approach to build a robust solid electrolyte, which enables highly efficient 
Li-metal anodes.

However, the dynamic volume change of Li metal during plating/
stripping renders a natural SEI (which is usually 10–20 nm and 
not flexible)[26,27] vulnerable to withstand the tremendous stress. 
The resulting crack in the SEI will cause a repeated formation of 
new SEI, thereby lowering the cyclability.[28]
Many strategies like designing new electrolyte formula[29–31] 
and/or building an artificial protective layer by pretreating Li 
surface[32–34] have been proposed to stabilize the Li/electrolyte 
interface and improve the cycling performance of Li-metal 
anodes. For example, LiNO3,[35] as a widely used electrolyte 
additive,[36–40] could form a relatively stable SEI containing 
LiNxOy and Li3N, which originate from the decomposition of 
LiNO3. Some fluorine-containing organic substances or salts 
were also used as the electrolyte additives because their decom-
position product (LiF), as a good electron insulator and poor ion 
conductor (the Li-ion diffusivity is 1.3 × 10−23 cm2 s−1),[41] could 
strengthen the SEI by suppressing dendrites, blocking electron, 
and facilitating ion transport.[42–44] Based on these strength-
ening mechanisms, other artificial protective layers, such as 
Al2O3,[45] LiF,[46] and Li–M alloys (M = Zn,[15] Sn,[47] Hg,[48] In,[49] 
etc.), were also developed to coat Li metal. In addition to the 
consideration for mechanical suppression, another key factor 
among these protective layers is the relationship between elec-
tron and ion conductivities, which determines whether Li ions 
rapidly diffuse through and deposit beneath the protective layer.
A good solid electrolyte layer should have several prerequi-
sites: 1) high Li-ion conductivity; 2) negligible electron transport; 
3) mechanical strength; 4) dense structure; 5) easy to fabricate; 
6) stability concerning thermal, chemical, and electrochemical 
decomposition; 7) flexibility. Simple binary Li salts (like LiF,[46] 
Li2O,[50] LiCl,[51] LiBr,[52] and LiI[53]) are convenient candidates 
because they are apparently electron insulators and can also 
allow modest transport of Li ions along their grain boundary. 
To mechanically suppress the dendrite growth, artificial solid 
electrolytes usually have to be thicker than a natural SEI. How-
ever, a thick solid layer imposes high kinetic resistance at a high 
charge/discharge rate.[54] In contrast, Li–M alloys usually have 
relatively high ion conductivity (Li–Ga,[55] 1.4 × 10−9 cm2 s−1; 
Bi–Ga,[56] 8.9  ×  10−5 cm2 s−1; Li–Sn,[57] 10−14 to 10−9 cm2 s−1; 
Li–In,[49] 10−8 to 10−6 cm2 s−1), probably enabling a thick film to 
suppress dendrites without sacrificing ion transports. Unfortu-
nately, their high electron conductivities undermine the advan-
tages to be an ideal solid electrolyte for Li-metal anodes.
In this work, we developed an artificial hydride solid electro-
lyte (HSE) by transferring electron-conductive liquid metal (LM) 
between electron-insulating LiF and oxides layers via a wetta-
bility-tuning strategy (as shown in Figure 1). Based on a series 
of theoretical modeling and experimental studies on interfacial 
wettability, we selected a stainless steel (SS) substrate to spread 
LM because of its medium wetting properties toward LM. After 
heat treatment in air, the superficially oxidized LM (soLM) were 
coated with Li metal. Because soLM interacted more strongly 
with Li (high wetting) than SS (medium wetting), the Li/soLM 
bilayer could be readily detached off SS. The newly exposed

2. Results and Discussion
2.1. Morphological and Structural Analysis
Spreading properties determine whether or not LM could 
be fabricated as a dense and uniform film. Four typical sub-
strates (SS, Cu, glass, and carbon) having varied wettability 
were elected to test the spreading properties of LM on them. 
Figure 2a shows the contact angle measurements of LM on 
varied substrates. LM does not wet carbon and glass, showing 
high contact angles. It is rather difficult to spread LM on a non-
wetting surface (like carbon and glass) and meanwhile form 
an even and continuous film. Cu and SS could spread LM well 
as demonstrated by low contact angles. Therefore, Cu and SS 
could be used to assist the spreading of LM and the formation 
of artificial HSE.
After annealing in air at 200 °C for 24 h, the top surface of 
LM was oxidized to form an soLM shell, which was thin and 
dense. Li foil was then compressed on the top of soLM. Because 
Li was electrically connected with substrates by external cir-
cuits and the oxidized shell was ion-conductive, LM was gradu-
ally lithiated and solidified to form an even and mechanically 
robust HSE. After holding on pressure (5–10  MPa), the sub-
strate was detached to release the Li/soLM. Figure  2b shows 
that the resultant soLM was so closely attached to Cu that after 
release, most of them remained on Cu and only a low fraction 
of LM residual was transferred to Li. A simple reason is that the 
adhesion between LM and Cu was stronger than that between 
Li and LM. Conversely, in the case of SS-assisted detachment, 
soLM was successfully transferred to Li foil while SS remained 
intact (Figure  2c). Therefore, it can be concluded that the 
transfer technology enabled by tuning interfacial wettability 
could realize the facile fabrication of continuous LM film.

To elucidate the interfacial structures, we analyzed the 
morphological and elemental distribution along the cross sec-
tions of samples during each step of fabrication. As shown in 
Figure S1a (Supporting Information), Li/soLM (no LiF coating) 
has a dense and even layer of soLM (8 µm thick), which closely 
adheres to Li foil. The energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy 
(EDX) elemental mapping images in Figure S1b (Supporting 
Information) show the uniform distribution of Ga, In, and Sn 
on the top of Li foil. In particular, the EDX image of elemental 
images exhibits a thin layer of oxygen signals distribution, indi-
cating that the transfer technology successfully sandwiched the 
oxides layer (Li2O and/or Ga/In/Sn oxides) between LM and Li 
foil. Since LM is flowable and can wet the transfer substrates 
during fabrication, the soLM layer tightly adheres to and fully 
cover the Li metal, forming a flat and smooth top layer as 
shown in Figure S1c (Supporting Information).
Furthermore, an additional thin layer of LiF was sputtered 
on the top of Li/soLM (see Figure  2d). The EDX elemental 
mapping image (Figure 2e) indicates that elemental F is only 
distributed on the top surface. Figure 2f demonstrates that the 
LiF coating is generally flat and the thickness of LiF is ≈70 nm 
(Figure S2, Supporting Information). The X-ray diffraction pat-
terns of Li/soLM/LiF in Figure S3 (Supporting Information) 
show that the Li/soLM/LiF is mainly composed of Li, Li–Ga, 
and In–Li–Sn. No obvious crystalline LiF and Li2O may be due 
to their low amount or amorphous state.
2.2. Simulation of the Transfer Mechanism
To further understand the wetting properties of LM on varied 
substrates, we first conducted molecular dynamics (MD) sim-
ulations. The Lennard-Jones (LJ)[35,60–62] and adaptive inter-
molecular reactive bond order potentials[63–65] were used for 
metal atoms and carbon, respectively. As the interactions 
between metal atoms and sp2-hybrid C could not be accurately 
described by the original LJ potentials, we fitted them by an ab 
initio method in Note S1 (Supporting Information) and used 
the geometric model for fitting in Figures S4-S7 (Supporting 
Information).
Figure 3a–c displays the simulation results of LM on SS, 
Cu, and C, respectively, which satisfactorily confirm the experi-
mental observations in Figure 2a. LM spreads well on Cu and 
however, has a higher contact angle on SS than on Cu. Such a 
difference could be further understood by examining the binary 
phase diagram of metal–Ga systems (Figure 3d, e). When Cu is 
in contact with liquid Ga (melting point is 29.8°C), Ga atoms 
will attack Cu atoms to induce the interdiffusion between Ga 
and Cu. This interfacial wetting is governed by the kinetics of 
interdiffusion. At low temperatures (<254 °C), CuGa2 is nearly 
in equilibrium with liquid Ga. Although the formation of solid-
state CuGa2 may slow down the diffusion of Ga atoms into bulk 
Cu phase, Cu atoms could readily diffuse into liquid Ga owing 
to its relatively high solubility in liquid Ga (4 at.% at 254 °C). In 
contrast, Fe is insoluble in Ga below 400 °C (only ≈1.7 at.% Fe is 
dissolved in Ga at 600 °C). Solid intermetallic compounds (e.g., 
FeGa3) may slow down the diffusion of Ga into bulk Fe while 
the insolubility of Fe in Ga prevents Fe from diffusing into Ga. 
Based on these analyses, we may conclude that as compared 
to SS, Cu induces the spreading of LM mainly because of the 
reactive wetting, thereby showing a much lower contact angle 
than SS. Figure  3c shows that LM forms a round droplet on 
C and exhibits a high contact angle and poor spreading prop-
erties. The MD results can attribute the de-wetting properties 
to the weak interaction between C and LM. As compared to 
wetting Cu and de-wetting C, only SS demonstrates a medium 
interaction strength to LM, which becomes a key factor to assist 
the transfer process.
To understand the release step of the transfer technology, 
we built heterogeneous interface models and calculated the 
work of separation (Wsep) between LiGa alloy phases and var-
ious substrates using density functional theory (DFT). Wsep 
is the energy required to separate the two slabs as shown in 
Figure  3g.  To  match two incoherent slabs, we followed the 
strategy of Jacobsen et al. to calculate the 2D strain tensor of 
the LM interfaces first and selected the interface models that 
have the lowest strain and are computationally feasible for the 
following DFT calculations.[66] Figure S8 (Supporting Informa-
tion) displays all typical models for Cu/Ga and SS/Ga interfaces. 
CuGa2 was used as the substrate because Cu alloys with Ga. As 
Ga in LM attacks most other metals by diffusing into their lat-
tice, for simplicity, LiGa (instead of Sn and In) was considered 
to represent the lithiated LM. Figure 3h,i presents the Wsep of 
various interfaces with respect to the distance. The interface 
of SS/LiGa has a lower Wsep than that of Li2O/LiGa, indicating 
that removing the substrate of Li/soLM/SS will readily detach 
SS through the SS/soLM interface. However, in the case of Li/
soLM/Cu, the high Wsep (Figure 3h) and relatively large area (as 
illustrated in Figure 3f) of the CuGa2/LiGa interface lead to the 
breakup of LiGa, thereby failing to transfer lithiated LM from 
Cu to the Li metal completely (as shown in Figure 2b).
The simulation of interfacial properties reveals that tuning 
the interfacial wettability allows us to spread a uniform and 
thin layer of intractable LM on Li metal. In addition, under-
standing and manipulating the strengths of various interfaces 
in Li/soLM/substrates enable the easy release of substrates, 
eventually realizing a successful transfer.
2.3. In Situ Characterization of Li Deposition

growth of Li metal, we observed the in situ growth of Li metal 
on bare Li and Li/soLM/LiF by an optical microscope. Upon 
negative polarization, dendrites were deposited on the surface 
of bare Li metal (Figure 4a). In contrast, Li/soLM/LiF induced 
uniform deposition of Li metal, which could be identified with 
the increased thickness of Li (Figure  4b). The top layers of 
soLM/LiF remained intact, implying an underneath plating. To 
further verify the growth mechanism, we disassembled the cell

consisting of top artificial HSE and Li metal. More importantly, 
the newly deposited Li could be distinguished from the original 
Li metal (which could also be compared to the un-plated Li as 
shown in Figure 9, Supporting Information). Li was nucleated 
at the Li/soLM interface, where was the farthest spots that

stark contrast, the surface of PTFE film in contact with soLM

surfaces of Li/soLM and Li/soLM/LiF, therefore preventing 
dendritic growth on the top and facilitating the underneath 
plating of Li metal.
Given the good insulating properties, it is intuitive for one 
to think of using only Li2O and/or LiF as the SEI. However, it 
should be emphasized that an artificial SEI must also conduct 
Li-ions well. We thus calculated the migration barriers of Li 
ions in bulk Li2O, LiF, and Li2Ga. As shown in Figure  4f, Li 
ions have to overcome high energy barriers of 1.71 and 2.66 eV 
through Li2O and LiF, respectively. To suppress the dendritic 
growth, a relatively thick layer is usually needed to maintain the 
mechanical strength of solid electrolytes. However, high energy 
barriers make thick solid electrolytes difficult to conduct Li-ions 
efficiently. Li2Ga exhibits a much lower energy barrier (0.43 eV) 
for Li-ion conduction than Li2O and LiF. Thus, lithiated LM can 
serve as a much better Li-ion conductor than Li2O and LiF. As 
lithiated LM is also a good electron conductor, LM-based solid 
electrolytes must be electrically passivated. Based on the above

2.4. Electrochemical Properties of Li/soLM/LiF Anodes
Figure 5a shows the electrochemical cycling properties of 
symmetric cells using bare Li and Li/soLM/LiF, respectively, 
at varied test conditions. The electrolyte was 1 m Lithium 
bis(trifluoromethanesulphonyl)imide (LiTFSI) in an electro-
lyte of 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME). 
At 1 mAh cm−2, both cells exhibit similar plating/stripping 
voltage gaps (about 12  mV) for less than 500 h probably 
because the current density (0.5 mA cm−2) is relatively low and 
the SEI does not build up at the beginning. With the increase 
of cycling number, the average voltage of bare Li starts to 
increase rapidly, which might be caused by the consumption of 
electrolytes and accumulation of SEI. Especially, after 1000 h, 
the bare Li cell exhibits noisy voltage signals, which are super-
imposed on a generally increasing average voltage. The noisy 
voltage response is a good indicator for short circuits (due to 
Li “dendrites”).[68,69] Eventually, the cell broke down in 1100 h. 
In stark contrast, the Li/soLM/LiF cell could be cycled with 
low average overpotentials (≈15 mV) for more than 4000 h. By 
zooming in the voltage profiles, one can see that the plating/
stripping voltage decreases from 25  mV in the first cycle to 
the minimum of 12 mV in the 125th cycle (500 h). The initial 
decrease in voltages can be attributed to electrode activation, 
which was also observed in previous studies.[70] After activa-

Figure 5b presents the cycling performance at a high current 
density of 5 mA cm−2. As compared to the low-rate properties 
in Figure  5a, the cyclability of the bare Li cell is dramatically 
reduced. A stable voltage profile could be maintained for only 
50 h. After about 85 h, the bare Li cell is broken. A significant 
increase in current density likely induces non-uniform plating 
and thus leads to dendritic growth, which unavoidably increases 
the surface area and consumes electrolyte to form more SEI. 
The newly formed SEI would in turn aggravate the situation 
of non-uniform plating. Such a worse loop accelerates the 
increase in plating/stripping voltage until the breakdown. Li/
soLM/LiF demonstrates a mechanically robust artificial HSE, 
which offers two protective effects: 1) the high ion-conductivity 
of the artificial HSE could homogenize the Li transport to avoid 
the concentration of current density; 2) the mechanical proper-
ties of the HSE help to suppress the dendritic growth. There-
fore, the Li/soLM/LiF cell could maintain the stable cycling 
overpotentials at a high current density of 5 mA cm−2 as well 
as at low rates. A similar conclusion of good cycling (for 1400 h) 
is further demonstrated at high loading cells (5 mAh cm−2) 
as shown in Figure  5c. The above analyses on varied rates 
and loading tests confirm that the artificial HSE dramatically 
improves the cyclability of Li-metal anodes.
More importantly, Li/soLM/LiF exhibits much higher cycling 
stability in ester electrolytes than bare Li. Figure  5d shows 
that Li/soLM/LiF can cycle for >1600 h at a high capacity 
(5 mAh cm−2) in ethylene carbonate (EC) and dimethyl car-
bonate (DMC). Despite a slight voltage increase after 1200 h, 
such a cycling property of Li/soLM/LiF far exceeds that of bare 
Li. Li metal usually does not cycle well in carbonate electro-
lytes because of the poor deposition behavior, which has been 
reported previously.[71–73] However, carbonates have wide poten-
tial windows and are used for high voltage systems to increase 
the energy density of batteries. Therefore, cycling a Li-metal 
anode well in carbonates is equally important for building high-
energy batteries.

LiFePO4 and sulfur were chosen as the cathodes to verify 
whether the good cyclability of Li/soLM/LiF in symmetric 
cells could be applied to full cells. Li/soLM/LiF and bare Li 
were first assembled with LiFePO4 using carbonate electrolytes 
(labeled as Li/soLM/LiF||LiFePO4 and Li||LiFePO4, respectively). 
Figure 6a shows that Li/soLM/LiF||LiFePO4 can be charged and 
discharged reversibly with a high capacity about 150 mAh g−1.

peaks (3.28 and 3.56  V) in cyclic voltammetric (CV) curves. 
The narrow gap between two CV peaks indicates good revers-
ibility. Figure  6b presents the cycling properties of Li/soLM/
LiF||LiFePO4 and Li||LiFePO4 cells at 0.1C. A N/P ratio of 3.43

and rapidly decays in about 80 cycles. Given the well-recog-
nized good cyclability of LiFePO4, the cell degradation can be 
attributed to the poor cycling properties of the bare Li anode.

In addition to pairing with intercalation cathodes, Li-metal 
anodes are also widely used in Li–S batteries to unleash the 
ultrahigh energy density of sulfur cathodes. We prepared Ru 
nanoparticles on reduced graphene oxide (Ru@rGO) to load 
sulfur (S/Ru@rGO) as shown in Figure S13 (Supporting Infor-
mation). Ru serves as a catalyst to accelerate the conversion of 
polysulfides while rGO could facilitate electron conduction. It 
should be noted that polysulfides are soluble in the liquid elec-
trolyte of DOL and DME. Polysulfides migration toward anodes 
will degrade the cycling performance of Li–S batteries. Poly-
sulfides absorbents are usually needed to confine polysulfides 
inside sulfur cathodes to mitigate the detrimental “shuttling 
effect.” We intentionally added no absorbents and allowed 
polysulfides to migrate. The shuttling of polysulfides offers an 
indicator to examine the role of artificial HSE. As shown in 
Figure 6d, the resultant Li/soLM/LiF||S/Ru@rGO cell exhibits 
two typical voltage plateaus at 2.35 and 2.07  V, which corre-
spond to the conversion reactions from long-chain S8 to Li2S4 
and from Li2S4 to Li2S2/Li2S, respectively.[76,77] A specific capacity 
of 1257.78 mAh g−1 was delivered in the first cycle, showing an 
initial CE of 99.1%. The reductive scan of the CV curves dem-
onstrates two distinct peaks at 2.3 V and 2.05 V, which are in 
agreement with the two plateaus in the discharge curves. A 
high capacity and well-separated redox peaks indicate that Ru@
rGO could appropriately catalyze the conversion reactions of 
the Li–S system. Despite having a similar initial capacity, the 
Li||S/Ru@rGO cell loses its capacity much more rapidly than 
the Li/soLM/LiF||S/Ru@rGO cell upon cycling (Figure 6e).
To elucidate the degradation mechanism, we disassembled 
the cycled cells to observe the cross-section of their anodes. 
Figure  6f shows that the artificial HSE with a relatively even 
thickness is coated on the cycled lithium, indicating that the 
plating of metallic lithium does not break the HSE. The EDX 
mapping images show that LM elements (Ga and Sn) are dis-
tributed uniformly while the signals of F and S are mainly pre-
sent at the top region (Figure 6g). Elemental F originates from 
the LiF coating and decomposed electrolyte. In contrast, the 
morphology of the cycled bare Li anode is changed to rough and 
dendritic aggregates (Figure 6h). The EDX mapping images in 
Figure 6i indicate that sulfur species are distributed unevenly 
along the thickness direction. It can be deduced that sulfur spe-
cies are diffused from cathodes and corrode the Li-metal anode, 
leading to non-uniform plating of Li during recharge. Dendritic 
growth in turn facilitates the diffusion of S species into the Li 
anode (Figure S14, Supporting Information). The increase of 
surface area due to dendritic morphology causes the decompo-
sition of electrolytes, redistributing F elements along the cross 
section.
The results of LiFePO4 and Li–S battery systems allow us to 
conclude that the artificial HSE significantly suppresses the den-
dritic growth of Li metal owing to mechanical strength, high ion, 
and poor electron conductivities. Other factors that also make 
important contributions and should not be overlooked are the 
flowability of LM and facile fabrication via the wetting-tuning 
transfer technology because only the dense, even, and sand-
wiched structure made the above advantages possible.
3. Conclusion
We have developed an artificial hybrid solid electrolyte by sand-
wiching LM between LiF and oxides via a wettability-tuning 
strategy. To successfully transfer a dense, continuous, and uni-
form LM, our DFT modeling and molecular dynamic simula-
tion was first conducted to reveal that the wetting properties 
on SS are appropriate for spreading and detaching LM film 
at the same time because the medium interaction between SS 
and LM is neither strong nor weak. Such a sandwich structure 
takes advantage of the high ion conductivity and flowability 
of LM and meanwhile passivates LM to eliminate its electron 
conduction and catalytic effects. The resultant HSE offers high 
ion conductive, electron insulating, and mechanical supporting 
properties as an ideal solid electrolyte layer for Li-metal anodes. 
Therefore, the HSE-coated Li-metal composite anodes exhibit 
superior cycling performance for over 4000 h at 0.5 mA cm−2 
and 1 mAh cm−2 in a symmetric cell. The high stability of the 
HSE was also demonstrated in LiFePO4 and Li–S batteries to 
enhance their specific energy and cyclability.
