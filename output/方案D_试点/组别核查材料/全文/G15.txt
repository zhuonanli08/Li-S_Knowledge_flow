Synthesis of Poly(allyl glycidyl ether)s (PAGE), the modiﬁcation of allyl side chains of PAGE with 3-((2- 
(2-(2-mercaptoethoxy)ethoxy)ethyl)thio)propanenitrile (CN) and furfuryl mercaptan (FM) (CN/FM-PAGE) 
for Li-salt dissociation and crosslinking, respectively, is reported. A CN/FM-PAGE with 3% crosslinking 
ratio as a solid polymer electrolyte (SPE) for Li–S battery exhibits the highest Li-ion conductivity of 
1.01 × 10 –4 S cm –1 with a [Li]/[O] ratio of 0.2 at 30 °C. Moreover, the SPEs manifest favorable operation up 
to 80 charge–discharge cycles with high coulombic eﬃciency ( > 95%) and good lithium plating/stripping 
performance.

1. Introduction 
Excessive use of poly(ethylene oxide)s (PEOs) is seen in com- 
mercially important ﬁelds such as lithium polymer batteries [1–4] , 
dye-sensitized solar cells [5] , drug delivery [6] , and biocompatibil- 
ity control [7] . PEO-based polymers with side chains enable fur- 
ther modiﬁcation, thus allowing the tunability of these valuable 
materials [8–10] . This enables new polymer designs for eﬃcient 
polymer electrolytes. High molecular weight PEOs, which are semi- 
crystalline, suffer from low ion conductivity at room temperature. 
In an attempt to alleviate low ion conductivity problem, various 
strategies, such as polymer blending, ceramic in polymer/polymer 
in ceramic design, nanoparticle additions, ionic liquid additivies, 
dynamic covalent bonds and crosslinking network have been ap- 
plied [11–15] . A simultaneously high Li-ion conductivity and phase 
stability of unmodiﬁed PEO is diﬃcult to achieve, the structural

modiﬁcation of PEO is the only way to achieve high ion conduc-

The main reason for the ion-conduction property of PEO is 
the presence of electronegative oxygen atoms in their backbones, 
which induce ﬂexibility and exhibit low T g [ 17 , 18 ]. In our recent 
study, we demonstrated that ionic conductivity is enhanced by 
suppressing the formation of crystal domain structures of PEO [9] . 
In this viewpoint, the advantage of chemical functionality and the 
inherent physical properties (low T g , lack of crystallinity) make 
poly(allyl glycidyl ether) (PAGE) an alternate candidate for PEO 
[19] . This tunability and facile reactivity of PAGE enable it to be 
applied in a variety of applications, such as ion-conducting elec- 
trolytes, therapeutics, and catalysts [19–21] . The allyl side chain is 
an asset for a facile modiﬁcation, which made us select allyl gly- 
cidyl ether as the monomer. 
Approaches such as cationic or anionic ring opening polymer- 
ization have been utilized to synthesize the PAGE with a variety 
of functionalities. However, optimized polymerization conditions 
for AGE and understanding for controlling the side reactions is 
still a challenging task. Many attempts have been made by var- 
ious groups to synthesize PAGE [ 22 , 23 ]. Erberich et al. [22] car- 
ried out an extensive study on the polymerization chemistry of 
AGE, however, the authors concluded that the polymerization is 
only controlled to 80% conversion. Hu et al. [24] synthesized PAGE

Good processability, ﬂexibility, and interfacial compatibility, 
along with improved safety are the required characteristics of solid 
polymer electrolytes (SPEs) [26–28] . The importance of SPEs stems 
from the fact that they act both as electrolytes and separators 
[2] . Since there is an all-time high interest in high capacity, high 
power, and high voltage lithium-ion batteries [29] , to meet these 
requirements, the electrolyte should also possess a high ion con- 
ductivity, dielectric constant, and anodic oxidation potential, along 
with demonstrating increased interaction with Li ions [ 9 , 30 ]. SPEs 
with high ion conductivity showed improved performance in bat- 
teries [30] . Possible methods to control the dielectric constant in- 
clude strategies such as changing the blend ratios of the polymer 
electrolytes [ 31 , 32 ], and the introduction of inorganic additives 
with a high dielectric constant such as TiO 2 , BaTiO 3 , and mont- 
morillonite [ 33 , 34 ]. In our recent paper, we described that double- 
comb poly (siloxane) SPEs show the enhanced dielectric constant 
and dimensional stability with crosslinking via quaternization [35] . 
Furthermore, quaternization improves dielectric behavior due to 
improved polarization and more intense oscillation of molecules 
[36] . Having polar groups such as –O–, –S–, –NH– etc., are effec- 
tive for dissolving lithium salts, besides enhancing the dielectric 
constant of the polymer [37] . The presence of the lowest unoc- 
cupied molecular orbital and their high electrochemical stability 
make the nitrile group an indispensable functional group for de- 
signing high-energy batteries with a wide electrochemical window 
[38] . Some of the examples of electrolytes based on nitrile groups 
include acetonitrile [ 39 , 40 ], succinonitrile [ 41 , 42 ], polyacrylonitrile 
[ 43 , 44 ], poly(ethyl α-cyanoacrylate) [ 45 , 46 ], and polyvinyl alcohol- 
beta-cyanide ethyl ether [ 47 , 48 ]. 
Polymers with structure-property relationships that contrive 
high energy density could be used to enhance Li-ion technology 
[49] . Li–S batteries have a high theoretical capacity of 1672 mAh 
g –1 , which is more than 6 times as high as lithium-ion batter- 
ies [50–52] . Hence, an electrolyte which is stable against Lithium 
metal anode is needed for high speciﬁc energy batteries with sul- 
fur cathodes [53] . The Li–S battery has a preferred choice because 
sulfur is cheap and with easy availability. The problems of Li–S bat- 
teries, such as a short cycling ability and low charge eﬃciency, are 
closely related to the dissolution of lithium polysulﬁde (Li 2 S x ) in 
a liquid electrolyte [ 50 , 51 ]. Replacing the liquid electrolyte with a 
SPE is the best way to palliate polysulﬁde dissolution problem [54] . 
Herein, we synthesized PAGEs by an anionic ring opening 
polymerization (AROP) using the benzenedimethanol/phosphazene 
base initiator system at room temperature. A facile modiﬁcation 
of allyl groups was performed with side chains possessing nitrile 
and furan groups via a thiol–ene click reaction. Crosslinking of the 
CN/FM-PAGEs was performed with bismaleimide via a Diels-Alder 
reaction. The crosslinked CN/FM-PAGE was used as an SPE in the 
construction of a coin cell for Li–S battery.

2.6. Crosslinking of CN/FM-PAGE for solid polymer electrolytes (SPEs) 
LiTFSI salt and CN/FM-PAGEs were mixed and crosslinked via 
a Diels-Alder reaction with bismaleimide (Scheme S1 in the SI). 
CN/FM-PAGE and bismaleimide were dissolved in a 1:1 furan to 
maleimide ratio with a small amount (1.0 ml) of DMF. After re- 
moving the solvent at 120 °C under vacuum, the crosslinking was 
carried out by Diels-Alder reaction at 65 °C for a minimum of 
24 h. The degree of crosslinking was varied with time (24, 48 
and 72 h), and the SPEs so formed are designated as P3, P4, and 
P5 which corresponds to 3, 4, and 5% of crosslinked CN/FM-PAGE, 
respectively. Non crosslinked CN/FM-PAGE was designated as P0. 
24 h was the minimum time required for the ﬁlm formation. The 
crosslinking was conﬁrmed by FT-IR spectroscopy (Fig. S5 in the SI) 
FT-IR (cm –1 ): 1180 (C–N–C, Diels-Alder adduct), 1700 (C = O, Diels- 
Alder adduct), and 1378 cm –1 (C–N, Diels-Alder adduct). 
2.7. Measurements of Li-ion conductivity 
The Li-ion conductivity was measured with an electrochemi- 
cal impedance spectroscopy (Solartron, SI 1280b) over a frequency 
range from 1 Hz to 20 kHz with an amplitude of 100 mV. All mea- 
surements were carried out in a temperature range of 30 to 80 °C. 
An Espec Model SH-241 benchtop type temperature and humid- 
ity chamber were used to maintain a constant temperature when 
the ion conductivity was measured. The prepared SPEs were sand- 
wiched with stainless steel electrodes (1.2 cm diameter) using a 
Swagelok cell. Their frequency region and resistance were checked 
by Bode and Nyquist plots, respectively. Impedances obtained by 
the measurements were used to calculate the ion conductivity by 
the following equation: 
σ = d 
RA 
where σ (S cm –1 ) is the ion conductivity, d (cm) is the thickness of 
the membrane, R (ohm) is the resistance, and A (cm 2 ) is the area 
of the membrane. The impedance of each sample was measured 
four times to ensure reproducibility. 
2.8. Cell preparation and assembly 
CN/FM PAGE prepared from PAGE (0.450 g) was dissolved in 
1.5 ml of DMF. Bismaleimide was dissolved in a 1:1 furan to 
maleimide ratio with a polymer solution. LiTFSI with [Li]:[O] = 0.2 
was added and stirred at 120 °C. The well-mixed solution was di- 
rectly cast onto a Celgard 3501 separator as porous support with 
a doctor blade and then heated in a vacuum oven at 65 °C for the 
Diels-Alder reaction to proceed for 24 h for attaining P3 (50 μm, 
thickness). A sulfur cathode was prepared by applying a slurry 
coating on an Al foil. We used S–C composites as an active ma- 
terial, which was synthesized by heat-treating a mixture of Ketjen 
black and elemental sulfur at 155 °C for 24 h in an Ar atmosphere. 
A slurry was cast on the Al foil after mixing the synthesized S–C 
composites, Super P, and PVDF in DMA, and then it was completely 
dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 24 h. To investigate the elec- 
trochemical behavior of crosslinked CN/FM-PAGE (P3), a 2032-type 
coin cell assembly was constructed. To activate the Li-ion transport 
pathway, 40 μL of 1 M LiTFSI (dioxolane: dimethoxyethane = 1:1 
vol%) solution was added on top of P3, which was stacked between 
the commercial Li metal and sulfur cathode in an Ar-ﬁlled glove 
box (H 2 O < 0.3 ppm). A similar procedure was employed to form 
bare Celgard separator. 
2.9. Electrochemical performance 
Cyclic voltammetry (potentiostat mode, WBSC30 0 0S, Won- 
A tech, Republic of Korea) analysis was conducted to con- 
ﬁrm the electrochemical stability with a Li metal/P3/Li metal 
cell conﬁguration. With the same cell components, Li-ion strip-

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Ionic conductivity of SPEs 
As shown in Fig. 1 , after comparing the obtained temperature 
dependent Li-ion conductivities of non crosslinked CN/FM-PAGE 
(P0) and PAGE ( Scheme 1 ), the P0 has the highest Li-ion conduc- 
tivity. The nitrile groups in the side chain of CN/FM-PAGEs allows 
the dissociation of LiTFSI salt as well as PEO-Li + complex, which 
further increases the Li-ion availability for transport, thereby in- 
creasing the ionic conductivity [55] . When we compared the ionic 
conductivities of P0–P5, we observed that the increase in crosslink- 
ing ratio decreased the ionic conductivity, as shown in Fig. 1 . The 
higher crosslinking ratio hindered the ionic transportation as the 
polymer chain mobility decreases [56–61] . Among P3, P4, and P5, 
the ionic conductivity decreases with crosslinking and P3 showed 
the highest value of 1.01 × 10 −4 S cm –1 at 30 °C. The polyether 
backbone, along with side chains, provide hopping site for Li + ions 
and pendant nitrile groups increases dissociation of lithium salt 
which also helped in enhancing the ionic conductivity. 
The effect of Li salt concentration on the conductivity was 
also examined using P3, as it showed the highest ionic conductiv- 
ity among crosslinked CN/FM-PAGEs. Three different [Li]/[O] ratios 
(0.15, 0.2, and 0.25) were tried, as shown in Fig. 2 , and highest Li- 
ion conductivity was observed when the ratio [Li]/[O] was 0.2. A 
decrease in ionic conductivity with increase in Li-salt concentra- 
tion is due to the formation of aggregated species, which hinders 
ion mobility with an increase in salt concentration and this has 
already been reported [ 11 , 16 , 19 ]. 
3.2. Thermal analysis of crosslinked CN/FM-PAGE 
A clear comparison of the T g ’s showed that it is increasing by 
increasing the crosslinking ratios from P0 to P5, as evident from 
Fig. 3 . The increase in crosslinking ratio tends to decrease poly- 
mer chain mobility, and hence the T g values increase from −46 to 
−13 °C. This correlation of T g with crosslinking ratios and chain 
mobility was also observed by others [ 35 , 62–64 ]. It also explains 
the decrease in Li-ion conductivity by increasing crosslinking ra- 
tios from P0–P5.

3.4. Cell performance 
From the electrochemical results of the CV and galvanostatic cy- 
cle tests with Li/P3/Sulfur cathode cell conﬁguration, the P3 elec- 
trolyte was available for Li–S batteries in consideration of its high 
electrochemical stability within the operating potential (1.5–3.0 V) 
and its ability to suppress the formation of Li dendrites. Two ev- 
ident reduction peaks were observed, which indicated the reduc- 
tion of S 8 to Li 2 S 8 and Li 2 S 6 in succession at the ﬁrst small cur- 
rent peak near 2.3 V, followed by Li 2 S 6 undergoing a serial reduc- 
tion from Li 2 S 4 to Li 2 S 2 and Li 2 S at the second current peak near 
2.0 V ( Fig. 5 a). One prominent oxidation current peak near 2.35 V 
was the reﬂection of the reverse processes. The CV curve corre- 
sponded to each of the two plateaus in the discharge cycle and 
the one plateau in the charge cycle, which has been known as typ- 
ical Li–S battery potential curves ( Fig. 5 b) [75] . Since there were no 
pronounced distortions of the current peaks in the depicted cyclic 
voltammetry graph, all components of P3 might be considered reli- 
able and chemically stable for utilization as polymer membranes in 
Li–S batteries. The electrochemical performance was conducted at 
60 °C with the same cell conﬁguration and a sulfur loading amount 
of 1.25 mg s cm –2 . 
The cycling performance of the Li–S cell was measured at a 
current density of 0.2 C ( Fig. 5 c). Although a rapid capacity drop 
occurred in the ﬁrst few cycles, the average capacity fade rate of 
the ﬁrst 5 cycles was 3.8%, while that of the last 5 cycles after 80 
was 0.33%. The above results meant that cycling was stabilized af- 
ter a few early cycles. Furthermore, the coulombic eﬃciency of the 
cell prepared with P3 cast celgard 3501 was remarkably superior 
to that of the cell prepared with bare celgard 3501 at even lower 
sulfur loading amounts (Fig. S8 in the SI). Unlike the reference cell, 
in the cell prepared with P3 cast celgard 3501, lithium polysulﬁdes 
were unable to diffuse to the anode, while lithium ions could be 
transported; thus, surface contamination could be prevented. The 
pore-ﬁlled membrane with P3 plays a crucial role of a membrane 
for only transferring Li-ion and for blocking lithium polysulﬁde 
(Li 2 S x , 4 ≤x ≤8) shuttle within a coin cell. The above process en- 
abled the favorable operation of the Li–S battery while maintain- 
ing a high coulombic eﬃciency ( > 95%). The results veriﬁed that 
the P3, i.e., crosslinked CN/FM-PAGE could effectively function as a 
SPE in a Li–S battery. 
4. Conclusions 
Poly(allyl glycidyl ether) (PAGE) was synthesized with con- 
trolled molecular weight via AROP. The modiﬁcation of PAGE was 
successful through a thiol-ene click reaction with 3-((2-(2-(2- 
mercaptoethoxy)ethoxy)ethyl)thio)propanenitrile (METPN) and fur- 
furyl mercaptane for lithium salt dissociation and crosslinking, 
respectively. The CN/FM-PAGE was crosslinked by a Diels-Alder 
reaction using bismaleimide for the preparation of stable SPE 
ﬁlms. The Li-ion conductivity of crosslinked CN/FM-PAGE (P3) is 
1.01 × 10 −4 S cm –1 at 30 °C, which is achieved due to the lithium 
salt dissociation by the high dielectric nitrile groups. The P3 as an 
SPE exhibited stable lithium stripping/plating behavior against the 
formation of Li dendrites with high coulombic eﬃciency ( > 95%) in 
Li–S battery. The above results endorse the fact that good macro-
