ABSTRACT: Li7La3Zr2O12 (LLZO) is one of the most promising
candidate solid electrolytes for high-safety solid-state batteries.
However, similar to other solid electrolytes containing volatile
components during high-temperature sintering, the preparation of
densified LLZO with high conductivity is challenging involving the
complicated gas−liquid−solid sintering mechanism. Further attention
on establishing low-cost laborastory-scale preparation craft platform of
LLZO ceramic is also required. This work demonstrates a “pellet on
gravel” sintering strategy, which is performed in a MgO crucible and box
furnace under ambient air without any special equipment or expensive
consumables. In addition, the competition between lithium loss from
the sintering system and internal grain densification is critically studied,
whereas the influences of particle surface energy, Li-loss amount, and
initial excess Li2O amount are uncovered. Based on the sintering behavior and mechanism, optimized craft platform for preparing
dense LLZO solid electrolytes including mixing, calcination, particle tailoring and sintering is provided. Finally, exemplary Ta-doped
LLZO pellets with 2 wt % La2Zr2O7 additives sintered at 1260−1320 °C for 20 min deliver Li+ conductivities of ∼9 × 10−4 S cm−1 at
25 °C, relative densities of >96%, and a dense cross-sectional microstructure. As a practical demonstration, LLZO solid electrolyte
with optimized performance is applied in both Li−Li symmetric cells and Li−S batteries. This work sheds light on the practical
production of high-quality LLZO ceramics and provides inspiration for sintering ceramics containing volatile compounds.
KEYWORDS: Li7La3Zr2O12, solid electrolyte, volatile Li-loss, densification, craft platform
1. INTRODUCTION
Solid-state batteries (SSBs) based on metallic Li anode and solid
electrolyte are next-generation alternatives to commercial Li-ion
batteries (LiBs), because of their safety and potential high
energy density.1 Garnet-type Li7La3Zr2O12 (LLZO) is one of the
few electrolytes that are electrochemically stable against Li
metal.2,3 LLZO allows the preparation into pellets, tapes, and
films under ambient air via low-cost conventional solid-state
reaction methods.4 Garnet-type Li-stuffed solid electrolyte
[Li+]3+2x[Ln3+]3[M(6−x)+]2O12 is first reported at 2003 by
Thangadurai et al. in the composition of M = Nb, Ta
(Li5La3Nb2O12 and Li5La3Ta2O12, Li5-garnet).5 The Li-ion
conductivity of Li5-garnet is 10−6−10−5 S cm−1 at room
temperature, which is far from practical application demand.6−9
Four years later, Li7La3Zr2O12 (Li7-Garnet), with a room-
temperature conductivity of 3 × 10−4 S cm−1, is reported by the
same group.10 Ta-doped LLZO (Ta-LLZO) with conductivity
∼1 × 10−3 cm−1 is reported by Li et al. in 2012, which shed light
on the application of Li-Garnets in solid-state batteries.
Therefore, numerous attempts on LLZO have been reported
in recent years,11 focusing on the structure, doping, sintering,
chemical stability/interfacial resistance against humidity/Li
anode, and battery fabrication.
However, obtaining dense LLZO ceramics with favorable
conductivity remains a challenge. Huang et al. proposed a gas−
liquid−solid sintering mechanism for Zr-site doped LLZO.12
Similar to volatile Na2O in Na-β″-Al2O3, respectively, the Li2O/
Li gas evaporates from LLZO bed powder to fulfill the sintering
container, and then condenses to a liquid phase at the particle
surfaces to enhance the material transportation between solid-
phase raw grains.13,14 The key in the LLZO sintering is
manipulating the highly reactive Li2O/Li gas atmosphere, which
can be hardly restricted during high-temperature sintering
followed by unsatisfactory Li-loss. Sustained efforts have been
made to tackle the serious Li-loss issue. First, green pellets are

contains 10%−15% excess Li (vs Li composition in chemical
formula) and the mother powder (2−40 g) contains 10%−40%

Besides, pressure applied and sealed-chamber involved sintering
techniques such as hot pressing, field-assisted sintering, and
spark plasma sintering has been developed.23−29 Yet, costive
equipment with chamber in regulated shapes is required, which
is not suitable for thin tape-cast samples. Furthermore, Pt
crucible has been adopted, which is softened at a high sintering
temperature (>1100 °C) and reacts with Li2O and O2 gases to
form adhesive LixPtOy compounds, affording a semi-sealed
chamber inside the crucible.30 Also, Pt weaves was involved to
separate the green bodies from the mother powder.12,31−33
Although the mother powder can be inexpensive Li-containing
compounds, such as LixZrOy or Nb doped LLZO,31 inevitable Pt
loss substantially increases the cost. Typically, Pt crucible and
weaves in total weight of 500 g would lose ∼40 g (8%) after
150−200 times of sintering process in one year. Therefore,
developing highly efficient but low-cost sintering strategy for
LLZO is needed.
Fortunately, the densification speed of LLZO is fast that dense
ceramics can be obtained after sintering at 1250 °C for only 1
min with a fast heating rate of 5 °C min−1.12 If the densification
speed of LLZO is faster than that of Li loss in green bodies,
ceramics can reach the final sintering stage without using the
mother powder. Hu et al. developed a flash sintering method for
LLZO ceramic densification in seconds,34−38 which can be
realized without any external mother powder or Li source under
ultrahigh heating speed and temperatures. However, the
conductivity and relative density of the LLZO electrolyte by
flash sintering require further optimization, compared to
conventional solid-state reactions or hot pressing.39−41 Wen et
al. provided a non-mother-powder sintering strategy using a
small-volume MgO crucible and Pt support to decrease the
serious Li loss and Pt loss.42 Dense Li6.4La3Zr1.4Ta0.6O12 samples
were obtained inside Φ30 mm (diameter) × H 27 mm (height)
MgO crucible with 10% excess Li inside the green body.
However, Pt is still applied to separate the LLZO green pellet
from the MgO crucible.43−45 On the other hand, the sintering
behavior and mechanism in the conventional solid-state reaction
preparation of LLZO or other ceramics containing volatile
components is scarcely studied.46 In particular, the competition
between densification speed and Li loss is the key issue in
sintering LLZO via conventional methods, which requires more
attention.
This work aims to develop repeatable and reliable laboratory-
scale ceramic preparation process platform for LLZO solid
electrolytes containing volatile components. First, primary
“pellet-on-gravel” sintering architecture without any Pt usage
is demonstrated for sintering Ta-doped LLZO solid electrolytes.
Second, in order to solve the poor consistency of LLZO
ceramics prepared with pellet-on-gravel architecture, detailed
study on the influence of particle size (surface energy), ramping
rate (Li-loss amount before densification), weight of green pellet
(initial excess Li2O amount), and temperature (thermal drive
force) on the sintering results was conducted to understand the
behavior of the competition between Li-loss from sintering
system and internal grain densification. Third, optimized
operation procedure including raw material mixing, cubic
LLZO phase powder calcination, particle top-down tailoring,
and green pellet sintering is provided for the preparation of
dense LLZO ceramics based on the understanding of the
sintering behavior and mechanism. Orthogonal experiments
applying optimized process platform for Ta-doped LLZO with
0−3 wt % La2Zr2O7 additives sintered at 1320−1220 °C are
conducted to determine the best composition and sintering
conditions. Finally, Li−Li symmetric and Li−S cells are
fabricated to evaluate the electrochemical application of Ta-
LLZO ceramics produced on a laboratory scale.
2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
2.1. Powders. Ta-LLZO, Nb-LLZO, and La2Zr2O7 (LZO)
powders were prepared via a conventional solid-state reaction method.
Taking Li6.5La3Zr2Ta0.5O12 with 10% excess Li (Ta5Li10) as an
example, starting precursors (200 g in total, as-designed product 160 g)
of LiOH·H2O (Aladdin, AR, 98%, 10% excess), La2O3 (SCRC, AR,
99.95%), ZrO2 (Aladdin, AR, 99%), and Ta2O5 (Aladdin, AR, 99.5%)
were planetary-milled (MITR, Model YXQM-8L) with the stochio-
metric ratio inside a nylon tank (MITR, Model MC-1L, internal
dimensions Φ 11 cm × H 10 cm) at 200 rpm (revolution speed,
rotation speed = 400 rpm) for 8 h, during which 700 g of yttrium-
stabilized ZrO2 (YSZ) balls (Φ 10 mm 100 g + Φ 5 mm 600 g) were
used as the milling media and 300 g isopropanol (IPA) was applied as
the solvent. The resulting viscous slurry was dried at 55−65 °C for 12 h
and the dried powder was sieved over 20 grit. 80 g of as-sieved raw
powder were calcinated at 950 °C for 6 h with 2 °C min−1 heating rate in
a Al2O3 crucible (Φ 8 cm × H 8 cm) without lid for facile evaporation of
solvents and H2O gas. As-synthesized Ta5Li10 powder were sieved
over 80 grit and restored in polypropylene bottles. Detailed preparation
procedure for Ta5Li6 (Li6.5La3Zr2Ta0.5O12 with 6% excess Li),
Nb5Li15, and LZO can be found in section SI_0.1 in the Supporting
Information.
2.2. Gravels. The detailed preparation of MgO lid for MgO crucible
is available in Figure S1 and section SI_0.2 in the Supporting
Information. Six grams (6 g) of Nb5Li15 was put in a new MgO crucible
(internal dimensions of Φ 26 mm × H 33 mm) and then sintered at
1280−1340 °C for 20−120 min and sealed with MgO lid. As shown in
Figure S2b in the Supporting Information, the powder was densified to
a block after sintering. The block was crashed by agate mortar and
pestle, and then sieved over 20 grits and 80 grits, respectively. Particles
in sizes smaller than 80 grit were collected and denoted as Gravel_80,
which were used as fine particles to separate stacked green pellets.
Particles in sizes >80 grit and <20 grit are collected and denoted as
Gravel_20, which were used as coarse particles to separate green pellets
from the bottom of the MgO crucible.
2.3. Ceramics: Consistency Test. Four batches of 20 g Ta5Li10
powder (prepared in section 2.1) added with 2 wt % LZO (denoted as
Ta5Li10L2) and 60 g of IPA were ball-milled (3SP2) at 125 rpm
(revolution) for 12 h inside MC-500 mL nylon tank. Four slurries were
dried at 60 °C for 6 h and then sieved over 200 grit. Three grams (3 g)
and 2 g of fine powder was pressed into Φ 19 mm pellets at 5 tons (170
MPa) and Φ 13 mm pellets at 3 tons (220 MPa), respectively, which
were denoted as Φ 19_1−4 and Φ 13_1−4, respectively. Nb5Li15
gravels applied in this section were particles only sieved over P20 grit.
These pellets were sintered upon gravel inside a MgO crucible at 1340
°C for 2, 10, and 20 min in a box furnace (Model NBD-M1500−12IT).
The relative density and Li+ conductivity of these pellets was tested to
evaluate the consistency of powder preparation and ceramic sintering
procedures.
The detailed experiments of studying the influences of particle size,
heating rate, weight, and optimizing the preparation craft for
Ta5Li10L0−3 can be found in section SI_0.4−0.7 in the Supporting
Information.

3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
3.1. Primary Pellet-on-Gravel Sintering Architecture
and Poor Consistency of Obtained Ceramic Pellets.
Figure 1a shows the sintering architecture. The phase of
Nb5Li15 bed powder is cubic LLZO, as shown in Figure S2(a)
in the Supporting Information. The new MgO crucibles are
pretreated with 6 g of Nb5Li15 powder at 1280−1340 °C for
20−120 min to saturate the trace amount impurity materials
such as Al2O3 and SiO2 on the internal surfaces. Gravel_20
crashed from sintered Nb5Li15 block (Figure S2(b) in the
Supporting Information) contains much less Li2O/Li than
pristine Nb5Li15 powder, exhibiting no shrinkage after further
sintering with Ta5Li10L2 green pellets. This readily avoids
curvature of ceramic pellets after sintering. Only one green pellet
was placed in one MgO crucible, to avoid the undesired
adhesion of stacked pellets after sintering. In this primary
architecture, the bottom of the upper crucible was used as the lid
upon the lower one. Therefore, the preparation of LLZO
ceramics can be achieved by employing the multilayer stacks of
MgO crucibles inside the box furnace. Despite the fact that the
MgO crucible requires new substitute ones after several tens of
sintering, because of the inevitable rupture with the presence of
Li2O (Li+ penetration in MgO lattice: Li2O →2LiMg

However, the consistency of as-sintered Ta5Li10L2 pellets
was unsatisfactory (see Figure 1b, as well as Figure S3 in the

The O2 atmosphere effectively suppresses the “Li-loss” problem,
as reported in the published literature.51,52 The main escaping
composition from the semiopen MgO crucible with MgO lid is
suggested to be Li and Li2O gases (see Scheme 1), which is soon
exhausted after its reaction with the Al2O3 substrate and the
Al2O3 refractory brick/inner-wall inside the box furnace. On the
other hand, the Li2O/Li gas among the raw grains inside the
green pellet is the key sintering aid for the densification of
LLZO.12 Hence, the successful preparation of LLZO ceramic is
highly dependent on the competition between the densification
against Li-loss. If the densification is faster than Li loss, namely,
the green pellet reaches the final sintering stage before
substantial Li-loss occurs, a densified LLZO ceramic with high
conductivity can be obtained. Otherwise, the LLZO green pellet
only reaches the intermediate sintering stage.
3.2.1. Influence of Particle Size and Sintering Temperature.
Figure 2 systematically demonstrates the impact of particle size
(surface energy) and sintering temperature on the sintering
results of Ta5Li6 ceramic pellets. As shown in Figure S5 in the
Supporting Information, bimodal size distributions of Ta5Li6
powders milled for short durations (20 min and 1 h) are
obtained with peaks at 0.7 and 7 μm. With prolonged milling

3.2.4. Summary: Key Parameters in Practical LLZO
Sintering. Table 3 summaries the main factors influencing the
Li-loss and densification during LLZO sintering. Since a larger
MgO crucible volume would result in more-severe Li-loss,42 a
MgO crucible with internal dimensions of Φ20 × 23 mm (7.2
mL) is applied in this section. Although smaller particle sizes
shall provide increased surface area and energy for grain
densification, the Li-loss during sintering is hardly affected. Both
higher sintering temperature and longer sintering duration bring
accelerated Li-loss and favor the densification process. On the
other hand, fast heating rate would inhibit the Li-loss while the
densification speed of LLZO is mainly determined by
temperature. Besides, the presence of initial excess Li
compounds inside green pellet and external gravels can also
effectively delay the exhaust of Li2O, and thus promote the
densification.
The sintering result is determined by the competition
between Li-loss and densification, which is influenced by a
series of parameters. Herein, three key parameters for densifying
LLZO should be noted. The first one is the particle size
distribution, which is related to the surface energy. Fine powder
provides high activity during sintering, promoting the
densification over Li-loss effectively. The second one is the
volume of MgO crucible, specifically, the cross-sectional area
parallel to the bottom and the height. Apparently, less slit
between crucible and lid, together with longer stroke from the
bottom to the slit, shall contribute to delayed Li-loss. However,
Al2O3, SiO2 and other reactive impurities can be easily
diminished by sintering Nb5Li15 powder at high temperatures,
which eliminates the quality variation concerning MgO crucibles
provided by different suppliers. The third one is the initial total
amount of excess Li2O inside the sintering container. Li2O is
provided by all the stacked green pellets and the Gravel_20 at
the bottom, which contain Li2O, LiOH, Li2CO3, LiOH·H2O, or
other Li-rich compounds. Apart from the above three
parameters, the sintering condition concerning temperature
and heating rate is also important, which can be easily controlled
in practical experiments. Nevertheless, dense LLZO ceramics
can be repeatably prepared in laboratory scale by addressing the
five parameters discussed above.

4. CONCLUSION
Achieving densified LLZO ceramics with high repeatability
results from the precise control over almost all procedure details
from the raw material mixing to green pellet sintering. The
sintering results of LLZO ceramics are dependent on many
factors, including the crystallinity of raw materials, the
crystallinity of calcinated powder in cubic LLZO phase, the
moisture control during milling/drying, the particle size
distribution after tailoring, the dimensions of MgO crucible,
the initial excess Li2O inside the sintering furniture, the
composition of LLZO, and the sintering condition. To
summarize, the critical point lies in the competition between
Li loss and densification. The three most important factors are
listed as follows:
• Particle size distribution. Small particles greatly improve
the surface area and the surface energy, thus increasing the
densification speed over Li loss.
• MgO crucible. Crucible should not contain impurities such
as SiO2 and Al2O3. A small crucible with an internal
diameter just larger than the target green pellet can
effectively delay the Li loss from the sintering system,
which enables the densification before Li-exhaust.
• Initial total amount of excess Li inside the crucible. Enough
excess Li-rich compounds continuously provide Li2O/Li
gas against the Li escaping.
A high sintering temperature is desired for providing enough
thermal driving force toward boosted densification speed. The
ramping speed should be fast to decrease the total Li-loss before
reaching the sintering temperature.
Moreover, the production craft platform involving powder
treatment and ceramic sintering procedure is provided. The
optimum composition and sintering condition for Ta-doped
LLZO with La2Zr2O7 additive is explored based on this
platform. Densified (>96%) Ta-LLZO with high conductivity
(>9 × 10−4 S cm−1) and high repeatability can be produced.
These ceramics are tested and examined in Li−Li and Li−S
batteries.
