A B S T R A C T
Poly(ethylene oxide) (PEO)-based all-solid-state lithium‑sulfur batteries have attracted considerable attention 
recently. However, the inherently low Li+ ion transport efficiency of PEO-based electrolytes, combined with the 
inevitable complex polysulfide shuttle effects, significantly impedes sulfur redox kinetics and results in sub­
stantial loss of active materials. Herein, a composite solid electrolyte (CSE) is developed by integrating TiO2 and 
a high-entropy Prussian blue analog (HEPBA) as dual-functional fillers into a PVDF-HFP/PEO copolymer matrix. 
Intertwined polymer network improves mechanical robustness and reduces PEO crystallinity, facilitating Li+
migration. Moreover, the interfacial regions between the inorganic fillers and the polymer matrix establish 
additional Li+ conduction pathways. Importantly, both TiO2 and HEPBA effectively immobilize lithium poly­
sulfides (LiPSs) via strong chemisorption, thereby suppressing the shuttle effect. HEPBA further contributes 
through catalytic conversion of trapped LiPSs. Benefiting from this synergistic design, the PPHTH-4 CSE exhibits 
a high ionic conductivity of 8.9 × 10−4 S cm−1 at 60 ◦C. Assembled solid-state lithium‑sulfur battery deliver an 
initial discharge capacity of 918 mAh g−1 and delivers a superior cycle stability over 500 cycles at 0.2C (with a 
capacity decay of only 0.0671% per cycle) with an average Coulombic efficiency exceeding 98.6%. This work 
provides a feasible strategy for high-performance all-solid-state lithium‑sulfur batteries.
1. Introduction
The rapid advancement of portable electronics and electric vehicles 
has intensified the demand for high-performance energy storage sys­
tems. Traditional lithium-ion batteries are increasingly inadequate to 
meet these growing requirements, constrained by limitations such as 
high cost, safety concerns, and relatively low theoretical energy density 
[1,2]. Lithium‑sulfur batteries have attracted widespread attention due 
to their high theoretical specific capacity (1675 mAh g−1) and theoret­
ical energy density (~2600 Wh kg−1). The low cost and natural abun­
dance of sulfur further enhance their appeal [3,4]. However, the 
commercialization of lithium‑sulfur batteries faces significant obstacles, 
including the polysulfide shuttle effect, lithium dendrite growth leading 
to internal short circuits, and the flammability of conventional liquid 
electrolytes [5–7]. To address these issues, a variety of approaches have 
been employed, including advanced cathode architecture design, sepa­
rator modification, electrolyte additives, and the adoption of solid-state 
electrolytes [8,9]. The utilization of solid-state electrolytes offers a 
fundamental solution due to its non-flammable and superior mechanical 
strength, which can not only address the issue of safety hazards but also 
suppresses the growth of lithium dendrites [10,11]. Particularly, solid 
polymer electrolytes offer advantages like better processability, flexi­
bility to accommodate electrode volume changes and favorable inter­
facial contact [12].

Among various polymer matrices, polyethylene oxide (PEO) has 
been widely investigated for solid polymer electrolytes due to its high 
safety, good film-forming ability and compatibility with electrodes 
[13,14]. However, PEO-based all-solid-state lithium‑sulfur batteries 
suffer from critical drawbacks. The high crystallinity of PEO impedes the 
formation of continuous Li+ conduction pathways, leading to a low 
room-temperature Li+ conductivity of PEO (10−8 to 10−6 S cm−1) 
severely limits charge transfer, resulting in sluggish sulfur redox kinetics 
[15]. Furthermore, the ether oxygen groups in PEO exhibit solubility 
toward lithium polysulfides (LiPSs), failing to effectively prevent the 
detrimental shuttle effect. Moreover, this phenomenon becomes more 
pronounced when operating at medium-to-high temperatures [16,17]. 
Additionally, electrolytes based on PEO suffer from inadequate me­
chanical strength, which limits their ability to effectively suppress 
lithium dendrite growth [18]. To address these drawbacks, several 
modification strategies have been developed. One common approach 
involves incorporating functional fillers: Li+-inert substances can 
enhance ionic conductivity by promoting lithium salt dissociation and 
increasing the population of free Li+ ions, while Li+-conducting mate­
rials can reduce PEO crystallinity and provide additional transport 
pathways for Li+ migration [19,20]. Moreover, polymer modification 
techniques such as blending [21], crosslinking [22], and copolymeri­
zation [23,24] have been employed to improve the ionic conductivity 
and mechanical strength of PEO-based electrolytes by developing 
composite solid electrolytes (CSEs). In recent years, dual-functional 
inorganic composite fillers have become a mainstream modification 
route for PEO-based solid electrolytes applied in all-solid-state lith­
ium‑sulfur batteries. Conventional single ceramic fillers (TiO2 [25], 
Al2O3 [26], LLZO [27]) only slightly reduce PEO crystallinity and 
strengthen films, but lack polysulfide catalytic activity and strong 
anchoring capacity, so they cannot fundamentally resolve shuttle- 
induced capacity fading. Porous framework materials such as mono- 
metal Prussian blue analogs and MOFs possess moderate LiPS adsorp­
tion capacity, yet severe particle agglomeration occurs when high 
loading is applied, deteriorating membrane mechanical integrity and 
bulk ionic conductivity. To overcome these defects, researchers have 
proposed hybrid filler systems like MOF-ceramic and metal sulfide- 
ceramic composites. Nevertheless, existing dual-filler designs generally 
face a performance trade-off of improving catalysis often sacrifices 
mechanical/conductivity performance and vice versa. Zhang et al. [28]
incorporated MOF-5 filler into a PEO/LAGP/LGPS composite electrolyte 
via solution casting. The abundant Lewis acidic sites within MOF-5 
could coordinate with the PEO matrix, effectively reducing its crystal­
linity, which subsequently enhanced the oxidation stability and 
increased the lithium-ion transference number of the composite elec­
trolyte. Ao et al. [29] prepared a solid electrolyte by cross-linking 
PEGDMA with PEO through ultraviolet light curing. The incorporation 
of PEGDMA not only reduced the crystallinity of PEO but also provided 
ester groups capable of binding with LiPSs, thus alleviating the shuttle 
effect in lithium‑sulfur batteries. However, despite these enhancements, 
it remains challenging for a single solid electrolyte to simultaneously 
achieve high ionic conductivity, excellent mechanical properties, and 
effective suppression of the polysulfide shuttle effect, which is essential 
for meeting the demanding performance requirements of lithium‑sulfur 
batteries. Consequently, the design and fabrication of optimal fillers are 
crucial for achieving high-performance PEO-based all-solid-state lith­
ium‑sulfur batteries.
Herein, we designed and fabricated a novel CSE by blending PEO 
with poly(vinylidene fluoride-co-hexafluoropropylene) (PVDF-HFP) as a 
polymer matrix, reinforced with dual-functional inorganic fillers: tita­
nium dioxide (TiO2) and a high-entropy Prussian blue analog (HEPBA) 
(Scheme 1). The incorporation of semi-crystalline PVDF-HFP disrupts 
the ordered arrangement of PEO chains, suppressing crystallization and 
creating more amorphous regions for rapid ion transport. In addition, 
the polar C–F bonds in PVDF-HFP can also facilitate the dissociation of 
lithium salts, further enhancing ionic conductivity. The nano-TiO2 dis­
rupts ordered PEO chain stacking, reduces matrix crystallinity and cre­
ates abundant inorganic-polymer interfaces for rapid Li+ transport. It

also provides moderate chemical adsorption toward LiPSs via its surface 
polar sites. HEPBA features interconnected cubic porous frameworks 
loaded with unsaturated Co/Ni/Cu/Mn/Zn multi-metal active sites. 
Incorporated into the polymer matrix, HEPBA immobilizes free TFSI−
anions to raise the Li+ transference number and mitigate concentration 
polarization at the Li metal interface. In lithium‑sulfur batteries, its 
porous skeleton firmly restrains soluble LiPSs, while multi-metal sites 
catalyze sequential LiPSs redox to eliminate intermediate accumulation 
and boost sulfur utilization. Electrolytes with TiO2 alone suffer severe 
shuttle effects and sluggish sulfur redox kinetics; single HEPBA filler 
tends to aggregate, impairing mechanical properties and ionic conduc­
tivity. Thus, the total filler loading is fixed at 5 wt%, with TiO2 partially 
substituted by HEPBA rather than fully replaced to achieve multidi­
mensional synergy in ion conduction, mechanical performance and 
LiPSs suppression. This dual trapping-catalysis coupling system reduces 
redox polarization and alleviates capacity decay originating from the 
shuttle effect. Benefiting from the synergistic interactions among PEO, 
PVDF-HFP, TiO2 and HEPBA, the optimized CSE (denoted as PPHTH-4) 
achieves a high ionic conductivity of 8.9 × 10−4 S cm−1 at 60 ◦C and 
exhibits enhanced mechanical properties. Impressively, the 1900 h long- 
term galvanostatic cycling test of PPHTH-4 Li symmetric cells was 
conducted at a constant temperature of 60 ◦C, and the critical current 
density can reach up to 0.60 mA cm−2. Furthermore, the assembled all- 
solid-state lithium‑sulfur batteries enables an initial discharge capacity 
of 918 mAh g−1 and delivers a superior cycle stability over 500 cycles at 
0.2C with a capacity decay of only 0.0671% per cycle. Therefore, these 
findings could pave the way for developing safer and high-performance 
solid-state lithium‑sulfur batteries.
2. Experimental
2.1. Preparation of TiO2
A calculated amount of TiCl4 (99%, Adamas), deionized water, urea 
and ethanol were mixed and stirred for 15 min with the temperature 
maintained below 5 ◦C. Urea was first dissolved in ethanol, followed by 
mixing with the precursor solution. The resulting solution was stirred for 
15 min, then transferred into a Teflon-lined autoclave, sealed and heated 
in an oven at 200 ◦C for 2 h. The product was centrifuged, washed with 
deionized water and ethanol, then oven dried under vacuum overnight.
2.2. Synthesis of HEPBA
Solution A was prepared by dissolving 0.4 mmol each of Co 
(NO3)2⋅6H2O, Ni(NO3)2⋅6H2O, Cu(NO3)2⋅3H2O, Mn(NO3)2⋅3H2O, Zn 
(NO3)2⋅6H2O and 2.25 mmol of Na3C6H5O7⋅2H2O in 50 mL deionized 
water. Solution B was prepared by dissolving 2 mmol of K3[Fe(CN)6] in 
50 mL deionized water. At room temperature, Solution B was poured 
into Solution A under stirring. After continuously stirring for 10 min, the 
obtained mixed solution was aged at room temperature for 24 h. The 
resulting product was centrifuged, washed with deionized water and 
ethanol, and oven dried under vacuum overnight.
2.3. Preparation of PEO-based CSEs
The PEO/PVDF-HFP/LiTFSI CSE, denoted as PPH-30, was prepared 
by a solution casting method. Typically, 0.1 g of PVDF-HFP (MW =
450,000) was dissolved in N, N-Dimethylformamide (DMF) under stir­
ring at 60 ◦C for 1 h. Subsequently, 0.9 g of PEO (MW = 600,000) and 
0.3 g of LiTFSI (EO: Li+ = 20:1) were added to the solution and stirred at 
60 ◦C for 12 h to form a homogeneous slurry. The slurry was then cast 
onto a polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) mold and dried at 60 ◦C for 24 h. 
The designation PPH-30 indicates the mass ratio of LiTFSI to the poly­
mer blend (PEO/PVDF-HFP) is 30:100. To incorporate TiO2, a series of 
PEO/PVDF-HFP/TiO2/LiTFSI CSEs (denoted as PPHT-X) were synthe­
sized by adding X wt% (X = 2.5, 5, 7.5, 10) of TiO2 relative to the total 
mass of the polymers during the slurry preparation. A series of PPHT-L 
composite electrolytes was developed by co-doping HEPBA and TiO2 
(total 5 wt%) based on the optimal PPHT-5 formulation, where L stands 
for mass percentage of HEPBA among total inorganic fillers (L = 1, 2, 3, 
4, 5), and the residual (5-L) wt% filler is TiO2. A pure HEPBA (5 wt%) 
control was also prepared for synergistic comparison. Thickness mea­
surements from five independent membranes per group (Table S1) 
ensured reliable ionic conductivity calculations.
2.4. Preparation of sulfur cathodes
The sulfur cathode was prepared by mixing sulfur, conductive car­
bon, and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) binder in a mass ratio of 7:2:1. 
First, sulfur and conductive carbon were thoroughly ground in an agate 
mortar. Separately, PVDF was dissolved in an appropriate amount of N- 
methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) under stirring to form a homogeneous so­
lution. The ground powder was then added to the PVDF/NMP solution 
and stirred at room temperature for 4 h to form a uniform slurry. The 
resulting slurry was coated onto an aluminum foil current collector 
using a doctor blade, followed by drying in a vacuum oven at 60 ◦C for 
12 h. Finally, the dried electrode was cut into 12 mm diameter discs, 
with a sulfur loading of 0.8 mg cm−2, 1.5 mg cm−2 and 2 mg cm−2, 
respectively.

2.6. Characterization and DFT theoretical calculations
The phase compositions of the CSEs were tested by X-ray diffraction 
(XRD, Rigaku MiniFlex600) with a scanning range of 10◦to 80◦. The 
morphology of the CSEs was characterized by scanning electron mi­
croscopy (SEM, TESCAN MIRA3) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectros­
copy (EDS). Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectra were 
performed on a Nicolet iS50. The thermal stability of the CSEs was 
measured by thermogravimetric analysis (TG) and differential scanning 
calorimetry (DSC) (SDT 650, TA) in a nitrogen atmosphere. The chem­
ical composition of the lithium surface after cycling was detected by X- 
ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Thermo Fisher Escalab 250 Xi).
The inhibitory effect of the membrane on the shuttle effect was 
studied by density functional theory (DFT) calculations. The generalized 
gradient approximation with the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) ex­
change correlation functional was used. For the plane wave calculations, 
a cut-off energy of 500 eV was applied, total energies were converged to 
within 10−5 eV, while structural optimization minimized the forces on 
all atoms to below 0.03 eV Å−1. In this calculation, a 2 × 2 × 1 k-point 
mesh was used to achieve the actual spacing less than 0.05 Å−1. To 
explore the interactions, the adsorption energies of Li2Sn were calculated

adsorption energy is defined as the change in the potential energy E of

3. Result and discussion
Inorganic filler high-entropy Prussian blue analog (HEPBA) was 
synthesized via a coprecipitation method, and titanium dioxide (TiO2) 
was obtained through a hydrothermal process. The prepared samples 
were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and X-ray 
diffraction (XRD). HEPBA displays a regular nano cubic morphology 
(Fig. S1a), which aligns with previous reports [31], while TiO2 appears 
as nanoparticles (Fig. S1b). XRD analysis further confirms the successful 
synthesis of both materials. The diffraction pattern of HEPBA (Fig. S2a) 
exhibits peaks consistent with the reported research, and the XRD pat­
terns in Fig. S2b confirm the synthesized TiO2 belongs to pure anatase 
phase (PDF #21–1272). The PEO/PVDF-HFP/TiO2/ HEPBA /LiTFSI 
(PPHTH-4) composite solid electrolyte (CSE) was fabricated using a 
facile solution-casting method, as illustrated in Fig. S3.
Fig. 1a-d shows the macroscopic appearance of the prepared elec­
trolyte membranes. The pure PEO membrane is transparent and uniform 
(Fig. 1a). Upon blending with PVDF-HFP (PPH-30), the membrane be­
comes translucent (Fig. 1b). The incorporation of TiO2 renders the 
PPHT-5 CSE white and opaque (Fig. 1c). Finally, the incorporation of 
HEPBA results in a brown, opaque appearance for the PPHTH-4 mem­
brane (Fig. 1d). As shown in the insets in Fig. 1, all CSEs exhibit excellent 
flexibility and can be bent without cracking, which is beneficial for 
maintaining stable interfacial contact with electrodes during battery 
cycling. Fig. 1e-h shows surface SEM images of the PEO, PPH-30, PPHT- 
5 and PPHTH-4 CSEs, respectively. The PEO CSE exhibits a relatively 
smooth surface. The introduction of PVDF-HFP leads to blocky distri­
bution. This blocky distribution becomes more pronounced with the 
addition of TiO2, accompanied by the appearance of white streaks be­
tween the blocks. After adding HEPBA, PPHTH-4 CSE exhibits more and 
smaller blocky distributions as well as more and denser white streaks. 
This morphological evolution is likely attributed to the incorporation of

The incorporation of PVDF-HFP, TiO2, and HEPBA into PEO signif­
icantly alters the phase composition and crystallinity of the resulting 
CSEs, as determined by XRD, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy 
(FTIR) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) analyses (Fig. 2) 
[30]. XRD patterns (Fig. 2a) reveal that pure PEO exhibits two charac­
teristic peaks at 19◦and 23◦, which are associated with the (120) and 
(112) crystal planes, respectively. Upon the addition of PVDF-HFP (PPH- 
30) and further fillers (PPHT-5, PPHTH-4), the intensity of these PEO 
diffraction peaks markedly decreases. This reduction indicates a sub­
stantial decline in PEO crystallinity, which is conducive to enhanced 
ionic conductivity, as the fillers disrupt the orderly arrangement of PEO 
polymer chains [32,33]. To further elucidate the impact of filler addition 
on the crystallinity of PEO, the crystal structure of the CSEs with varying 
filler contents was analyzed. As provided in Fig. S4, the addition of 
varying fillers significantly weakens the characteristic diffraction peaks 
of PEO, indicating a reduction in crystallinity. This transformation of 
crystalline regions into amorphous domains facilitates Li+ migration, 
thereby contributing to the enhanced ionic conductivity.
To further elucidate the structural interactions between fillers and 
the polymer matrix, FTIR spectroscopy was conducted. Fig. 2b presents 
the FTIR spectra of PEO, PPH-30, PPHT-5, and PPHTH-4 CSEs. The three 
characteristic peaks at 1060 cm−1, 1110 cm−1, and 1150 cm−1 corre­
spond to the stretching vibrations of the C-O-C ether bond in PEO [34]. 
Compared with pure PEO, the triplet peak profile of the C-O-C stretching 
vibration in PPH-30, PPHT-5, and PPHTH-4 broadens due to the intro­
duction of PVDF-HFP, indicating that PVDF-HFP influences the 
morphology of the PEO framework [35–38]. The peak at 880 cm−1, 
absent in pure PEO, appears in PPH-30, PPHT-5, and PPHTH-4, which is 
attributed to the amorphous phase of PVDF-HFP [39–42]. The peak at 
1195 cm−1 corresponds to the stretching vibration of CF3 groups in 
LiTFSI [43]. Additionally, peaks at 1360 cm−1 and 1340 cm−1 are 
assigned to CH2 wagging vibrations, while those at 1280 cm−1 and 1240 
cm−1 correspond to CH2 twisting vibrations, and peaks at 960 cm−1 and 
845 cm−1 are attributed to CH2 rocking vibrations [44,45]. Notably, the 
dichotomous peaks at 1340 cm−1 and 1360 cm−1 (recognized as key 
indicators of crystallinity) merge into a single peak at 1350 cm−1 in PPH- 
30, PPHT-5, and PPHTH-4. This merged peak is characteristic of 
amorphous PEO. The merging effect is more pronounced in PPHT-5 and 
PPHTH-4, suggesting that the introduction of TiO2 and HEPBA further 
reduces the crystallinity of PEO. These changes, together with the 
broadening of the C-O-C triplet peaks, indicate that the incorporation of 
LiTFSI, PVDF-HFP, TiO2, and HEPBA promotes a transition of PEO from 
a crystalline to an amorphous state [34,46,47]. DSC measurements 
(Fig. 2c) quantitatively confirm this effect. The melting transition tem­
perature (Tm) of pure PEO electrolytes is 65.99 ◦C. With the addition of 
fillers, the Tm values for PPH-30, PPHT-5, and PPHTH-4 CSEs decrease 
to 58.36 ◦C, 56.54 ◦C, and 55.08 ◦C, respectively. A lower Tm indicates 
reduced crystallinity of PEO, attributed to the disruption of PEO chain 
ordering by PVDF-HFP, TiO2, and HEPBA. This disruption promotes 
segmental motion of the PEO chains, which is beneficial for Li+ transport 
within the electrolyte [48]. Moreover, the thermal stability of the CSEs 
was evaluated by thermogravimetric (TG) analysis (Fig. 2d). The 
PPHTH-4 CSE demonstrates good thermal stability up to 350 ◦C. Initial 
weight loss (room temperature to 200 ◦C) is attributed to water vola­
tilization and residual solvent evaporation. A rapid weight loss between 
350 and 440 ◦C results from the thermal decomposition of the polymer 
matrix and LiTFSI [49]. Mechanical properties are another critical 
aspect for CSE performance. Tensile tests (Fig. 2e) show that the PPHTH- 
4 CSE membrane possesses a favorable combination of ductility (5170% 
elongation) and tensile strength (2.68 MPa), outperforming pure PEO 
and PPH-30 CSEs. Although its strength is slightly lower than that of 
PPHT-5 CSE (3.12 MPa, 5290% elongation), the mechanical properties 
of PPHTH-4 are considered adequate to alleviate the volumetric

To verify the improved electrochemical performance of PPHTH-4 
CSEs, these samples were used in the lithium‑sulfur cells and tested at 
60 ◦C. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements were conducted at a scan 
rate of 0.1 mV s−1 to investigate the redox behavior of the solid elec­
trolytes. As shown in Fig. 4a, all CSEs display one oxidation peak and 
two reduction peaks. The PPHTH-4-based cell exhibits the lowest 
oxidation peak potential (2.54 V) and the highest reduction peak po­
tential (1.90 V), resulting in the smallest redox potential gap (0.64 V), 
significantly lower than those of PEO (0.78 V), PPH-30 (0.74 V), and 
PPHT-5 (0.74 V). This reduced polarization indicates enhanced kinetics 
of the sulfur redox reactions and improved conversion efficiency of LiPSs 
during cycling [57–59]. First-cycle galvanostatic charge-discharge pro­
files further confirmed these advantages as shown in Fig. 4b. The 
PPHTH-4 cell exhibited a lower charge plateau and a higher discharge 
plateau compared to the other CSEs, along with the smallest polarization 
voltage (225 mV) and the highest initial discharge capacity (901 mAh 
g−1). These features suggest effective suppression of the polysulfide 
shuttle effect and improved active material utilization, attributable to 
the incorporation of HEPBA filler, which enhances redox kinetics and 
promotes LiPSs conversion.
Rate capability tests were further subjected to evaluate the electro­
chemical performance of the composite electrolyte membranes 
composed of various components in lithium‑sulfur batteries. The 
PPHTH-4 cell exhibited superior performance, delivering reversible 
capacities of 1037, 907, 795, 655, and 411 mAh g−1 at current rates of 
0.1C to 2C, respectively (Fig. 4c). When the current rate was returned to 
0.1C, a high reversible capacity of 917 mAh g−1 was restored, indicating 
excellent rate recoverability. In contrast, cells with PEO, PPH-30, and 
PPHT-5 exhibited fading or disappearing charge-discharge plateaus at 
elevated rates (Fig. S15), whereas PPHTH-4 maintained distinct plateaus 
even at 2C (Fig. 4d). The Li//PPHTH-4//S cell exhibited exceptional 
long-term cycling stability, delivering discharge capacities of 901, 865, 
799, and 732 mAh g−1 at the 1 st, 10 th, 50 th, and 100 th cycles, 
respectively, while maintaining a well-defined and stable discharge 
plateau even after 100 cycles (Fig. 4e). In contrast, cells employing other 
CSEs suffered pronounced capacity fading and progressive degradation 
of the discharge plateau (Fig. S16). EIS was performed on the four types 
of cells before and after 20 cycles to evaluate interfacial evolution. The 
charge transfer resistances of Li//PEO//S, Li//PPH-30//S, Li//PPHT- 
5//S, and Li//PPHTH-4//S cells before cycling were 208, 134, 98, and 
90 Ω, respectively (Fig. S17). After cycling, these values decreased to 
173, 107, 81, and 77 Ω, respectively (Fig. 4f). The PPHTH-4 cell 
exhibited the lowest interfacial resistance both before and after cycling. 
The general reduction in impedance is attributed to the formation of a 
stable SEI/CEI layer at the electrode-electrolyte interface, which im­
proves interfacial ion transport [29]. Long-term cycling performance 
further confirmed the superiority of the PPHTH-4 CSE. As shown in 
Fig. 4g, the Li//PPHTH-4//S cell achieves an initial discharge capacity 
of 918 mAh g−1, outperforming the Li//PEO//S (687 mAh g−1), Li// 
PPH-30//S (805 mAh g−1), and Li//PPHT-5//S (851 mAh g−1) cells. 
After 500 cycles at 0.2C, the capacity decay of PPHTH-4 cell is only 
0.0671% per cycle, lower than PEO (0.19%) and PPH-30 (0.14%), and 
the Li//PEO//S cell failed after only 170 cycles. These results collec­
tively confirm that the PPHTH-4 CSE significantly enhances electro­
chemical performance through improved reaction kinetics, effective 
LiPSs trapping, and stable interfacial properties. To evaluate the prac­
tical viability, cells with elevated sulfur loadings (1.5 and 2.0 mg cm−2) 
were assembled and tested at 0.2C and 60 ◦C (Fig. S18). The cell with a 
1.5 mg cm−2 loading maintained a capacity of >412 mAh g−1 after 100 
cycles (CE > 98%), retaining ~58% of its initial capacity (~710 mAh 
g−1). Even at a high loading of 2.0 mg cm−2, the cell delivered an initial 
capacity of ~670 mAh g−1 and retained >362 mAh g−1 without sig­
nificant degradation. These results confirm that the synergistic effect of 
TiO2 and HEPBA enables effective polysulfide confinement and stable 
interfacial contact even under severe volume fluctuation and heavy LiPS 
accumulation, revealing the broad applicability of the composite 
electrolyte beyond low sulfur loading. Furthermore, the superiority of 
the optimized PPHTH-4 formulation is further corroborated in Table S3, 
which illustrates that PPHTH-4 delivers the most balanced and superior 
overall performance among all compositions.
To investigate the inhibitory effect of the CSEs on the polysulfide 
shuttle effect, XPS analysis was performed on the lithium anodes of 
lithium‑sulfur batteries after cycling [20]. As shown in Fig. 5a and b, the 
S 2p XPS spectra of the Li anodes in Li//PEO//S and Li//PPHTH-4//S 
cells after five cycles can be deconvoluted into two main regions: 
peaks at higher binding energies are attributed to LixSOy and TFSI−
species, while those at lower binding energies correspond to LiPSs 
[21,30]. Specifically, peaks at 164.1 eV and 162.4 eV are correspond to 
Li2Sx, while peaks at 165.9 eV and 167.1 eV ascribed to LixSOy. Peaks 
observed at 168.2 eV and 169.6 eV originate from TFSI−anions [60,61]. 
Notably, the intensity of LiPSs-related signals on the Li anode surface in 
the PEO-based cell is significantly higher than that of the PPHTH-4 CSE- 
based cell, indicating more severe polysulfide shuttling in PEO-based 
cell. To further investigate the reaction kinetics, galvanostatic inter­
mittent titration technique (GITT) measurements were performed on the 
Li//PPHTH-4//S cell (Fig. 5c and Fig. S19). GITT measurement was 
conducted to quantitatively evaluate the dynamic redox kinetics of 
lithium polysulfides for all four electrolyte-based lithium‑sulfur batte­
ries. The quasi-equilibrium charge and discharge potentials extracted 
from GITT profiles are fixed at 2.24 V and 2.16 V for every sample, since 
these two plateaus correspond to the inherent thermodynamic equilib­
rium potentials of sulfur species reduction/oxidation and will not 
change with different electrolyte fillers. In contrast, the practical dy­
namic polarization overpotential (ΔVp), defined as the voltage gap be­
tween quasi-equilibrium plateau and transient working voltage during 
galvanostatic pulse, varies remarkably across samples. The PPHTH-4 
cell possesses the smallest (ΔVp) of 170 mV, which is lower than PEO 
(300 mV), PPH-30 (200 mV) and PPHT-5 (180 mV). This striking 
reduction of polarization overpotential verifies that the HEPBA filler 
introduces abundant surface catalytic active sites to facilitate fast con­
version of soluble lithium polysulfide intermediates. Moreover, internal 
resistance during charge/discharge was calculated for all cells (Fig. 5d). 
The Li//PPHTH-4//S cell demonstrated the lowest resistance, particu­
larly during Li2S activation and nucleation, indicating a reduced energy 
barrier for polysulfide conversion and accelerated redox kinetics. 
Variable-scan-rate CV measurements were performed to prove active 
catalysis of HEPBA (Fig. S20). For the PPHTH-4 cell, the b-values for 
Peak C1 (2.4 V) and Peak C2 (1.9 V) were determined to be 0.639 and 
0.618, respectively. Situated between the theoretical limits of diffusion 
control (0.5) and surface control (1.0), these values indicate that HEPBA 
shifts the LiPS redox kinetics toward a mixed diffusion-catalysis regime 
[62,63]. For the PPHT-5 electrolyte filled with TiO2, the b-values drop to 
0.517 (Peak C1) and 0.501 (Peak C2), close to 0.5, which corresponds to 
pure diffusion-controlled kinetics without obvious surface catalytic ef­
fects. The CV results dynamically prove that the TiO2 primarily opti­
mizes ionic conductivity and mechanical properties, offering only 
moderate LiPS anchoring, while multi-metal centers in HEPBA enable 
simultaneous immobilization and electrocatalytic conversion. To pro­
vide a more intuitive proof of the inhibitory effect of the CSEs on the 
shuttle effect, LiPSs permeation tests were implemented using H-type 
electrolytic cells. The left chamber was filled with 12 mL of 0.03 M Li2S6 
solution, and the right chamber contained an equal volume of DOL/DME 
(v/v = 1:1). As shown in Fig. 5e, the right-chamber solution turned light 
yellow within 1 h and dark yellow after 12 h when PEO or PPH-30 CSE 
was used as the separator. The cell using PPHT-5 CSE as the separator 
showed only slight yellow discoloration after 3 h and turned light yellow 
after 12 h. In contrast, the solution separated by PPHTH-4 CSE remained 
virtually colorless even after 12 h, demonstrating effective suppression 
of LiPSs shuttle effect. These visual observations were verified by 
UV–Vis spectroscopy, which showed significantly lower absorption in­
tensity in the 260–340 nm range for the PPHTH-4 group (Fig. 5f), con­
firming minimal polysulfide diffusion.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were employed to 
further elucidate the adsorption behavior of LiPSs on different filler 
materials. The adsorption configurations of Li2S2, Li2S4, Li2S6, and Li2S8 
on PEO, PVDF-HFP, TiO2, and HEPBA are illustrated in Fig. 5g and 
Fig. S21. The corresponding adsorption energies are compared in 
Fig. 5h. HEPBA and TiO2 exhibited strong adsorption toward LiPSs, 
showing high adsorption energies for Li2S2, Li2S4, Li2S6, and Li2S8, with 
values of −3.86 eV and −
3.67 eV for Li2S6, respectively. Higher 
adsorption energy means that Li2S6 molecules are more likely to be fixed 
on the cathode side, thereby inhibiting the polysulfide shuttling [22]. In 
contrast, PEO and PVDF-HFP show lower adsorption energies for Li2S2, 
Li2S4, Li2S6, and Li2S8, making it difficult to adsorb lithium polysulfides, 
allowing polysulfide migration and exhibiting a significant shuttle ef­
fect. These results confirm that the TiO2 and HEPBA fillers in PPHTH-4 
CSE effectively anchor soluble polysulfides, thereby inhibiting their 
shuttling and enhancing battery performance.
