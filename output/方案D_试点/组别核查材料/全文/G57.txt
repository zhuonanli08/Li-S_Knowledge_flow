ABSTRACT: All-solid-state batteries are considered as a reasonable further
development of conventional lithium-ion batteries. While the same active
materials may be used, solid electrolytes may offer higher safety than liquid
electrolytes and enable the reversible operation of the lithium metal anode. Also,
solid-state lithium/sulfur (Li/S) batteries are being investigated due to their high
theoretical specific energy. Li2S-based composite positive electrodes have been
demonstrated to achieve their theoretical capacity, high rate performance, and
good cycling stability under high stacking pressures. However, during charging,
Li2S is converted to sulfur and shrinks by 45%, and the resulting loss of contact
between the composite particles leads to mechanical degradation during cycling.
To better understand the correlation between the charge−discharge capacities and stack pressure during cycling in high-capacity
Li2S-based active materials, the dynamic volume change of the positive electrode layer is measured. The volume change of the Li2S-
based composite positive electrode is observed by using in situ scanning electron microscopy. Furthermore, by using Li4Ti5O12,
which is well-known to undergo almost no volume change during cycling, as the counter electrode, charge−discharge capacities were
found to depend on the initial volume change of the composite positive electrode. Finally, candidates for the negative electrode in
full-cell applications of all-solid-state Li/S batteries are discussed. This study represents a major step toward mitigating mechanical
deterioration in all-solid-state Li/S batteries stemming from the volumetric expansion and contraction of sulfur-active materials.
■INTRODUCTION
All-solid-state lithium-ion batteries have been extensively
developed as alternatives to conventional lithium-ion batteries
owing to the high safety of nonflammable solid electrolytes
(SE). Although oxide-type active materials have been the main
focus as positive electrode active materials for a long time, they
still suffer from problems such as limited energy density,
specific capacity,1 and poor interfacial stability with many
SEs.2−4 Sulfur-based active materials such as elemental sulfur
(S) and lithium sulfide (Li2S) are attractive alternatives to
these conventional positive electrode materials due to their
high theoretical capacities5−8 and environmental abundance
(with sulfur being a cheap byproduct from the oil industry). In
particular, cells using Li2S as active material in the positive
electrode can be assembled in the discharged state, allowing
the use of lithium reservoir-free negative electrodes such as
silicon9,10 and carbon11 or simply lithium-free current
collectors.12 However, due to the insulating nature of Li2S
and sulfur, it is necessary to prepare composite electrodes with
conductive additives to enable a sufficiently high number of
sites for charge transfer.13
Previous research efforts have resulted in Li2S-based
composite positive electrodes that are capable of reaching
their theoretical capacity at rates up to 2C and good cycling
stability under laboratory conditions.14−20 Despite those
advances, the large volume changes that occur during the
cycling of sulfur-based active materials remain a major
challenge in the development of fully functional all-solid-state
Li/S batteries (ASSLSB). The volume of Li2S during charging
to S decreases by 45%, leading to mechanical degradation
caused by loss of contact between the composite particles.21−24
Therefore, in laboratory-scale experiments, small particles are
used in the composites, and high stack pressures are applied to
mitigate mechanical degradation. However, in actual applica-
tions, a battery cell should operate with as little external
pressure as possible. Hence, it is vital to understand this
volume change of the sulfur-active material and its influence on
the integrity and microstructure of the composite. Unfortu-
nately, it is inherently challenging to directly evaluate the
volume change of the active material during cycling due to the

Here, we investigated the dynamic volume change of the
positive electrode layer and the correlation between the
charge−discharge capacities and stack pressure during cycling
in high-capacity Li2S-based active materials. Li2S−LiI compo-

to the theoretical capacity of Li2S.20,32 Here, we observed the
volume change of the Li2S−LiI composite positive electrode
using in situ SEM. Furthermore, because Li4Ti5O12 (LTO) is
known to undergo almost no volume change during the
charge−discharge process,33 we used it herein to demonstrate
that the charge−discharge capacities are affected by the initial
volume change of the composite positive electrode. Finally, we
discuss candidates for the negative electrode in full-cell
applications of ASSLSBs.
■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Synthesis of Electrode Materials. All procedures were
conducted in a dry Ar atmosphere (p(H2O)/p < 0.5 ppm; p(O2)/p
< 2 ppm). The composite positive electrodes comprised Li2S, LiI,
conductive carbon, and sulfide solid electrolytes (SSEs). First, Li2S
(99.9%, Mitsuwa Chemicals Co., Ltd., 80 mol %) and LiI (99.999%,
Sigma-Aldrich, 20 mol %) were mixed using a planetary ball mill
(Pulverisette 7; Fritsch GmbH). The materials (total weight: 0.3 g)
were placed in a ZrO2 pot (45 mL) with 28.8 g of ZrO2 balls (4 mm
diameter). Ball-milling was conducted at a rotational speed of 510
rpm for 10 h. The Li2S:LiI molar ratio of 80:20 was chosen because in
previous studies, positive electrodes of this composition showed
excellent electrochemical performance at 25 °C and 2 C rate for 2000
cycles.20,32 Our previous research also clarified the related charge−
discharge mechanism.34 The milled sample was mixed with vapor-
grown carbon fibers (VGCF, Showa Denko) by first using an agate
mortar and then ball-milling. The VGCF was pretreated by drying in a
vacuum at 160 °C for 24 h. The 80Li2S·20LiI-VGCF composite was
finally mixed with SSEs using a ball mill (Pulverisette 23; Fritsch
GmbH). This mixing method is as mild as mixing in an agate mortar
and pestle. The weight ratio of Li2S:LiI:VGCF:SSEs in the final
composite was 29:21:10:40.
Argyrodite Li6PS5Cl (LPSC, NEI Co. USA, ca. 5 μm) and Li3PS4
(LPS) glass were used as SEs. Two types of LPS with different particle
diameters (ca. 5 and 20 μm) were denoted as small-LPS and large-
LPS, respectively. Large LPS was prepared as described previously.35
Specifically, the starting materials Li2S and P2S5 (99%, Sigma-Aldrich)
in a stoichiometric ratio (Li2S:P2S5 = 75:25, mol %) were weighed
and put into a ZrO2 pot (250 mL) containing 450 g of ZrO2 balls (4
mm diameter). Ball-milling was performed at 210 rpm for 70 h using a
planetary ball mill (Pulverisette 5; Fritsch GmbH). Small LPS was
obtained by further wet milling of large LPS with dehydrated heptane
and dibutyl ether under the same ball-milling conditions as before.
The negative electrode was either an In/InLi eutectic alloy or a
Li4Ti5O12 (LTO) composite. The In/InLi negative electrode was
prepared from a 250 μm thick Li foil and a 100 or 300 μm thick In
foil. The amount of Li was controlled to form an In/InLi eutectic two-
phase material36 even after plating Li from the positive electrode on

Cell Assembly. All solid-state cells of the type In/InLi | SE | Li2S−
LiI composite were assembled as described previously.15,37 First, SE
powder (80 mg) was placed in a polyether ether ketone (PEEK) or
polycarbonate tube as a separator layer and gently compressed. Then,
the composite positive electrode (approximately 5 mg) was placed on
the separator layer and uniaxially pressed between two stainless-steel
rods at 360 MPa for 5 min. The Li and In foils were placed on the
separator layer (In first) opposite the composite positive electrode
and pressed together again at 20 MPa. For cells with an LTO negative
electrode (LTO composite | SE | Li2S−LiI composite), the SE (80
mg) was placed in a PEEK tube and pressed manually. The Li2S−LiI
composite (3−5 mg) and LTO composite (ca. 60 mg) were placed on
opposite sides of the SE pellet before applying a uniaxial pressure of
360 MPa for 3 min. Stainless steel or Cu foil was used as the current
collector. During the electrochemical measurements, defined uniaxial
stack pressures were applied and monitored in the setups as described
previously.27,28,33,38 All the procedures were conducted in a dry Ar
atmosphere.
Electrochemical Tests. Charge−discharge tests were performed
on the assembled cells using an electrochemical workstation (VMP3
and VMP300, Bio-Logic Co.). The cells of the type In/InLi | SE |
Li2S−LiI composite were cycled at a C/10 rate (ca. 130 μA cm−2) at
25 or 30 °C in the voltage range of 0−3 V vs. In/InLi. The cells of the
type LTO composite | SE | Li2S−LiI composite were cycled at a C/10
rate (ca. 130 μA cm−2) at 25 °C in the voltage range of 0−1.8 V vs.
LTO. All procedures were conducted in a dry Ar atmosphere.
SEM Observations. SEM observations of the powdered samples
were conducted by using a field-emission scanning electron
microscope (FE-SEM, JSM-6610A, JEOL Ltd.). Cross-sectional
SEM observations were conducted in situ and on cycled cells ex
situ. The ex situ cells were examined using FE-SEM (SU8220, Hitachi
High-Technologies Corp.). Before observation, the fractured surface
of the pellets was polished by using an Ar+ ion milling system (E-
3500, Hitachi High-Technologies Corp.). The in situ SEM was
performed on the same structural all-solid-state cell by applying a
constant pressure of 50 MPa. A rectangular region was defined in the
SEM secondary electron image to evaluate volume change in the
positive electrode during cell cycling. The pore volume was
segmented by marking this area using GIMP software (version
2.10.34). The area of the composite was then calculated as the total
rectangular area minus the area occupied by pores within this
rectangle.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
First, we investigated the size of SE particles within the
composite positive electrode and their effect on the charge−
discharge capacities of the all-solid-state cell. Smaller particles
in the composite are expected to increase the contact area
between the active material and the ion-conducting SE, as well
as better accommodate volume changes in the active material.
Figure 1a,b show secondary electron images of the prepared
LPS samples. The diameters of large-LPS particles were in the
range of ca. 10 μm, whereas those of small-LPS were under 5
μm. Figure 1c,d show cross-sectional SEM images of the
pressed pellets of the Li2S−LiI−VGCF electrode with large-
and small-LPS, respectively. In both cases, the pellets were
dense, and almost no pore space was formed in the composite.
In Figure 1c, the darker areas were Li2S−LiI−VGCF (marked
with “AM”), and the brighter areas were large-LPS (marked
with “SE”). The size of large-LPS is consistent with that shown
in Figure 1a. In contrast, in Figure 1d, the darker area is small-
LPS, and the bright area is Li2S−LiI−VGCF, which was
confirmed by EDS mappings (Figure S1); thus, the contrast
between the active material and SE is the opposite of that in
Figure 1c. When using small-LPS, surface irregularities may be
caused by an Ar+ ion milling treatment. In summary, particles
of small-LPS were better dispersed in the composite positive
electrode than those of large-LPS, thereby allowing more
efficient transport of lithium ions during cycling. Figure 1e,f
show the charge−discharge curves of cells prepared with large-
and small-LPS, respectively. Uniaxial stack pressure was
applied using an external frame, and the screw was tightened
as much as possible, resulting in a cell stack pressure of over 50
MPa. The initial charge capacity of the cell with a small LPS
(1080 mAh g−1) was larger than that with a large LPS (804
mAh g−1). The cell with small LPS reached a capacity similar

Next, the volume change of the Li2S positive electrode was
examined under a controlled stack pressure using in situ SEM.
A constant uniaxial pressure of 50 MPa was applied during
cycling, and the cross-section of the pellet was imaged. The in
situ SEM images are shown in Figure 2 and are summarized as
videos (see Supporting Information, Videos S1, S2, S3, and
S4). As expected, the pellet shrank during charging and
expanded during discharging, which is a theoretically
conceivable dynamic change due to the electrochemical
delithiation of Li2S upon charging and subsequent lithiation
during discharging. Despite the significant volume change of
the active material, the positive electrode remained almost
dense under the applied measurement conditions. As shown in
Figure 2, although a large crack was observed at the interface
between the positive electrode layer and the separator layer
after the first and second charging, the crack was repaired
following the subsequent discharging. This is because the large
volume change of the active material results in the particle
interface disappearing and reappearing repeatedly. Then, the
area changes were calculated using image analysis. For this
purpose, the size change of an area with approximately the
same material content and the pores inside this area were
manually segmented and evaluated.
Figure 3 summarizes the calculated area fractions of
representative areas before and after charge-discharging.
According to Figure 3, the area of the composite decreased
by ca. 20% upon charging and increased again by ca. 20% upon
discharging. Interestingly, this corresponds to the total
expected volume change of the composites (assuming that
only the volume of active material changes while the volume of
SE and carbon remains unchanged, see Figure S2b). However,
the magnitude of the observed change in the area is not
necessarily identical to the change in volume, as this
relationship is determined by the geometry of the sample
and of the cross-section (Figure S2a). As expected, the fraction
of pore space also increased as the active material shrank upon
charging.
Next, the influence of the initially applied stack pressure on
the performance of the Li2S−LiI electrode was investigated.
Usually, high stack pressures on the order of 50 MPa are
applied to sulfur-based active materials. For simplicity, the In/
InLi electrode is often used as a counter electrode in all-solid-
state batteries36 because of its low reactivity toward sulfide-
based SEs. In fact, the In/InLi negative electrode shows a
larger molar volume change than the Li2S-based positive
electrode in the case of the In/InLi|Li2S−LiI full cell.27,33,41
Therefore, the stack pressure inside the cell is dominated by
the volume change of the negative electrode. To explore the
correlation between the charge−discharge capacities and stack
pressure dominated by the volume change of the Li2S positive
electrode, a negative electrode with a minimal volume change
needs to be used, such as LTO.27,33,42 Thus, the charge−
discharge capacities of the LTO|Li2S−LiI cells were evaluated
at stack pressures of 5, 10, 25, and 50 MPa. Note that the stack
pressure was regulated before cycling and remained uncon-
trolled thereafter. Although this differs markedly from the
above in situ SEM experiments, in which the stack pressure
was kept constant, the pressure in the regulated cells only
fluctuated slightly (within a few percent) over a longer period.

at different stack pressures. The overvoltage increased when
the stack pressure was decreased, indicating poor particle
contact at lower pressures. Figure 4b shows the respective
charge−discharge capacities at different stack pressures.

of the applied pressure within the investigated range. We

Figure 4c shows the cycle performance of each cell, and
Figure 4d,e shows the conversion and Coulomb efficiencies,
respectively. The conversion efficiency (ΦC) was calculated by
dividing the charge capacity by the discharge capacity of the
previous cycle,23 whereas the Coulomb efficiency (ΦQ) was
defined according to the IUPAC guidelines and calculated by
dividing the discharge capacity by the charge capacity in the
same cycle.43 Differentiating between these two efficiencies
allows us to discern the origins of the capacity loss. This
calculation was used in a previous study on sulfur-based
positive electrodes for ASSLSBs.23 All of the cells studied here

almost reached 100%, ΦQ was significantly lower than 100%,
meaning that the capacity fading was occurring during the
discharge process. This suggests that only the particle-to-
particle contact area restored during discharging was active in
the subsequent charging process. The capacity loss was thus
caused by a reduced contact area during subsequent
discharging. In this experiment, additional capacity was
presumably lost due to the irreversible capacity in the LTO
counter electrode. However, this irreversible capacity occurs
similarly at all pressures (see Figure S3). Consequently, the
lower reversibility of cell cycling at low pressure can be
assigned to the loss of contact upon charging. Therefore, a
sufficiently high stack pressure at the end of charging can
improve the cell performance, although this is difficult to
achieve in practical batteries.
To further prove the effect of low pressure on the particle-
to-particle contact, two cells were charged at fixed pressures of
10 and 50 MPa. The positive electrode layer was then observed
by SEM Figure 5a,b shows cross-sectional SEM images of the
positive electrodes after the first charge at stack pressures of 10
and 50 MPa. As expected, more voids formed in the electrode
that was charged at a lower pressure compared to when higher
pressures were applied. These voids indicate a loss of contact
between particles, which decreases the subsequent discharge
capacity. In addition, the voids were observed mainly between
the SE and AM particles. This means that interface formation
between SE and AM particles is important to achieve a high
capacity at low stack pressure. It is also necessary to select
suitable solid electrolytes for mechanical properties and
chemical compatibility with sulfide active materials.
Cells with In/InLi as the negative electrode showed a high
reversible capacity at a high stack pressure set before cycling
and at a constant stack pressure of 50 MPa during cycling. In
contrast, the cells using LTO as the negative electrode showed
a high initial charge capacity, even at 5 MPa, but poor cycle
performance, even at 50 MPa. This demonstrated the effect of
the initial stack pressure on the charge−discharge capacity of
the cell, which depends on the choice of the negative electrode.
In/InLi and LTO composite electrodes exhibit significant
differences in volume changes during cycling. If the volume
change of the negative electrode compensates for the volume
change of the positive electrode, the stack pressure might be
maintained during charging and discharging even though both
electrodes change their volume. Thus, cells with an In/InLi
negative electrode should exhibit superior cycling performance.
To test this hypothesis, cells of the types LTO|Li2S−LiI and
Li−In|Li2S−LiI were cycled under similar conditions with the
stack pressure set to 10 MPa only before cycling, and Figure 6
shows the cycling performance of these cells. Although the
initial charge capacities were almost identical, the capacity
retention was higher for the cell with In/InLi than with LTO.
This difference in capacity is clearly due to the different molar
volume changes of the negative electrode, as discussed in the
following in terms of the molar reaction volume |ΔrV̅m|of the
cell reaction. In/InLi shows |ΔrV̅m| = 7.89 cm3 mol−127 during
Li insertion or extraction, whereas LTO shows a volume
change close to zero upon lithiation/delithiation.33 The
reaction volume of the sulfur conversion reaction is |ΔrV̅m| of
Li2S to S is 6.09 cm3 mol−1,27 which is not too different from
that of In/InLi. Because of these similar volume changes, we
consider the combination of In/InLi and S/Li2S electrodes as
mechanically reasonably well-balanced compared to that of
LTO and S/Li2S. This combination prevents a significant
decrease in the stack pressure upon charging, resulting in
better capacity retention of the cell. However, even the In/
InLi|Li2S−LiI cells showed a decrease in capacity during
cycling. In the cell with LTO, capacity decreased in the
discharge process, suggesting that voids formed in the Li2S
positive electrode at charging lead to contact loss. On the other
hand, in the cell with In/InLi, the In/InLi alloy expands the

■CONCLUSIONS
In ASSLSBs with a high-capacity Li2S-based positive electrode,
we measured the dynamic volume change of the positive
electrode layer and explored the correlation between the
charge−discharge capacity and stack pressure during cycling,
where the stack pressure was solely regulated by the positive
electrode. High charge−discharge capacities and excellent
cyclability were demonstrated when the stack pressure was
maintained constant or when a very high stack pressure was
applied only before cycling. In contrast, when the stack
pressure was not maintained during cycling or set too low,
irreversible capacity loss occurred, and the cell capacity
deteriorated rapidly. In cells utilizing an LTO composite as
the negative electrode, the first charge capacity was almost
independent of the applied pressure, whereas the subsequent
discharge capacity strongly depended on the stack pressure.
The capacity deteriorated upon discharging, which was
attributed to the loss of particle-to-particle contact after
charging at pressures that were too low. To overcome this
problem, an external force must be applied. Another promising
solution is balancing the volume change with a suitable
negative electrode that displays the opposite volume change
corresponding to the volume change with the Li2S-based
positive electrode. Our study presents novel insight into the
electrochemical effects of the large volume expansion and
contraction of sulfur-active materials, which is a serious hurdle
on the way to practically relevant cells.
