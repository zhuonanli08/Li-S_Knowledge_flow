A B S T R A C T  
Lithium-sulfur (Li–S) batteries are attractive due to their high theoretical energy density. However, conventional 
Li–S batteries with liquid electrolytes undergo polysulfide shuttle-effect and lithium dendrite formation during 
charge/discharge process, leading to poor electrochemical performance and safety issues. Garnet type 
Li7La3Zr2O12 (LLZO) solid-state electrolyte (SSE) restricts the penetration of polysulfides and exhibits high ionic 
conductivity at room temperature (RT). Herein, Li6.5La3Zr1.5Nb0.5O12 (LLZNO) ceramic electrolyte using Li3PO4 
(LPO) as sintering aids (LLZNO-LPO) is prepared by the rapid sintering method and is applied to construct a 
shuttle-effect free solid-state Li–S battery. The SSE displays high conductive pure cubic-LLZO phase; during the 
rapid sintering, LPO melts and junctions the voids between the grains, thus improves Li+ conductivity. As a 
result, the LLZNO-LPO ceramic electrolyte with Li+ conductivity of 4.3 × 10−4 S cm−1 and high critical current 
density (CCD) of 1.2 mA cm−2 is obtained at RT. The Li–S solid-state battery which utilizes LLZNO-LPO ceramic 
electrolyte can deliver an initial discharge capacity of 943 mA h⋅g−1 and 602 mA h⋅g−1 retention after 60 cycles. 
In the same time, the initial coulombic efficiency is as high as 99.5%, indicating that the SSE can effectively block 
the polysulfide shuttle towards the Li anode and fulfill a shuttle-free Li–S battery.   
1. Introduction 
Li–S batteries, based on the reversible reaction of 16Li+ + S8 + 16 
e−↔ 8 Li2S, are attractive due to high theoretical energy density (2600 
Wh⋅kg−1), specific capacity (1675 mA h⋅g−1), environmental friendli­
ness, the abundance of sulfur as well as low cost [1–4]. However, the 
inherent polysulfides shuttling problems of Li–S batteries seriously limit 
their practical application in energy storage systems. The intermediate 
polysulfides generated during the charge/discharge process dissolve 
into electrolytic species causing the shuttle effect. These polysulfides 
diffuse through the separator towards the lithium metal anode, causing 
the loss of active material and the corrosion of lithium anode, resulting 
in severe capacity decay and poor coulombic efficiency [5]. Moreover, 
Li-dendrite penetration through the diaphragm causes short circuit and 
thermal runaway, thus igniting flammable organic electrolyte and 
causing burning, even explosion [6–11]. To alleviate these problems, 
various methods have been adopted for both the S, Li electrodes and the 
separators, such as synthesizing S host materials to trap polysulfides [12, 
13], coating Li with polymer to protect the Li electrode, modifying 
separator to suppress polysulfide shuttle and accelerating polysulfide 
conversion [14–16]. However, although some progress has been made 
in the recent works, these strategies cannot fundamentally solve the 
above problems in liquid-state Li–S batteries [16]. 
More recently, replacing the liquid electrolyte with solid-state elec­
trolytes (SSE) such as oxides [17–20], sulfides [21], polymers [22,23], 
and their compounds [24,25] to construct solid-state Li–S batteries are 
highly valued. Among them, Li-stuffed garnet-type Li7La3Zr2O12 (LLZO) 
SSE have drawn much more attention because it has high Li-ion trans­
ference number ~1 and high conductivity above 1 × 10−4 S cm−1 at 
room temperature [26,27]. Moreover, it exhibits superior thermal and 
chemical stability and can be prepared and stored in the air, which is 
suitable for large scale production [28]. Most importantly, it provides a

LiAlO2 [34], ZnO [35], Li2O [36], LiF [37] to densify LLZO ceramics at 
lower temperatures. These additives can form liquid phases below the 
sintering temperature [38], which will accelerate the sintering process. 
Xie et al. [39] reported Li6.4La3Zr1.4Ta0.6O12 (LLZTO) incorporated with 
amorphous Li3BO3 (LBO) to lessen the pores between the grains and also 
reduce the grain boundary resistance, a total Li+ conductivity of 5.47 ×
10−4 S cm−1 was achieved via LLZTO/LBO solid-state electrolyte. 
Li6.75La3Zr1.75Nb0.25O12 with LiAlSiO4 as an additive was prepared by 
Chen’s group [33], which displayed the improved relative density and 
enhanced total ionic conductivity of 6.93 × 10−4 S cm−1 at room tem­
perature. LZO was also added as a sintering aids to densify LLZTO 
ceramic [40]. However, the sintering process still need several hours or 
more and big grains are usually formed [39], which are harmful to the 
fracture strength of ceramic electrolyte [41]; and “Li-loss” is still inev­
itable, so mother powders are still required in the preparation process 
[32]. Recently, our group presented a rapid ultra-high-temperature 
sintering method without mother powder to manufacture high-quality 
LLZO ceramics [17]. This method can shorten the sintering time to 
only 10 min or less, which can effectively restrain “Li-loss” and inhibit 
grain growth, manufacture LLZO ceramic with high conductivity. This 
method was also later verified by Hu’s group [42,43]. With the help of 
sintering aids, the rapid ultra-high-temperature sintering method might 
offer a prospect to prepare LLZO ceramics with small grain size, dense 
microstructure and high conductivity. 
In this work, the garnet ceramic electrolytes using Li3PO4 (LPO) as 
sintering aids are manufactured by the rapid ultra-high-temperature 
sintering method and are applied to construct a shuttle-effect free 
solid-state Li–S battery. Nb5+ was doped into the LLZO structure to 
stabilize the cubic phase; LPO is used as sintering aids to eliminate the 
grain clearance and to improve the conductivity of the SSE. The optimal 
rapid sintering conditions for the LLZO ceramics are discussed. The 
compatibility of Li anode/LLZNO-LPO ceramic electrolyte is evaluated 
by the interface resistance detected by electrochemical impedance 
spectroscopy (EIS) and the critical current density (CCD). Then, a solid- 
state Li–S battery is constructed using the sintered ceramic as an elec­
trolyte, and the electrochemical performance of the as-assembled bat­
tery was tested at a specific current of 0.1C within a voltage window of 
1.7–2.8 V at 25 ◦C. 
2. Experimental section 
2.1. Preparation of solid electrolyte 
Li6.5La3Zr1.5Nb0.5O12 (LLZNO) powders were prepared via a con­
ventional solid-state reaction method, and the ceramic electrolytes were 
sintered by using the rapid ultra-high-temperature sintering method 
described in our previous work [17]. Stoichiometric amount of 
LiOH⋅H2O (99%, Adamas, 10 wt% excess to compensate lithium vola­
tilization), La2O3 (99.9%, pre-heated at 900 ◦C for 12 h), ZrO2 (99.99%, 
Aladdin), Nb2O5 (99.99%) were mixed with planetary ball milling at 
500 rpm for 10 h with isopropanol and yttrium stabilized zirconium 
oxide beads. The obtained mixture was dried at 70 ◦C and calcinated in 
MgO crucible at 950 ◦C for 6 h to form LLZNO powders. 1 wt% Li3PO4 
(LPO) as sintering aids was then added into the as-prepared powders. 
The mixture was ground at 400 rpm for 16 h through wet ball-milling 
method, and then dried at 70 ◦C to obtain fine powders. The obtained 
powder was uniaxially pressed into green pellets at 300 MPa with a 
diameter of 15 mm. Afterward, the green pellets were sintered in a small 
MgO crucible using the rapid ultra-high-temperature sintering method. 
In this sintering method, no mother powder is needed. Finally, the 
LLZNO-Li3PO4 (LLZNO-LPO) ceramic electrolyte pellets were obtained, 
and the optical sintering schedule was discussed. The preparation pro­
cess of ceramic electrolyte is illustrated in Fig. 1. 
2.2. Characterization of electrolyte 
The sintered CEs were dry-polished by sandpaper from 400 to 2000 
grit before characterization and assembling in a solid-state battery. 
Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) (Bruker AXS D8, Bruker AXS GmbH) 
was used to analyze the phase composition of the LLZNO powders and 
the sintered pellets. The particle size distributions of the garnet fine 
powders were measured by a laser granularity analyzer. The conduc­
tivities of the CEs were tested using an electrochemical workstation 
(Zahner, Model Zennium). Pt/LLZO/Pt was kept at 25 ◦C for at least 30 
min before the test. The frequency ranged 4 M–1 Hz and the amplitude 
was 20 mV. 
The densities of the CEs were calculated via weight/volume. The 
relative densities (R.D.) are defined as the ratio of calculated densities 
with the theoretical density, which was obtained from XRD results (5.14 
g cm−3 for Li6.5La3Zr1.5Nb0.5O12). The fractured surface morphology of 
as-prepared LLZO CE was investigated by scanning electron microscopy, 
JSM-6610LV.

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. The significance of Li3PO4 additive 
The cross-sectional morphology of the as-prepared LLZNO and 
LLZNO-LPO ceramic electrolyte (CE) are displayed in Fig. 2a–b. It can be 
seen that LLZNO CE owns smooth polyhedron grains with good crys­
tallinity; gaps and voids emerge between the grains, which would 
magnify grain boundary resistance and reduce ionic conductivity [46]. 
Interestingly, these void spaces almost disappear after the addition of 
LPO, as shown in Fig. 2b. During ultra-high temperature sintering pro­
cess of about 1340 ◦C, the low-melting-point LPO (~837 ◦C) [47] 
transforms into a liquid phase and occupies the void spaces in LLZNO, 
which will make the structure more compact; Li3PO4 also exhibits ion­
ically conducting property [48]. Thus, Li3PO4 may behave as the binder 
to consolidate the grain boundaries and improve the conductivity. The 
grain sizes for pure LLZNO and LLZNO-LPO are listed in Table 1. It can 
be found that the LLZNO-LPO grains grow a little larger than pure 
LLZNO, but the grain sizes of both are all below 20 μm.

CEs, XRD was carried out. As shown in Fig. S1 and Fig. S2, LLZNO 
powders own cubic-LLZO phase, and the particle size distribution of 
LLZNO and LLZNO-LPO fine powders possess similar shapes. These fine 
powders own D(50) ~0.5 μm and can be cold-pressed into green pellets 
easily. Furthermore, they were sintered by the rapid ultra-high-

3.2. Determination of rapid sintering temperature for LLZNO-LPO 
ceramics 
To further improve the quality of the LLZNO-LPO ceramic electro­
lyte, the optimal rapid sintering schedule is studied. First, the LLZNO- 
LPO ceramics were sintered at a series of high temperatures of 
1320 ◦C, 1340 ◦C, and 1360 ◦C at a constant sintering duration of 1 min. 
The morphology of the as-prepared samples at different sintering tem­
peratures is displayed in Fig. 3a–c. The average grain size at 1320 ◦C × 1 
min is 4–12 μm and 4–18 μm at 1340 ◦C × 1 min. The grain boundaries

Li-ion conductivity.

3.3. Optimal rapid sintering time for LLZNO-LPO ceramics 
The effect of rapid sintering time duration was studied by sintering 
pellets at optimal sintering temperature of 1340 ◦C with different time 
durations of 1 min, 8 min, and 16 min. The morphology of the as- 
prepared samples at different sintering time duration is shown in 
Fig. 4a–c. The average grain size at 1340 ◦C × 1 min is 4–12 μm. As the 
sintering time increases to 8 min, grain boundaries are consolidated 
more tightly with a modest grain size of 5–20 μm. Furthermore, some 
giant grains with grain size >100 μm appears when sintered for 16 min 
due to AGG phenomenon with void spaces develop at grain boundaries. 
These void spaces will reduce the R.D. and Li-ion conductivity of the 
electrolyte. 
The calculated values of conductivity and relative densities are listed 
in Table 3. LLZNO-LPO ceramic sintered for 1 min illustrates the lowest 
conductivity. When sintered for 8 min, the conductivity and R.D. reach 
the highest value (4.28 × 10−4 S cm−1 and 92.5%). While sintered for 
16 min, the gaps and void emerge due to the AGG phenomenon; 
therefore, the conductivity and R.D. reduced to 3.43 × 10−4 S cm−1 and 
90.6% respectively. As a result, 1340 ◦C × 8 min is considered as the 
optimal rapid sintering temperature and time for LLZNO-LPO to achieve 
the ceramic electrolyte with the highest conductivity and improved R.D.

3.4. Compatibility of anode/LLZNO-LPO ceramic electrolyte 
To better depict the interface compatibility of lithium anode/LLZNO- 
LPO ceramic electrolyte, two kinds of Li symmetric cells were assembled 
and tested in Swagelok-type cell molds. For Li/LLZNO-LPO/Li cell, 
molten Li (295 ◦C) directly contacts the LLZNO-LPO ceramic and can 
wet the ceramic surface well, as shown in Fig. S4. Electrochemical 
impedance spectroscopy (EIS) spectra of as-assembled and stand for 96 h 
Li/Garnet/Li batteries were displayed in Fig. 5a–b. The high-frequency 
region (above 4.1 × 105 Hz) in the EIS plot corresponds to the bulk/ 
grain-boundary of LLZO ceramic, and the large semicircle mainly in 
105 Hz-Hz indicates the Li/LLZO interface resistances. And the x-inter­
cept of the plot specifies the resistance value [50]. The LLZNO-LPO 
ceramic electrolyte contributes 112 Ω cm2, whereas Li/LLZNO-LPO/Li 
displayed an initial total resistance of 3460 Ω cm2. Hence, the total 
interface resistance can be obtained by subtracting the value of total 
resistance of Li/LLZNO-LPO/Li with LLZNO-LPO ceramic electrolyte, 
which is found to be 3348 Ω cm2 (3460–112 = 3348 Ω cm2). Dividing 
the above value by a factor of 2, the single interface resistance can be 
attained, which is ~1674 Ω cm2. After 96 h of assembling, the total 
resistance increased to 6740 Ω cm2 with a single side interface resistance 
of ~3314 Ω cm2. A recent study reveals that a significant reduction of 
Nb5+ takes place in Nb-doped LLZO when in contact with Li metal [51]. 
This instability makes the interface resistance large and increase 
significantly with time, such as after 96 h. 
The cell assembled by wetting with LE (Li/LLZNO-LPO + LE/Li) 
displayed an initial resistance of 411 Ω cm2 with a single side interface 
resistance of ~150 Ω cm2. Moreover, after 96 h, total resistance 
increased slightly to 470 Ω cm2 with single side interface resistance of 
~179 Ω cm2. The LE not only contributes to decreasing the interface 
resistance by wetting the interface but also prevents the direct contact of 
LLZNO with Li metal. The slightly increased interface resistance in­
dicates that a more stable anode side interface is constructed and the LE 
may protect LLZNO-LPO electrolyte from reduction by lithium anode. 
Galvanostatic cycling of Li/LLZNO-LPO + LE/Li symmetric cell at 
25 ◦C with a fixed stripping/plating capacity of 0.05 mA h cm−2 is 
shown in Fig. 5c. The current densities were set to increase subsequently 
from 0.05 to 3.2 mA cm−2. As the current density increases, the voltage 
increases linearly. When the current density is as high as 1.4 mA cm−2, 
the voltage suddenly drops to a low level and becomes a saw-tooth 
configuration, showing the occurrence of partial short circuit. Thus, 
1.2 mA cm−2 is determined to be the CCD for the LLZNO-LPO ceramic 
electrolyte in Li/LLZNO-LPO + LE/Li symmetric cell. However, in the 
case of Li/LLZNO-LPO/Li symmetric cell, the CCD for the LLZNO-LPO 
ceramic electrolyte is only 0.05 mA cm−2 (Fig. S4), which is probably 
because of the instability of the interface between the LLZNO and Li 
metal due to overpotential and formation of lithium dendrites [50].

The EIS curve of the Li/LLZNO-LPO/S battery before cycling shows 
one semicircle, one short flat line, and a long upward tail as indicated in 
Fig. 6a. The semicircle from 3.9 × 105–32 Hz corresponds to the in­
terfaces of Li/LE, LLZNO-LPO/LE, and the diffusion of Li-ion from LE to 
S cathode [52]. The short flat line from 32–0.8 Hz corresponds to the 
charge transfer at the cathode side. The total resistance of the Li-Sulfur 
solid battery is ~337 Ω cm2, which is lower than garnet based solid 
batteries published recently [39,53]. 
Electrochemical performance of the Li/LLZNO-LPO/S battery tested 
at a specific current of 0.1C at 25 ◦C is shown in Fig. 6b–c. The coin cells 
were cycled within a voltage window of 1.7–2.8 V. At ~2.35 V and ~2.1 
V, two voltage peaks appeared, as shown in Fig. 6b. The multistep 
reduction mechanism of S with Li resulted in an intermediate highly 
soluble polysulfide (Li2Sn, n = 8–1), which finally leads to Li2S/Li2S2 
[50]. The discharge specific capacities for the 1st, 3rd, 5th, 10th, 20th, 
40th, 60th cycles are ca. 943, 933, 902, 805, 758, 638, 602 mA h⋅g−1, 
respectively. The voltage hysteresis increases from the initial 0.25 
V–0.27 V as the cycles prolong. The initial coulombic efficiency reaches 
99.5%, and the average coulombic efficiency for all the cycles is 99.4%. 
The high initial coulombic efficiency indicates that the LLZNO-LPO solid 
electrolyte can effectively block the polysulfide shuttle towards the Li 
anode and fulfill a shuttle-free Li–S battery. Although the initial specific 
capacity reaches 943 mA h⋅g−1, the capacity retention is not satisfactory. 
The capacity loss is probably due to the formation of “dead” sulfur at the 
cathode side [18] and the polarization caused by the relatively large 
interface impedance and the insufficient conductivity of solid electrolyte 
compared with liquid electrolyte. During the charging process, the 
redistribution of active sulfur attached on the electronic insulated areas 
may form the “dead” sulfur. All these issues are worthy of further study 
by researchers. 
4. Conclusion 
In summary, LLZNO ceramic electrolyte using LPO as sintering aids 
are prepared by the rapid sintering method and are applied to construct 
a shuttle-effect free solid-state Li–S battery. The LLZNO-LPO ceramic 
electrolyte owns cubic LLZO structure without any impurity phase, 
signifying that this rapid sintering method along with Nb doping and 
LPO additive is beneficial to develop a high conductive cubic-phase 
LLZO ceramic electrolyte; during the rapid ultra-high temperature sin­
tering process, LPO transforms into a liquid phase and occupies the voids 
in LLZNO, consolidates the grain boundaries and improves Li+ con­
ductivity. Among different sintering conditions, 1340 ◦C × 8 min is an 
optimal rapid sintering schedule for LLZNO-LPO to achieve a high 
conductivity of 4.3 × 10−4 S cm−1 at RT; the CCD for the LLZNO-LPO CE 
in Li/LLZNO-LPO + LE/Li symmetric cell is as high as 1.2 mA cm−2. 
Utilizing LLZNO-LPO ceramic electrolyte in Li–S solid-state battery, the 
cell delivers an initial discharge capacity of 943 mA h⋅g−1 and 602 mA 
h⋅g−1 after 60th cycle. The initial coulombic efficiency reaches a high 
value of 99.5%. As a result, it can conclude that the LLZNO-LPO SSE can 
successfully restrict the polysulfide shuttle. Therefore, the rapid sinter­
ing of garnet electrolyte with sintering aids is a constructive approach to 
prepare high quality ceramic SSE to fulfill shuttle-effect free solid-state 
Li–S batteries.
