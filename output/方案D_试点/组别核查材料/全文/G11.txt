A B S T R A C T  
The blend of air stability and high lithium-ion (Li+) conductivity is not a simplistic approach to attain for sulfide- 
based solid-state electrolytes (SSEs), which hinders the exploitation of high energy all-solid-state lithium-sulfur 
batteries (ASSLSBs). Herein we report a novel lithium superionic conductor of Li7Sb0.05P2.95S10.5I0.5 as solid-state 
glass-ceramics electrolytes obtained by an annealing treatment in a solid-state reaction route. It systematically 
explores the impact of facile aliovalent dual doping into the newly synthesized solid electrolytes, which have 
influenced higher Li+ conductivity of 2.55 × 10−3 Scm−1 at room temperature, and wide range of voltage sta­
bility vs. Li/Li+ up to 7 V. Except that, an activation barrier of Li7Sb0.05P2.95S10.5I0.5 for Li+ migration drops 
expressively due to optimizing the dopant contents, and subsequently defects produced. The electrolyte also 
achieved significant air stability based on Hard and Soft Acid/Base (HSAB) theory. The intrinsic structural as­
pects of the air stability for Li7P3S11 and Li7Sb0.05P2.95S10.5I0.5 solid-state electrolytes are premeditated using a 
combination of ex-situ X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, XRD as well as Raman spectroscopy and SEM. The Li2S- 
VGCF-SSE composite cathode with Li7Sb0.05P2.95S10.5I0.5 SSE exhibited a high initial discharge capacity of 622.3 
mAhg−1 at 0.060 mAcm−2, and ASSLSB was retained over 683.3 mAh g−1 after 15th cycle at room temperature, 
better than pristine Li7P3S11 SSE-based cell. This research provides a novel concept on the design of air-stable and 
superionic conductor solid-state electrolytes for high-performance ASSLSBs.   
1. Introduction 
Rechargeable Lithium-Sulfur (Li/S) batteries are promising candi­
dates of high energy densities for extensive next-generation energy 
storage systems [1–3]. In finical, the shuttling effect of dissolved poly- 
sulfides between Li metal and the sulfur-based cathode is limiting the 
consumption of active mass and exacerbates the electrochemical prop­
erties [4,5]. Consequently, Li/S batteries generally exhibited rapid self- 
discharge and poor cyclic stability. Besides, owing to the flammability of 
organic solvents and the continued production of lithium dendrites, the 
safety of the Li/S batteries was another critical issue to resolve. More­
over, safety concerns appear to be a nightmare for liquid-based Li/S 
batteries [6,7]. The findings are emphasizing to the aspect that liquid 
phase organic electrolyte is significant culprits. 
Inorganic solid-state electrolytes are fast emerging candidates as a 
valuable element of Li/S battery due to their excellent mechanical 
attributes, superior electrochemical stability, and working over a broad 
range of temperatures [8]. With the assist of a solid-state approach, the 
shuttle and dissolution of polysulfide, as well as outflow issues, can be 
entirely rectified [9]. The solid-state electrolytes for all-solid-state bat­
teries primarily comprise a sulfide-based electrolyte system and an 
oxide-based electrolyte system [10–13]. In the evaluation of both solid- 
state electrolytes, the oxide-based electrolyte can demonstrate a lower 
grain boundary resistance and a lower Li+ conductivity to achieve 10−4 
S cm−1 at room temperature with poor interface in between electrode/ 
electrolyte such as Garnet [14–17]. 
Lithium thiophosphates are incredibly believed as a prospective solid 
electrolyte in all-solid-state Li/S batteries due to their intrinsic 
extremely high ionic and low electronic conductivity along with struc­
tural stability [18]. Additionally, it was promulgated that thiophosphate

The air stability of the lithium thiophosphate adopts the principle of the 
Hard and Soft Acid/Base (HSAB) theory. Hard acids are of small ionic 
radii, empty orbitals in the valence shell, and high energy LUMOs, non- 
polarizable as compared to soft acids, which are large in size, filled 
orbital with low energy LUMOs and easily polarized. According to the 
HSAB theory, lithium thiophosphate-based solid electrolytes are highly 
reactive with oxygen because oxygen is a hard base that reacts favorably 
with hard acid phosphorus as compared to soft base sulfur. 
Herein, a novel modest air-stable and superionic conductor lithium 
thiophosphate-based Li7P3S11 solid-state electrolyte reported which 
acquired via dual Antimony sulfide (Sb2S5) and Lithium Iodide (LiI) 
aliovalent dual doping, based on the HSAB theory. Soft acid antimony 
(Sb) was selected as a dopant element with phosphorus (P). This forms a 
composite with sulfur and consequently impact on stability against ox­
ygen, which is a significantly harder base than sulfur. By increasing Sb 
fraction, the migration pathway for Li+ enlarges and creates a site dis­
order among I−
and S2−. The rising defects are associated with a 
decrease in activation energy and increase the Li+ conductivity over 
orders of magnitude. Partially doping of Sb with P offers noticeable 
advantageous stability against water and oxygen. The problems 
addressed in this research (1) can cause an aliovalent cation of Sb and 
anion I improve conductivity and decrease in the activation barrier? (2) 
Does the Sb-doped thiophosphate have anticipated air and water sta­
bility? In this study, the Sb5+ and I−ions were simultaneously intro­
duced into the conductive framework of Li7P3S11. The air stability of the 
thiophosphate-based solid-state electrolytes was demonstrated for the 
first time by the ex-situ X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy. Raman 
spectroscopy and SEM have been used to explain further the inherent 
dynamics of air stability in the Li7P3S11 and dual doped Li7Sb0.05­
P2.95S10.5I0.5. The crystal structure, Li+ conductivity, activation energy, 
examining the electrochemical stability with Li of pristine Li7P3S11 and 
Li7Sb0.05P2.95S10.5I0.5 were also periodically characterized. Moreover, 
the electrochemical performance of assembled all-solid-state Li/S bat­
teries with pristine Li7P3S11 and aliovalent dual doped Li7Sb0.05­
P2.95S10.5I0.5 solid-state electrolytes was also demonstrated. 
2. Experimental 
2.1. Preparation method 
Li2S (99.9%; Alfa Aesar), P2S5 (99%, P ≥27%, Macklin), Sb2S5 (98%; 
Macklin) and LiI (98.5%; J&K) were used as precursor compounds 
which were amalgamated in the stoichiometric proportional ratio to 
synthesized Li7SbxP3−xS11−yIy (x = 0.05, 0.1 and y = 0.5, 0.7) amor­
phous solid-state electrolyte via high-energy ball milling. The mixture of 
all precursors was milled at 510 rpm for 40 h at room temperature. A 
high-energy ball milling setup (Fritsch, Pulverisette 7) comprising a 
zirconia pot (volume: 45 mL) with five balls (diameter: 10 mm) made of 
zirconia. The as-prepared Li7SbxP3−xS11−yIy (x = 0.05, 0.1 and y = 0.5, 
0.7) amorphous powdered, annealed at 250–280 ◦C/4h to obtained 
glass-ceramics solid-state electrolytes samples respectively to the Dif­
ferential Thermal Analysis (DTA) plots Fig. S1. All the procedures, as 
mentioned, were carried out in an argon-filled glove box (H2O & O2 <
0.1 ppm). 
2.2. Theoretical method 
For the appliance of building Li7Sb0.05P2.95S10.5I0.5 modal framework 
supercell consisting 84 formula unit (f.u.) of Li7P3S11 was designed to 
simulate with (x = 0.05, y = 0.5). One P atom and one S atoms in the 
modal framework supercell were exchanged by Sb atom and two I atom 
respectively, according to sustain electronic neutrality. The simulated 
XRD pattern for Li7Sb0.05P2.95S10.5I0.5 was simulated at (0.154 nm) Cu 
Kɑ radiation wavelength with VESTA software. 
2.3. Characterizations 
X-ray Diffractometer (XRD, D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany) mea­
surements were conducted with copper Kα radiation by utilizing 
VANTEC-1 detector. To avoid the effect of air, these samples were 
covered by a thin polymer film. XRD characteristic peaks were examined 
for the phase identification of as-synthesized glass–ceramic electrolytes 
from 10◦to 60◦with a scan rate of 5◦min−1. Raman spectra of glass- 
ceramics electrolyte samples were conducted by using Renishaw-2000 
Raman spectrophotometer with laser beam 532 nm wavelength. The 
morphology and elemental mapping of the synthesized sample were 
measured by using a Scanning electron microscope (SEM, SUPRA 55, 
ZIES, Germany). The analysis of elements was carried out with X-ray 
photoelectron spectroscopy (Thermo Fisher Scientific ESCALab 250Xi, 
USA). The differential thermal analysis (DTA) plots were measured by 
using an (SII TG/DTA6200, EXSTAR, Japan), which operates from 0 to 
350 ◦C at the heating rate of 10 ◦C under Argon gas flow. 
2.4. Electrochemical measurements of glass-ceramics electrolytes 
The ionic conductivity of solid-state electrolyte samples was 
measured by analysis of electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), 
which was conducted on an electrochemical workstation (PARSTAT, 
2273) immoderate range of frequency 10 Hz to 1 MHz under the 15 ◦C- 
75 ◦C temperature range. The dual doped and pristine SSEs samples 
were cold-pressed into tablets at 360 MPa (thickness: ~1mm, diameter 
10 mm) with two stainless steel plates (SS) used as counter blocking 
electrode in the argon-filled glove box (H2O & O2 < 0.1 ppm). The 
electrochemical stability (Cyclic Voltammetry) of solid-state electrolytes 
was analyzed by using an electrochemical workstation (PARSTAT, 
2273) with scan rate 0.5mVs−1 and voltage range from −0.5 to 7 V vs. 
(Li/Li+) at room temperature. The SS was used as the working electrode 
and Li metal as a counter electrode, respectively. 
The amount of Hydrogen sulfide (H2S) produced from as-synthesized 
solid-state samples were measured. Tableted samples (200 mg), an H2S 
gas sensor (GX-2009, Riken Keiki Co, Ltd, Japan) which filled with moist 
air. The occupied volume of the jar was (2880 cm3). The quantity of H2S 
gas produce form the prepared sample was measured from their con­
centration at room temperature, and the relative moisture level was kept 
40–47% during the calculation.

noticed that the Li-ion as an only mobile entity in the synthesized solid- 
state electrolyte, the movement of Li-ion in solid-state electrolytes, is 
analogous to that of commercial used liquid electrolytes. 
For solid-state electrolyte, the addition of external elements into the 
lattice via aliovalent cation and anion dopants disturbed the lattice of 
synthesized composite and generated the higher interstitials and defects.

between framework and migrating ions. At doping level (x = 0.05 y =
0.5), the formation of conductive units (PS4

the Li-ion conductivity approaches is reached to maximum limit [36]. 
The fine-tuning of carrier species and optimization of the lattice size in 
framework unit by aliovalent ions doping or substitution contributed in 
superionic conductivity and reduced the activation barrier [37,38]. It is 
significant to note that activation energy versus the amount of doped Sb 
and I showed a reversed peak of conductivity Fig. 3 (c). The activation 
energy for ionic conduction, calculated from the slope of an Arrhenius 
graph, was 20.04 KJmol−1 for the Li7Sb0.05P2.95S10.5I0.5 sample. Ac­
cording to the equation 1, the conduction of ions is affected by heating 
exponentially over the Ea. A low value of Ea was in agreement, with the 
minor changing in the conductivity while varying the temperature. After 
the aliovalent dual doping, in solid-state electrolyte a low Ea for Li-ion 
hopping represents that more polarizable lattice and facilitated the ion 
movement. An optimum radius of foreigner element induced (Sb+5 =
0.61 nm, P+5 = 0.33 nm) supports the ionic mobility within the structure 
and minimize the activation energy. For instance, the Ea of the larger ion 
is less as compared to a smaller one [39]. It can be concluded that by 
using the newly prepared solid-state solution of Li7Sb0.05P2.95S10.5I0.5, a 
weaker lattice dynamic and lower activation energy for Li+ mobility 
within conductive framework were observed. 
3.3. Air stability 
The principal aim of this contribution is to attain air stability of 
sulfide-based solid-state electrolytes. One major disadvantage of sulfide- 
based solid-state electrolytes is their instability toward moist air, which 
would lead to the generation of toxic H2S gas. According to Hard and 
Soft, Acid/Base theory, the instability of the existing Li with P sulfides 
root showed higher oxophilicity behavior because of the P5+ hard acid 
nature. The soft dopant acid of Sb5+ is expected to connect more strongly 
with the existing soft base of S2−
as compare to P5+ and shows the 
oxophobicity. This analysis is opposite in Li7P3S11, which suffers from 
immediate hydrolysis and changes their conductive phase framework 
when revealing to air. Therefor a solid-state electrolyte has shown sta­
bility in air. 
To check the stability of Li7Sb0.05P2.95S10.5I0.5 in ambient atmo­
sphere, this synthesized sample was exposed in higher humidity of 
40–47% in the air for a certain amount of period. It was noticed that 
hydrogen sulfide gas (H2S) appeared as a significant raw product when 
solid-state electrolytes were exposed to moisture; the amount of pro­
duced H2S versus time graph was plotted, which described the stability 
of solid electrolyte samples. The results indicate that Li7Sb0.05­
P2.95S10.5I0.5 reacts with moisture air in Fig. 4 (a) after t = 35 min, and 
the amount of H2S was only 0.37 cm3g−1 (26 ppm), while 1.32 cm3 g−1 
(92 ppm) for Li7P3S11 at the same time. Fig. 4 (b) evaluate ex-situ XRD 
plots of pristine Li7P3S11 after it was exposed at laboratory conditions 
(40–47% humidity, RT) for 20 min. The ex-situ XRD curve intensities 
broadening as well as diminishing of conductive phase peak after the air 
exposure could be due to moisture absorbed in the Li7P3S11. As expected 
for Li7Sb0.05P2.95S10.5I0.5 the peak intensity also remained unchanged 
after air exposure.

this 
work,

solid-state

Li7SbxP3−xS11−yIy (x = 0.05, 0.1 and y = 0.5, 0.7) was synthesized by an 
annealing treatment in a solid-state reaction route via high energy ball 
milling. Attaining high Li+ conductivity, along with remarkable air 
stability that enables battery processing, leftovers a great challenge for 
the solid-state electrolyte. Specifically, a new superionic Li+ conductor 
Li7Sb0.05P2.95S10.5I0.5 was prepared that exhibited an auspicious Li+
conductivity of 2.55 × 10−3 S cm−1 at room temperature and consid­

stable against oxidation and hydrolysis. The exclusive mechanical 
studied of dual doped Li7Sb0.05P2.95S10.5I0.5 was carried out by a com­
bination of ex-situ X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, XRD as well as 
Raman spectroscopy and SEM. The structural changes and large lattice 
volume, which lead to a decrease in the activation barrier for Li+
mobility, induced by aliovalent dual doping of large size Sb5+ and I−. 
Combined with the Li2S-composite cathode includes Li2S, VGCF, and 
solid-state electrolyte.

discharge capacity of 622.3 mAh g−1 and retained over 687.3 mAh g−1

discharge capacity than solid-state cells based on a Li7P3S11 electrolyte

has excellent potential in the practical application for all-solid-state 
lithium-sulfur batteries.
