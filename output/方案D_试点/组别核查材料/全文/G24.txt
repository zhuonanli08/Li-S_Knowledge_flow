A B S T R A C T  
Solid polymer electrolytes (SPEs) were investigated for several decades due to their good compatibility with 
electrodes. However, meeting the essential requirements for solid polymer electrolytes, including enhanced ionic 
conductivity, superior solubility with conductive lithium salts, and robust interface stability when in contact with 
a Li anode, remains a formidable challenge. In our work, we found that transition metal carbides (NiNbCeC) over 
mesoporous silica (SBA-15) can be filled into the Polyethylene Oxide (PEO) matrix to form composite solid 
electrolytes which can greatly improve the ionic conductivity of PEO. We investigate a series of transition metal 
carbides with varying mass ratios of Cerium (5%, 10%, and 20%) to fabricate All-Solid-State Li-S batteries 
(ASSLi-S). Our results demonstrate that different mass ratios of Cerium play a pivotal role in enhancing the ionic 
conductivity of the composite solid electrolytes, reducing impedance levels from 9150 O (20% Ce) to 4000 O (5% 
Ce) to 1730 O (10% Ce). At room temperature, the high initial specific capacity at 1C and reversible specific 
capacity after 100 cycles are 525 and 363 mAh/g, 605 and 403 mAh/g, 246.3 mAh/g and 63.28 mAh/g for 5% 
Ce, 10%, and 20%, respectively. Furthermore, long-term cycling tests of ASSLi-S cells at 0.5C reveal promising 
performance, with the cell containing 10% Ce solid electrolytes delivering an initial specific capacity of 1165.7 
mAh/g and maintaining 335 mAh/g after 200 cycles. This represents a significant breakthrough, demonstrating 
that PEO-based solid electrolytes can achieve impressive electrochemical results at room temperature. SEM 
characterization was employed to assess the volume change in the solid electrolyte layer and elemental distri­
bution of solid electrolyte materials through cross-sectional analysis. This analysis revealed that the failure 
modes of solid-state Li-S cells can also be attributed to the migration of sulfur elements from the cathode to the 
solid electrolyte layer during the cycling process. XRD was utilized to study the crystallinity of composite solid 
electrolytes. This study represents a significant stride in the progress of high-performance solid polymer elec­
trolytes for All-Solid-State Li-S batteries, shedding light on the failure modes of such batteries. It underscores the 
potential of composites based on transition metal carbides in addressing crucial challenges for their practical 
implementation in Solid-State Lithium-Sulfur Batteries.   
1. Introduction 
Li-S batteries have emerged as a highly promising alternative to 
traditional Li-ion batteries due to their owing to their exceptional 
theoretical specific capacity (1675 mAh⋅g−1) and energy density of 2600 
Wh⋅kg−1, coupled with their cost-effectiveness (abundance in earth) and 
environmentally friendly nature (nontoxic of sulfur). [1–4] However, 
the practical implementation of Li-S batteries was hindered by several 
challenges such as the notorious shuttle effect of lithium polysulfides 
(LPS), the formation of lithium dendrite, and the safety concerns 
associated with liquid electrolytes solvent. [5–7] Consequently, 
tremendous efforts have been devoted into surmounting these limita­
tions inherent to Li-S batteries. Among all the strategies, the develop­
ment of All-Solid-State Li-S batteries gained significant attention to 
overcome the limitations of traditional Li-S batteries. [8–10]. 
Generally, solid electrolytes can be categorized into inorganic solid 
electrolytes (sulfide and oxide-based) and polymer electrolytes based on 
their chemical composition. Inorganic solid electrolytes, especially 
sulfide-based solid electrolytes, have been widely investigated due to 
their high ionic conductivity (>10-3 S cm−1 at room temperature) and

(LPS), and Li6PS5Cl (LPSCl).[11–19] All of these materials have been 
extensively explored. Additionally, the oxide electrolytes possess ad­
vantages like excellent thermal stability, high ionic conductivity (10-6 S

windows such as Li7La3Zr2O12 (LLZO), Li0.33La0.557TiO3 (LLTO) and

is impeded by their interfacial issues, instability under ambient condi­

window. [26,27]. 
Besides the inorganic solid electrolytes, extensive research efforts 
have been directed towards incorporating diverse inorganic fast ionic 
conductors into the structure of organic polymers, along with various 
lithium salts.[28,29] In recent years, there has been a surge in research 
focused on exploring different solid polymer electrolytes (SPE) featuring 
high molecular weight. These SPEs are predominantly based on dielec­
tric polymer hosts, including poly (ethylene oxide) (PEO), poly (vinyl­
idene fluoride) (PVDF), polyacrylonitrile (PAN), and poly (methyl 
methacrylate) (PMMA).[30–40] Among the SPEs, particularly poly 
(ethylene oxide) (PEO) has garnered significant attention due to its ionic 
conducting property and its ability to interact with various lithium salts 
through the ether oxygen bonds, facilitating cation solvation. [41] In 
addition, PEO-based SPEs exhibit desirable qualities such as flexibility, 
plasticity, excellent contact properties, and compatibility with electrode 
interfaces. However, it’s important to note that PEO is inherently a 
crystalline polymer, and the crystalline nature of PEO hinders the 
migration of lithium ions because crystallization slows down the motion 
of polymer chains.[42–44] Conversely, the amorphous phase, charac­
terized by freely activated chain segments above its glass transition 
temperature (Tg), is conducive to efficient ion transport. [45,46] Despite 
these advantages, PEO-based SPEs face challenges due to their low ionic 
conductivities at room temperature, limited thermal stability, mechan­
ical properties, and narrow electrochemical stability windows. Conse­
quently, the practical application of PEO-based SPEs in commercial 
lithium-ion batteries has been limited.[47–49] As a result, extensive 
research efforts have been devoted to mitigating PEO’s crystallinity and 
enhancing the ion conductivity and mechanical properties of PEO-based 
SPEs. [50–53]. 
Various strategies have been employed to enhance the ionic con­
ductivity of solid polymer electrolytes (SPEs) based on poly (ethylene 
oxide) (PEO). These approaches encompass blending with other poly­
mers, incorporating plasticizers and fillers, grafting, and crosslinking 
modifications. Among these methods, the incorporation of granular 
ceramic fillers into the PEO matrix has been extensively researched, as it 
offers simultaneous improvements in ionic conductivity, mechanical 
strength, and electrochemical stability. Ceramic fillers are believed to 
impede the crystallization of the polymer, while also creating ion 
transport pathways at the interface between the filler and polymer, 
thereby augmenting ionic conductivity. Typically, fillers used in poly­
mer matrices fall into two categories: inert fillers, such as Al2O3[54] and 
SiO2,[55] and active fillers, such as Li3N[56,57] and Li6.4La3Zr1.4­
Ta0.6O12 (LLZTO). [58] Inert fillers do not actively participate in the 
migration of lithium ions, whereas active fillers play a role in facilitating 
the movement of lithium ions. Furthermore, Metal–organic frameworks 
(MOFs), constructed from metal ion clusters and organic linkers, have 
demonstrated their effectiveness as filler materials when incorporated 
into polymer matrices, achieving noteworthy success. Wu et al. utilized 
the widely used MOF material, UIO-66, as an additive in the poly­
ethylene oxide (PEO) matrix to develop novel solid electrolytes for 
lithium-ion batteries. These solid-state lithium-ion batteries, featuring 
the PEO-n-UIO solid electrolyte, demonstrated an initial discharge ca­
pacity of 151 mAh/g. Notably, they exhibited remarkable capacity 
retention, retaining 95 % of their capacity even after undergoing 100 
charge–discharge cycles at 0.5C and 60 ◦C.[50]. 
Our group investigated a number of Ni-based carbides as inert fillers 
for PEO, leveraging its commendable thermal stability, electrical 
conductivity, and mesoporous structure. Our early publication focused 
on NiWC,[51] while this work focused on NiNbCeC. The inclusion of Ce 
was a deliberate choice due to its impact on altering the size of primary 
carbides. [59–61] This adjustment in carbide size is crucial, as smaller 
carbides facilitate enhanced interaction with Li ions, thereby improving 
the conductivity of the solid electrolytes. In this paper, we introduced 
transition metal carbides (TMCs) (specifically NiNbCeC) over meso­
porous silica (SBA-15) as fillers incorporated into the PEO matrix. This 
innovative approach was aimed at augmenting the ionic conductivity 
and subsequently utilized to develop composite solid electrolytes for 
lithium-sulfur (Li-S) cells. Moreover, the effects of varying cerium (Ce) 
ratios within the composite solid electrolyte were explored and ulti­
mately the 10 % Ce was identified as the optimal choice. Eventually, the 
Solid-State Li-S cells deliver an initial specific capacity as 1165.7 mAh/g 
and maintain 335 mAh/g after 200 cycles at 0.5C at room temperature. 
The outstanding performance of TMCs-PEO-LiTFSI-IL-LCO is evident 
when compared with the data from the previous publications in Table 1. 
The novel composite solid-state Li-S batteries demonstrate markedly 
improved cycling stability at high C-rate and high specific capacity at 
room temperature. 
2. Experimental steps 
2.1. Materials 
Elemental Sulfur, N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, 99.5 %), (Poly 
(ethylene oxide) (PEO, average Mv = 300,000), lithium bis(tri­
fluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI, 99.0 %), polyvinylidene fluoride 
(PVDF, average Mw = 534,000), Anhydrous acetonitrile were purchased 
from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MI, USA). Lithium cobalt (III) oxide (LCO, 
97.0 %) was obtained from Alfa Aesar. 
2.2. Preparation of NiNbCeC/SBA-15-IL 
NiNbCeC/SBA-15-IL (Transition metal carbides: TMC) with different 
content of Ce (5 %, 10 %, 20 %) were prepared by impregnation method 
as reported previously.[64] 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium bis(tri­
fluoromethylsulfonyl imide) [BMIMTFSI], and N-butyl-N-methyl­
pyrrolidinium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl) imide [PYR14TFSI] were 
prepared and used as room-temperature ionic liquids as described pre­
viously. [65] The prepared NiNbCeC/SBA-15 was mixed with the ionic 
liquid (IL) with a weight ratio of 3:4 by ultrasonic for 10 min, and then 
heated at 120℃ under a vacuum for 12 h to obtain the NiNbCeC/SBA- 
15-IL (TMCs-IL).

2.3. Preparation of PEO based composites solid electrolytes 
Initially, PEO and LiTFSI were vacuum-dried at 60 ◦C for 24 h prior 
to usage. Subsequently, PEO and LiTFSI were dissolved in acetonitrile 
under magnetic stirring at 60 ◦C for 2 h to create the PEO-LiTFSI solu­
tion. The PEO-LiTFSI solution was then combined with TMCs-IL and 
LCO, and the mixture was subjected to magnetic stirring at 60 ◦C for 24 h 
to produce the composite solid electrolyte materials. 
2.4. Materials characterizations 
X-ray Diffraction (XRD) analysis was employed to characterize 
crystallinity of the prepared materials, including TMCs, composite solid 
electrolyte, and PEO. The instrument utilized for this purpose was the 
Bruker D2 Phaser, operating with Cu Kα radiation at room temperature 
from 10◦to 80◦. Field-emission scanning electron microscopy (JSM- 
7600 FESEM) was employed to analyze the morphology and elemental 
distribution of materials. 
2.5. Preparation of sulfur cathode and assembly solid-state Li-S cells 
Firstly, the cathode slurry was prepared by employing the melt- 
diffusion method as previously reported, which involved mixing 
elemental sulfur with CoS@MWCNT-20 % [1]. The composites consist of 
CoS@MWCNT-20 %/S, solid electrolytes, super P, PVDF are mixed with 
40 wt%, 40 wt%, 10 wt%, and 10 wt%, respectively. A proper amount of 
NMP was added to form cathode slurry. In addition, LCO was introduced 
into the slurry, followed by grinding in an agate mortar for 1 h. Subse­
quently, the slurry was doctor-bladed onto an aluminum foil and 
vacuum-dried at 60 ◦C for 6 h. After obtaining the cathode materials, a 
solid electrolyte slurry was doctor-bladed onto the dried cathode sur­
face. Both the cathode and solid electrolyte layers were then vacuum- 
dried at 60 ◦C for 6 h. The cathode and solid electrolyte were subse­
quently cut to a diameter of 14 mm, while the lithium foil was cut to 
12 mm. Assembly of the all-solid-state Li-S coin cells took place within 
an Ar-filled glovebox (with O2 < 0.1 ppm and H2O < 0.1 ppm). 
2.6. Electrochemical measurements 
The electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and cyclic vol­
tammetry (CV) experiments were tested by using a CH Instruments 
CHI660D Electrochemical Workstation. Specifically, the scan rate of CV 
is 0.1 mV/s and the frequency of EIS is from 0.1 to 1.0 MHz Galvano­
static discharge–charge tests were conducted by a Maccor Model 4200 
Automated Test System between a voltage range of 1.5–3.0 V (vs. Li/ 
Li + ) at room temperature.

determine the optimal Ce content for enhancing ionic conductivity and 
overall electrochemical performance. The electrochemical impedance

The 10 % Ce solid cells underwent further investigation due to their 
remarkable electrochemical performance. Cycling tests for the 10 % Ce 
solid electrolyte cells were conducted at varying C rates (0.1C, 0.5C, 1C, 
and 2C) over 200 cycles at room temperature. As depicted in Fig. 2a, the 
initial specific capacities and the corresponding reversible capacities 
after 200 cycles are as follows: 1580 mAh/g and 0 mAh/g (degraded 
after 163 cycles), 1165.7 mAh/g and 335 mAh/g, 624 mAh/g and 155 
mAh/g, and 497 mAh/g and 50.4 mAh/g for the 10 % Ce cells at 0.1C, 
0.5C, 1C, and 2C, respectively. The observed rapid capacity degradation 
in the All-Solid-State Li-S cells can primarily be attributed to notable 
volumetric variations and the migration of elemental sulfur into the 
solid electrolyte matrix. This deduction is substantiated by the concur­
rence found in the SEM images depicted in Figs. 3 and 4. Specifically, the 
specific capacity of 10 % Ce sample at 0.1C is higher than at 0.5C for the 
initial 25 cycles, as expected. However, the relatively sluggish and more 
profound electrochemical reaction at 0.1C may cause more sulfur 
migration, as indicated in the EDX-mapping (Fig. 4). Subsequently, after 
25 cycles, the 0.5C exhibits higher capacity than the 0.1C. Nevertheless, 
it is evident that achieving a notable improvement in the high-rate 
performance of these All-Solid-State Li-S cells remain a crucial goal. 
Fig. 2b presents the cyclic voltammetry (CV) curve of the 10 % Ce solid- 
state cells, obtained by sweeping the voltage range from 1.5 V to 3.0 V at 
a scan rate of 0.1 mV/s. The reduction peak of the 10 % Ce cell is 
distinctly observed at 2.2 V. This single peak is postulated to be a result 
of the merging of the typical reduction peaks at 1.9 V and 2.2 V, a hy­
pothesis supported by the broad width of this peak extending from 1.8 V 
to 2.3 V. This phenomenon implies the reduction process in solid-state 
Li-S system is from S8 to Li2S, bypassing the formation of the 
intermediate lithium polysulfides which might be catalyzed by the ex­
istence of Ce element. [68,69] Conversely, the oxidation peak at 2.6 V 
indicates the reaction from Li2S back to S8. Importantly, the positions of 
these peaks closely correspond to the reduction and oxidation peaks 
observed in typical liquid electrolyte Li-S cells. [5,70,71] Furthermore, 
there is minimal alteration in the shapes (peak positions) of the CV curve 
over the course of these three cycles. Particularly noteworthy, the CV 
curves for the 2nd and 3rd cycles exhibit the same area, suggesting the 
absence of capacity decay for the first 3 cycles.[72] Fig. 2C illustrates the 
coulombic efficiency of All-Solid-State Li-S cells with 10 % Ce at various 
C rates. Overall, the coulombic efficiency of 10 % Ce at different C rates 
(0.1C, 0.5C, and 1C) demonstrates a notable consistency in 
performance.

Fig. S3a and d, comprehensive views of the entire Li metal components 
are evident. Upon disassembly of the all-solid-state Li-S cells, the lithium

Subsequently, SEM characterization was employed to examine the 
morphology of the solid electrolyte. The results unveiled a smooth and 
intact surface of the solid electrolyte before cycling. However, Fig. 3d 
illustrates the deteriorated surface of the cycled solid electrolytes. 
Overall, the SEM images show the failure modes of all-solid-state Li-S

Furthermore, the examination of the cross-sectional compositions, as 
depicted in Fig. 3a and 3b, was expanded through the utilization of 
Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) mapping. This compre­
hensive analysis unveiled a distinct migration pattern of sulfur (S8) el­
ements from the cathode side towards the solid electrolyte layer within 
the All-Solid-State Li-S system. Notably, a discernible migration of sulfur 
elements was observed towards the solid electrolyte, contributing to an 
increase in the impedance of the solid electrolytes. This migration aligns 
coherently with the impedance data presented in both Fig. 1b and 
Fig. S1, thus corroborating the observed impedance trends. Addition­
ally, the migration of the cerium (Ce) element was witnessed, further 
accentuating its role in the electrochemical reactions occurring within 
the All-Solid-State Li-S cells. The migratory behavior of Ce, in 
conjunction with the observed electrochemical performance disparities 
among solid-state Li-S cells with distinct Ce concentrations, underscores 
the multifaceted impact of this element on the solid-state Li-S cells’ 
performance. Specifically, this observation was consistent with the 
previous publication that the Ce element was utilized as catalyst and 
adsorption for polysulfides in Li-S batteries. [68,69] What’s more, an 
intriguing finding surfaces as the distribution of nickel (Ni) elements 
remains unaffected before and after cycling. Nickel has been investi­
gated in the previous publication by our group. [51] Moreover, there is 
no obvious migration of nickel element observed by the SEM-Mapping 
image in Fig. 4. Therefore, we conclude that nickel does not actively 
participate in the electrochemical reactions within the solid-state Li-S 
batteries. Concurrently, cerium (Ce) has been identified as an effective 
catalyst in enhancing the performance of lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries, 
as evidenced in references.[68,69] Based on these observations and 
supporting literature, we hypothesize that cerium functions more effi­
ciently as a catalyst for the conversion of lithium polysulfides compared 
to nickel. This intricate interaction between element migration, elec­
trochemical reactions, and compositional stability enriches our under­
standing of the underlying mechanisms at play in the solid-state Li-S 
system, thereby facilitating targeted enhancements in its overall 
performance. 
X-ray Diffraction (XRD) analysis was employed to investigate the 
crystalline characteristics of the composite solid electrolyte. Fig. 5 il­
lustrates the XRD patterns of three samples: pure Polyethylene Oxide 
(PEO), PEO-NiNbCeC/SBA-15-IL composite, and NiNbCeC/SBA-15-IL. 
The XRD profile of PEO reveals distinct peaks at 19.1◦and 23.3◦, indi­
cating a notable crystalline structure within the PEO matrix (PDF#00- 
057-1528). [34,73] In the XRD pattern of the composite solid electrolyte 
(PEO-NiNbCeC/SBA-15-IL), the peak at 23.3◦is no longer observed, and 
the intensity of the 19.1◦peak significantly diminishes. This observation 
implies that the inclusion of transition metal carbides (NiNbCeC/SBA- 
15) markedly reduces the crystallinity of PEO, thereby enhancing the 
ionic conductivity of the polymer-based composite solid electrolyte. 
[47,51] The XRD pattern of NiNbCeC/SBA-15 demonstrates prominent 
peak positions in conjunction with the composite solid electrolyte at 45◦. 
The XRD analyses substantiate that the observed decrease in Electro­
chemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS) of the composite solid electro­
lyte can be attributed to the disruption of the polymer’s crystallinity 
(PEO) caused by the presence of NiNbCeC/SBA-15.

crystallinity of the polymer matrix, resulting in enhanced ionic con­
ductivity. This disruption of crystallinity is a key factor in improving the 
performance of composite solid electrolytes. Our findings offer valuable 
insights into the design and optimization of composite solid electrolytes 
for advanced Li-S batteries. The multifaceted interactions and mecha­
nisms identified in this study contribute to a deeper understanding of the 
behavior of solid-state Li-S cells and provide a foundation for further 
research in this field. As we look toward the future of energy storage, the 
knowledge gained from this study will play a crucial role in the devel­
opment of high-performance, stable, and safe solid-state Li-S batteries, 
addressing the growing demand for efficient energy storage solutions in 
various applications.
