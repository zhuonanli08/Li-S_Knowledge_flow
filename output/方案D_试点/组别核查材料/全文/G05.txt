ABSTRACT
All-solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) with polymer electrolytes have high theoretical energy density and improved
safety, providing appealing potential for next-generation energy storage. However, limiting the dissolution of long-chain
polysulfides (LiPSs) in polymer electrolyte and achieving high S loading in cathodes remains a challenge. To tackle this issue,
we demonstrate a 3D electrode composed of a mixture of styrene butadiene rubber and lithium carboxyl methyl cellulose as
the binder, equipped with a high concentration of all-solid-state polyethylene oxide (PEO) -based electrolyte filled with LLZTO
(Li6.5La3Zr1.5Ta0.5O12) and LiPSs (named LiPS/LLZTO-CL/S). The synergistic design exhibits outstanding structural stability,
preventing the dissolution of long-chain LiPSs, enhancing the kinetics of short-chain LiPSs, alleviating volume expansion, and
offering pathways for ion/electron transport. The battery possesses attractive cycling stability and can maintain high capacity
retention (1106 mAh g−1 even after 500 cycles) at 0.2 C. Its structural and electrochemical advantages enable stable cycling at
a high sulfur loading of 7.0 mg cm−2 and 0.5 C without electrolyte in the cathode. This work proposes a novel strategy for the
structural design of high-loading polymer ASSLSBs.

Introduction
Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries with polymer solid electrolytes can
suppress uncontrollable Li dendrites, and the flammability of liq-
uid electrolytes originates from their high flexibility and reliable
safety [1, 2]. Beyond that, polymer solid electrolytes exhibit good
interfacial compatibility with electrodes compared to inorganic
solid electrolytes [3, 4]. Designing ideal polymer all-solid-state
lithium-sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) can accommodate the ever-
growing demand for advanced batteries with increased energy
storage. However, it remains difficult to limit the dissolution
of long chain polysulfides (LiPSs) in polymer electrolytes and
achieve high S loading in cathodes [5, 6].
Up to now, the hosts for sulfur routinely used in polymer ASSLSBs
are nonpolar carbon materials, which exhibit neither the capabil-
ity to effectively immobilize liquid-phase LiPSs nor the catalytic
activity to facilitate LiPSs conversion kinetics [7–9]. Particularly,
low-polarity carbon materials as hosts cannot sufficiently confine
the dissolution of LiPSs; the LiPSs could be dissolved in polyethy-
lene oxide (PEO)-based solid electrolytes during the discharging
process, which resulted in lower utilization of sulfur and deterio-
rated cycling performance of the battery. Whereas the materials
with the capability to adsorb and catalyze LiPSs typically used
in liquid-state Li-S batteries, such as metal oxides [10–12], metal
sulfides [13], heterostructures [14, 15], and metal/covalent organic
frameworks (MOFs) structures [16–18], suffer low electrical

materials hosts (including grafted EMIM+-Br−to carbon nan-

[20]) and adding fillers (including ceramic or inorganic nanopar-

in electrolyte have been explored, which contributes significantly
to suppression of shuttle effect and enhanced reaction kinetics.
However, it is crucial to note that the cathodes in these battery
systems still have low S loading. To date, even though some
polymer-based Li-S batteries have achieved a high sulfur loading
of around 5 mg cm−2, the incorporation of electrolyte in the
electrode formulation to improve ionic transport properties and
interfacial compatibility significantly increases the fabrication
cost [1, 20].
The key to preparing stable high-loading ASSLSBs lies in the
construction of the cathode and electrolyte, as well as the degree
of coordination between the cathode and electrolyte, which
is crucial for enabling the active materials of the battery to
undergo multiple and fully reversible reactions. First, selecting
a fitting binder for the sulfur-carbon cathode is instrumental
in optimizing the electrochemical performance of the battery.
Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) is currently the most widely used
binder for lithium batteries, featuring excellent electrochemical
stability and binding performance [24, 25]. However, the dis-
solution of PVDF during the preparation of electrode slurries
requires the use of N-methylpyrrolidone, a toxic organic solvent
[26]. In Li-S batteries with PEO-based electrolytes, the mixture
of PEO and LiTFSI is the most commonly used component for
electrode fabrication [27–29]. Despite its advantages in improving
ionic conductivity and interfacial compatibility, the use of a
PEO-LiTFSI composite binder in PEO-based ASSLSBs inevitably
involves toxic organic solvents and requires relatively stringent
environmental conditions during electrode fabrication. More
unfortunately, the mechanical strength of PEO and PVDF is
insufficient to withstand the severe volume change of electrodes
with high sulfur loading [30]. Hence, using elastic-structured
composite sulfur cathodes would serve to mitigate mechani-
cal breakdown caused by the intrinsic volumetric expansion
throughout the sulfur redox process [31]. Carboxymethyl cel-
lulose (CMC) integrated with styrene-butadiene rubber (SBR)
applied as a water-soluble binder in sulfur cathode enables to
form a 3D conducting network, thus contributing to stabilizing
the cathode electrode during repeated electrochemical cycles.
Specifically, the elastomeric CMC-SBR binder rendered the sulfur
electrode with a high areal loading because of its high mechanical
strength [32–35]. Nevertheless, reports on the advantages of
employing CMC-SBR in PEO-based ASSLSBs remain extremely
scarce at present. Second, adding appropriate additives into PEO-
based electrolytes not only disrupts the crystalline regions of
PEO but also effectively suppresses the loss of active material
caused by LiPSs dissolution [36]. Goodenough’s group has pre-
pared Li2S6-integrated PEO-based all-solid-state electrolytes for
lithium-metal batteries; the relevant experimental results verify
that the introduction of Li2S6 lowers the crystallinity of PEO,
thereby promoting Li+ migration [37]. In addition, when the sat-
urated LiPSs present in the PEO electrolyte of Li-S batteries may
effectively prevent further dissolution of LiPSs of the electrode.
However, it is still difficult for Sx
2−of long-chain LiPSs to separate
from Li+ and thus inhibits the conductivity [38, 39]. To construct
Li+ conduction pathways, reduce the crystallinity of PEO, and
avoid the loss of LiPSs on the cathode, the incorporation of LLZTO
(Li6.5La3Zr1.5Ta0.5O12) and LiPSs into PEO-based solid electrolytes
effectively enhances ionic conductivity and sulfur utilization.
Based on the above analysis, here we demonstrate a 3D electrode
prepared by CMC-Li/SBR (CL/S) coordinated high concentration
PEO-based electrolyte to limit the dissolution of long-chain LiPSs
in polymer electrolyte and achieve high S loading in cathodes.
Notably, electrolytes are avoided in the cathode of this work,
which not only simplifies the electrode fabrication process but
also significantly reduces material costs. Additionally, the entire
battery system can facilitate the conversion of short-chain LiPSs,
prevent the large-scale deposition of short-chain LiPSs, and alle-
viate sulfur volume expansion, thereby demonstrating excellent
recovery capability. Therefore, the resulting Li-S battery possesses
attractive performance, a high capacity retention (1106 mAh g−1
even after 500 cycles) at 0.2 C. Its structural and electrochemical
advantages enable it to maintain a specific capacity of 444 mAh
g−1 after 250 cycles at 0.5 C, even under a high sulfur loading of
7.0 mg cm−2.

Results and Discussion
2.1
Characterization of CL/S Cathodes
To evaluate the suitability of CL/S as a binder, it was mixed with
Ketjenblack and the sulfur active material to prepare the cathode.
For comparison, PVDF and PEO, which are commonly used
binders in solid-state batteries, were selected. Notably, LiTFSI
was incorporated into the PVDF and PEO binders to enable
ionic conductivity. In contrast, the CL/S cathode was fabricated
without the addition of any lithium salts or electrolytes. The
morphology of electrodes using different binders was examined
using SEM, the results can be found in Figure S1. When CL/S
is used as the composite binder, the sulfur-based composite elec-
trode exhibits a uniform and compact 3D network structure. The
conductive carbon, sulfur active materials, and solid electrolyte
particles are tightly bonded. The CL/S binder forms a contin-
uous fibrous and film-like coating on the surface of electrode
particles, constructing a robust 3D conductive and ion-transport
framework [31]. The unique 3D network formation arises from
its molecular architecture and intermolecular interactions. On
the one hand, as an ion-conducting polymer with charged car-
boxylate groups, the CMC-Li component undergoes electrostatic
repulsion in the dispersion medium, promoting chain extension
and the formation of a continuous network upon drying. On the
other hand, the elastomeric SBR provides flexibility and forms
filamentous connections between particles, creating a resilient
3D skeleton that maintains structural integrity. Therefore, the
combination of rigid CMC-Li and SBR enables the formation of
an interconnected network through physical entanglement. In
contrast, the PVDF- and PEO-based electrodes exhibit a powdery
and severely aggregated structure, implying their insufficient
ability to buffer the drastic volume variation of sulfur upon
cycling. This is because PVDF is a linear, semi-crystalline polymer
that tends to form isolated domains rather than a continuous
network, while PEO is also linear and tends to crystallize, lacking
the charged functional groups that drive network formation. It is
worth noting that there are cracks in the PVDF-electrodes and

Further research was conducted on the interaction between
binders and lithium polysulfides. For visualized adsorption tests,
the electrodes with different binders added to orange-colored
Li2S6 were incubated for 12 h, and the result is shown in Figure 1a.
In all cases, the solution color faded to varying extents, whereas
the solution containing the CL/S electrode became significantly
lighter, appearing almost colorless. Furthermore, the UV–vis
absorption spectra of the solutions were detected (Figure 1b), and
the adsorption of the Li2S6 decreased in the order: CL/S-cathodes
> PVDF-cathodes >PEO-cathodes. In addition, theoretical cal-
culations reveal that the CL/S binder has the strongest binding
energy with LiPSs, explaining the optimal ability of CL/S binder
for trapping LiPSs, which is essential for suppressing the shuttle
effect effectively (Figure 1c).
To verify the catalytic effect of binders toward LiPSs conversion,
symmetric batteries were assembled with different cathodes
(Figure 1d). The battery with CL/S binder exhibits two pairs
of redox peaks at −0.05/0.05 V and −0.57/0.57 V in the cyclic
voltammetry (CV) curve, which belong to the reversible inter-
conversions among S8 to Li2S6 and Li2S6 to Li2S, respectively.
Only a pair of redox peaks exists derived from the transformation
of S8 to Li2S6 in batteries with PVDF (−0.01/0.01 V) and PEO
(−0.17/0.17 V) binder. This indicates that, compared with PVDF
and PEO, the CL/S binder is more conducive to facilitating the
conversion of insulating Li2S. Additionally, the battery with PEO
cathode displays the lowest response current and the largest
peak potential separation; the peak values and responses of the
CL/S cathode are the largest, and the PVDF cathode shows
the smallest peak separation. This may be attributed to the
inherently stable 3D conductive network structure and catalytic
capability of the CL/S binder, which provides strong driving
forces for promoting the conversion of sulfur species. Meanwhile,
the smaller peak separation observed for the PVDF-based cathode
may originate from its superior ability to reduce Li2S6 to Li2S4.
This result demonstrated the excellent ability of CL/S binder
to catalyze the conversion of long-chain LiPSs and its superior
catalytic capability for the conversion of short-chain LiPSs [14,
15]. Li2S nucleation experiments were conducted to identify
the catalytic effect of binders during the soluble LiPSs to solid
Li2S conversion (Figure 1e). The CL/S-cathodes display a Li2S
precipitation capacity of ∼107 mAh g−1, which is significantly
higher than PVDF-cathodes (64 mAh g−1) and PEO-cathodes
(51 mAh g−1). Moreover, the responsivity of Li2S nucleation was
earlier on CL/S-cathodes (1005 s) than on PVDF-cathodes (1705
s) and PEO-cathodes (2097 s). Furthermore, potentiostatic charge
experiments were also carried out to study the dissolution kinetics
of Li2S. As shown in Figure S5, the CL/S-cathodes shows higher
oxidation current density (1.62 mA) and earlier rinsing time (439
s) than PVDF-cathodes (0.91 mA and 697 s) and on PEO-cathodes
(0.82 mA and 827 s), which indicates the best electrocatalytic
activity of CL/S toward the oxidation of Li2S. These results clearly
confirmed the rapid kinetics of promoting Li2S precipitation and
dissolution for the CL/S binder. The Gibbs free energy from S
to Li2S on binders was calculated and compared to evaluate the
electrocatalytic effect of binders (Figure 1g). All the reduction on
PEO possesses the highest reaction free energy, demonstrating
the slow sulfur redox kinetics. The reaction free energy of CL/S
is just a little higher than that of PVDF during the conversion
process of liquid LiPSs, indicating that PVDF has the greatest
ability to catalyze the conversion of liquid LiPSs, which is in
good agreement with the smallest peak separation recorded in
the CV results of the PVDF-based symmetric cell. Interestingly,
the reaction free energy for the conversion of Li2S2 to Li2S on
CL/S is only −0.59, which is much lower than that on PEO and
PVDF, indicating that the CL/S binder can lower the energy
barrier associated with this conversion, which is a key factor in
improving sulfur utilization and discharge capacity. The promis-
ing performance of CL/S toward LiPSs may originate from the
abundant hydroxyl groups on its surface [41]. These functional
groups immobilize polysulfide intermediates via ion-dipole or
Lewis acid-base interactions while facilitating fast Li+ transport.
Consequently, this synergistic effect lowers the energy barrier
for sulfur redox reactions, leading to enhanced electrochemical
performance.
2.2
Characterization of LiPS/LLZTO Electrolyte
While the cathode modification effectively provides a stable 3D
conductive structure and functions to regulate the intermediate
products, further improvements in overall cell stability and
ion transport depend largely on the properties of the solid
electrolyte. Therefore, the subsequent work focused on analyzing
the PEO-based solid electrolyte with the introduction of LiPSs
and LLZTO (Figure S6). Specifically, the incorporation of LiPSs
is intended to prevent the LiPSs generated on the cathode
from migrating into the electrolyte, which would otherwise lead
to capacity degradation. The introduction of LLZTO serves to
provide conductive pathways for Li+ transport, thus facilitating
ionic conductivity. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) was
utilized to probe the mechanism underlying the improved Li+
conductivity. PEO-electrolyte with and without LiPS, LLZTO,
and LiPS/LLZTO were measured (Figure S7a). The melting
points of the bare, LiPS, LLZTO, and LiPS/LLZTO electrolytes
are 65◦C, 58◦C, 57◦C, and 41◦C, respectively. The addition of
additives increases the concentration of electrolytes, which in
turn inhibits the crystallization of PEO. The reduced intensity
of the PEO peak in the powder diffraction pattern further
confirms the disruption of PEO crystallinity (Figure S7b). All
results show that the high-concentration electrolytes containing
LiPS and LLZTO are most effective at destroying the crys-
talline region of PEO and promoting the movement of PEO
segments.

The Li stripping and plating dynamics were studied by assem-
bling Li//Li symmetric cells containing LiPSs, LLZTO, and
LiPS/LLZTO electrolyte. As depicted in Figure 1i, at a current
density of 0.3 mA cm−2 and a capacity of 0.3 mA h cm−2,

based cell achieves superior cycling stability, maintaining stable
overpotentials and a lifespan of over 1800 h, thus surpassing
the performance enabled by the LiPS electrolyte. Moreover,
Li//Li symmetric battery with LiPS/LLZTO electrolyte maintains
a stable voltage hysteresis of 17 mV under 2000 h cycling,
which is inferior to that of LLZTO electrolyte (31 mV). The
introduction of LiPSs disrupts the crystalline structure of PEO
and develops a stable interface layer at the electrode/electrolyte
interface [37], thereby increasing its amorphous phase content
and enhancing Li+ mobility. Meanwhile, the LLZTO particles
construct inorganic pathways that further facilitate rapid Li+
transport. The synergistic effect of the two enables Li+ to migrate
swiftly through the polymer electrolyte, leading to a reduction
in overpotential. The rate performance of the Li||Li symmetric
batteries with LiPS/LLZTO electrolyte under the current density
varying from 0.1 to 0.5 mA cm−2 in Figure S10 exhibits stable
operation at different current densities, which demonstrates the
interface stability between LiPS/LLZTO electrolyte and Li foil.
The electrochemical stability windows of different batteries
assembled by various cathodes matched with various electrolytes
were characterized by linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) at 60◦C.
Figure S11 shows that the electrolytes in the LiPS/LLZTO-

meets the operating voltage range of Li-S batteries (1.7–2.8 V).
The positive shift in the oxidation onset potential and the broad-
ening of the electrochemical stability window observed for the
LiPS/LLZTO-CL/S battery can be attributed to the high oxidative
stability of LLZTO and the strong interaction between polysulfide
ions and CL/S. This enhanced oxidation resistance contributes to
improved interfacial stability during battery operation.
2.3
ASSLSBs Assembly and Testing
Electrochemical tests were conducted to demonstrate the prac-
tical application of CL/S binder and LiPS/LLZTO electrolyte
in PEO-based ASSLSBs (Figure S12). As shown in Figure 2a,
after activation at 0.1 C (1 C = 1672 mAh g−1) between 1.7
and 2.8 V, the CL/S-LiPS/LLZTO system delivered an initial
discharge capacity of 1106 mAh g−1 at 0.2 C. After 500 cycles,
the discharge capacities were 808, 412, and 305 mAh g−1 for
the LiPS/LLZTO-CL/S system, LiPS/LLZTO-PVDF system, and
LiPS/LLZTO-PEO system, which represented 73.5%, 39.2%, and
39.1% of their initial capacities, respectively. However, LLZTO-
CL/S system and LiPS-CL/S system show less than 200 cycles of
poor lifespan. Additionally, the corresponding charge–discharge
profiles of first cycle at 0.2 C reveal that the CL/S-LiPS/LLZTO
battery exhibits the smallest voltage hysteresis and the highest
Q2/Q1 ratio (The Q1 and Q2 refer to the capacity of the high and
low discharge voltage plateaus, respectively, Q2/Q1 ratio is an
index to evaluate the LiPSs redox kinetics), indicating minimal
electrochemical polarization and maximum LiPSs conversion
efficiency in contrast to the other counterparts (Figure S13).
Furthermore, after 50 cycles, the Q2/Q1 ratios of LiPS/LLZTO-
PVDF (2.75 to 1.7), LiPS/LLZTO-PEO (2.20–1.4), LLZTO-CL/S
(2.57–1.6) and LiPS-CL/S (2.61–1.1) battery decrease significantly,
while the LiPS/LLZTO-CL/S system maintains a high value of
2.3, demonstrating its superior cycling stability (Figure S14).
The rate performance of the LiPS/LLZTO-CL/S system was
further evaluated (Figure 2b; Figure S15), and the discharge
capacities of the LiPS/LLZTO-CL/S battery from 0.1 to 2.0 C
were 1438, 1011, 904, 799, and 642 mAh g−1, respectively. When
the current density was switched back from 2.0 C to 0.1 C, the
high discharge capacity was recovered to 798, 1011, 894, 989, and
1092 mAh g−1, respectively, and the performance was significantly
better than those of the LiPS/LLZTO-PVDF system, LiPS/LLZTO-
PEO system, LLZTO-CL/S system, and LiPS-CL/S system at all
tested rates. Notably, the charge–discharge profiles at various C-
rates demonstrate that the LiPS/LLZTO-PEO system maintains
observable solid-phase LiPSs conversion plateaus even at high
discharge current, evidencing its exceptional sulfur utilization
efficiency under high-rate operation (Figure S15a,b). Thus, the
battery with the LiPS/LLZTO-CL/S also demonstrated stable
cycling capacity at 1 C and 2 C (Figure 2c). It delivered an initial
capacity of 815 mAh g−1 and sustained an average coulombic
efficiency of 99.89% over 300 cycles. Even under 2 C high current,
the LiPS/LLZTO-CL/S battery exhibited a high capacity retention
of 597 mAh g−1 after 120 cycles. Besides, the LiPS/LLZTO-CL/S
battery discharged 250 cycles at 0.5 C, the sulfur loads of 1.2,
4.0, and 7.0 mg cm−2 maintain the discharge capacity of 853, 637,
and 444 mAh g−1, respectively (Figure 2d). Unlike studies that
incorporate additional electrolytes into the composite cathode to
enhance ion transport, this work introduces no extra electrolyte
into the cathode, which is beneficial for improving the overall
energy density. Although LiPS/LLZTO-CL/S exhibits a relatively
low initial specific capacity under high mass loading due to
limitations in ion transport kinetics, its specific capacity gradually
increases upon progressive cathode activation during charge–
discharge cycling, ultimately enabling stable cycling under
high‑loading conditions. Therefore, the battery system in this
work features a simpler structure and lower cost, demonstrating
promising prospects for practical applications. Among those of
ASSLSBs (Table S2; Figure 2e) reported in recent years [1, 20, 42–
46], the comprehensive performance of the LiPS/LLZTO-CL/S
battery is one of the finest. The outstanding electrochemical
behavior primarily arises from the improved ionic conductivity
and effective control of LiPSs enabled by the unique design of
LiPS/LLZTO electrolyte and CL/S electrode.

reaction polarization. The R2 in LiPS/LLZTO-PEO, LiPS/LLZTO-
PVDF first decreases and then increases; R3 shows a negligible
increase. During discharge, LiPSs generated in the cathode act
as an in situ wetting agent, where their fluid nature enables
effective wetting of the cathode surface and fills voids between
the cathode and the LiPS/LLZTO electrolyte, thereby creating
a physically intimate and chemically compatible interface with
reduced resistance. However, as LiPSs fully form, the breakdown
of the electrolyte negatively impacts the reaction. In contrast, the
R2 of LiPS/LLZTO-CL/S and LLZTO-CL/S continues to decrease,
and the R3 only has a little change. This phenomenon benefits
from the remarkable structural stability provided by the binder,
which has been verified by SEM. It also shows that the generation
of long-chain LiPSs does not hinder ion migration in the presence
of LLZTO. As stated in the previous literature, LLZTO provides
transport pathways and enhances ionic conductivity.

4
Experimental Section
4.1
Preparation of CL/S Based Cathodes
To evaluate the suitability of CL/S as a binder for cathodes,
the binder, Ketjenblack, and the sulfur active material were
mixed in a mass ratio of 1:1:8. The sulfur active material was
prepared by mixing Ketjenblack and sublimed sulfur powders at
a mass ratio of 1:4 and heating the mixture in a sealed Teflon
bottle at 155◦C for 12 h. The resulting mixture was dispersed
in water, coated onto aluminum foil, and dried to form the
cathode. For comparison, two additional cathodes were prepared
using the same procedure, with PEO/LiTFSI (mass ratio 10:1)
and PVDF/LiTFSI (mass ratio 10:1) serving as the binders,
respectively.
4.2
Preparation of Solid-State Composite
Polymer Electrolytes
A total of 200 mg LLZTO, 4 mM Li2S8, 750 mg PEO (Mn =
600 000, Aladdin, 99.9%), and 500 mg LiTFSI (Aladdin, 99%) were
added into a 9 g anhydrous acetonitrile (Aladdin, 99%) solution.
After stirring for 12 h at 60◦C, LiPS/LLZTO electrolyte solutions
were obtained. Finally, a LiPS/LLZTO electrolyte membrane was
obtained through casting the LiPS/LLZTO electrolyte solutions
into a polytetrafluoroethylene round mold with a diameter of
16 mm and drying under vacuum at 60◦C for 48 h. Specifi-
cally, adding 0.04 mL of LiPS/LLZTO electrolyte solution to
each mold will yield a LiPS/LLZTO electrolyte film with a
thickness of approximately 80 µm and an area of 2.01 cm2.
For LiPS electrolyte and LLZTO electrolyte, the other steps
are the same as the preparation of LiPS/LLZTO electrolyte,
except for not adding LLZTO and LiPS, respectively. In all of
our tests, the electrolyte membranes used were about 80 µm
thick.
4.3
Preparation of LiPS/LLZTO-CL/S Full
Batteries
The LiPS/LLZTO-CL/S full batteries were assembled in CR2032
coin cells within an Ar-filled glovebox. The batteries were
fabricated by sandwiching the polymer electrolyte membrane
between the CL/S cathode (diameter of 12 mm, area of 1.13
cm2) and a lithium metal anode (diameter of 15.6 mm, area
of 1.91 cm2). A stainless steel spacer and spring were placed
on the anode side to ensure good internal contact. Then,
the cells were sealed using a coin cell crimping machine.
