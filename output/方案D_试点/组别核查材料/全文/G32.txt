interfacial evolution in all-solid-state lithium–

With a remarkably high energy density and high safety, all-solid-state lithium–sulfur (ASSLS) batteries
have emerged as promising next-generation energy storage systems. Direct tracking of the structural
evolution at the solid–solid interfaces in an ASSLS battery is highly significant for deep understanding of
the reaction mechanism to further improve the electrochemical performance. Herein, we present in situ
monitoring of the evolution processes at both the cathode/electrolyte and anode/electrolyte interfaces
in working ASSLS batteries via real-time optical microscope (OM) imaging. An irreversible transformation
from bright-white to dark-brown in the polymer–ceramic composite electrolyte was directly captured
upon discharge/charge, which indicates a shuttling process of polysulfides in the solid-state electrolyte
further supported by XPS and Raman analyses. Furthermore, the in situ visualization of the temperature
dependency of structural evolution clearly reveals that temperature greatly influences the polysulfide
shuttling, irreversible volume-change of solid-state electrolytes and volume expansion of Li metal, which
are directly correlated with the degradation of battery performance. These results provide a deep insight
into the evolution processes of both structure and component in a working ASSLS battery, which could
guide one to explore the electrochemical reactions at solid–solid interfaces and failure mechanism to
design high performance lithium–sulfur batteries.
Broader context
All-solid-state lithium–sulfur (ASSLS) batteries have prospective potential to meet further energy-storage requirements because of their remarkably high energy
density and high safety. However, their practical application has been largely hindered by many challenges including the interfacial instability between the
solid-state electrolyte and electrode, as well as the side reactions resulting from polysulfide shuttling and electrolyte decomposition, which are highly related to
the interfacial properties. It’s thereby of importance to directly track the evolution processes via real-time imaging inside a working ASSLS battery for in-depth
understanding of the interfacial behavior and degradation mechanism. Herein, straightforward visualization of structural evolution and dynamics processes is
successfully achieved upon discharge/charge in working ASSLS batteries via real-time optical microscope imaging. Based on the in situ observation, the color
transformation of the solid-state electrolyte directly reflects the behavior of polysulfide shuttling further supported by spectroscopy, and structure-deformation
of both the electrode and electrolyte during cycling is proved to be temperature dependent. This work could potentially promote a profound understanding of
the fundamental electrochemical reactions and failure mechanisms in ASSLS batteries, which further guide one to build strategies for the design and
optimization of ASSLS batteries with superior performance.
1. Introduction
Among the various kinds of lithium–metal secondary batteries,
the lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries are considered as one of the
most appealing energy storage systems, which is ascribed to
their high energy density (2600 W h kg1) and theoretical
specific capacity (1675 mA h g1).1,2 However, their practical
application, in view of the above benefits, is limited by several
intrinsic issues resulting from the electro-insulated nature of

So far, extensive studies have been focused on seeking suitable

of batteries. Solid polymer electrolytes (SPEs) have been the most
promising in all-solid-state lithium–sulfur (ASSLS) batteries
because of their manufacturability, excellent compatibility

interfacial resistance of polymer electrolytes,23–25 could stabi-

relieve PS dissolution.26 However, the cyclability and capacity
fading of SPE-based ASSLS batteries are still far from expecta-
tions and practical application. One of the key strategies lies in
the deep understanding of interfacial and structural evolutions
during discharge/charge in ASSLS batteries, which requires
proper selection of in operando characterization techniques.27
Nevertheless, in situ and non-destructive imaging charac-
terization, which could directly visualize the morphological
evolution and dynamic process at the electrode–electrolyte inter-
faces of ASSLS, has not been reported owing to the technical
challenges. For scanning electron microscopy (SEM)28,29 and
transmission electron microscopy (TEM)30 apparatus, due to
the volatility of elemental sulfur in a high vacuum chamber,
observation of the structural transition of a sulfur electrode is
difficult. Although transmission X-ray microscopy (TXM) can
monitor the interfacial processes in the real time environment,31
it is limited for simultaneously taking both the sulfur cathode
and lithium anode into account due to their different X-ray
absorption coefficient. Additionally, for scanning probe micro-
scopy (SPM), it is very difficult to capture images in large rough
ranges for in situ observation of complicated electrochemical
processes in Li–S batteries.32 Thus, taking the advantages of
in situ optical microscope (OM) imaging into the underlying
investigation for ASSLS systems, it could be a realizable and
simplified strategy for directly tracking the structural evolution
processes to further clarify the reaction mechanism.
Herein, we focus on the structural and interfacial properties
of ASSLS batteries with polymer–ceramic composite electrolytes
(CEs) at both the cathode/electrolyte and anode/electrolyte
interfaces using in situ OM imaging from the cross-section
observation. The migration and shuttling of PSs are clearly
captured at the cathode/electrolyte interface, indicating an
irreversible Li–S reaction upon discharging/charging. Raman
and XPS spectroscopy were further utilized to analyse the
soluble sulfur species, showing that the accumulation of S4

and dynamic processes from 45 1C to 75 1C. The in situ
observations and ex situ characterizations elucidate the struc-
ture and components of ASSLS batteries, and provide a direct
insight into the performance aging mechanism of batteries,
which contributes to the optimized design of ASSLS systems.
2. Experimental
2.1.
Materials
The poly(ethylene oxide) (PEO, molecular weight of 600000 g mol1)
and lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI, purity
499.5%) and acetonitrile (purity 499.9%) were from Sigma-
Aldrich. Li6.75La3Zr1.75Ta0.25O12 (LLZTO) powders were pur-
chased from Hefei Ke Jing Materials Technology Co. Ltd and
ball milled to obtain LLZTO nanoparticles. Additionally, the
elemental sulfur, poly(vinyl difluoride) (PVDF) and N-1-methyl-
2-pyrolidone (NMP) were from Alfa-Aesar. PEO and LiTFSI were
dried under vacuum for 24 h to remove water, and other
materials were used without further purification.
2.2.
Preparation of composite electrolyte (CE) membranes
and sulfur cathode
A typical solvent-casting method was used to prepare CE
membranes. Briefly, PEO and LiTFSI (20 : 1 of molar ratio) were
dissolved in acetonitrile solvent and stirred at 60 1C for 12 h to
obtain a homogeneous solution. The LLZTO powers of 40 wt%
were then added into the solution, followed by mechanical
stirring for an additional 12 h. After that, the mixture was
coated onto the cathode electrode and dried at 60 1C for 24 h to
remove solvents. All preparation processes were performed in
an argon-filled glove box.
The composite cathode was prepared by mixing 70% sulfur
active materials, 20% Super P conductive carbon and 10%
poly(vinyl difluoride) (PVDF) binder in N-1-methyl-2-pyrolidone
(NMP). The resultant slurry was coated on a carbon-coated Al foil
and dried at 60 1C for 8 h. The active material loading is about
0.41 mg cm2.

2.3.
Fabrication of all-solid-state lithium–sulfur cells
The ASSLS battery was composed of 70% active sulfur as a
cathode, CE as a solid-state electrolyte and lithium foil (Acros
Organics, 99%) as an anode. The lithium–metal anode was
not embellished and used without any current collector. The
layered sheets of Li/CE/sulfur were assembled, and then a
ceramic knife progressively cut through the sample to obtain
a high quality cross-section. The cubic sheet (0.5 cm (length) 
0.3 cm (width) of the electrode section) pressed using a stain-
less steel and a Teflon plate was positioned in a sealed
container with an optical glass window. A 38 mm diameter seal
ring of per-fluorinated elastomers was used to seal the in situ
optical cell. Fig. S1 (ESI†) shows a schematic diagram of three-
layered sheets and photograph of an ASSLS cell. In situ optical
observations of ASSLS batteries were performed in a cross-
section view. All assemblies were performed in a glove box
filled with argon (Mikrouna, Super 1220/750, H2O o 0.1 ppm,
O2 o 0.1 ppm).
2.4.
In situ optical microscope imaging of ASSLS batteries
The in situ optical experiments were carried out using a laser
confocal microscope combined with a diﬀerential interference
contrast microscope (LCM-DIM, OLS-4000 OLYMPUS). An opti-
cal cell was assembled on the optical system coupled with a
potentiostat to study the interfacial processes in real time.
A CHI760E electrochemical workstation (Chenhua, Shanghai)
was utilized to control the potential change of cyclic voltam-
metry in the range of 1.50 V–3.50 V at a 0.5 mV s1 or 1 mV s1
sweep rate. The potential was swept towards the negative
direction from open circuit potential (OCP, ca. 2.60 V) to
1.50 V during the discharge process, and then the position
direction to 3.50 V during charging.
2.5.
Electrochemical measurements
Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) testing was
carried out using an Autolab electrochemical workstation
(Metrohm). A sandwich cell of stainless steel (SS)/CE/stainless
steel (SS) was used to measure the ionic conductivity of CEs in
the range of 0.1 to 1 000 000 Hz with an AC amplitude of 5 mV.
Additionally, the EIS of ASSLS batteries was subjected to the
frequency range from 0.1 to 200 000 Hz with an AC amplitude of
10 mV. The discharge–charge performances under different
temperature conditions were tested using 2032-type coin cells
under a current density of 0.05 mA cm2 between 1.5 V and
3.0 V using CT2001A (LANTHE Corp.).
2.6.
Material characterization
Raman spectroscopy was applied to characterize the dissolved
sulfur species in the solid-state composite electrolytes by Raman
microscopy (Thermo Scientific DXR) with a 532 nm focused
laser beam and a 50 objective (Olympus). The typical acquisi-
tion time was 120 s per spectrum. The X-ray photoelectron
spectroscopy (XPS) of the lithium electrode was measured by
an ESCALab220i-XL electron spectrometer (VG scientific) with
300 W monochromatized Al Ka radiation. X-ray diﬀraction
(XRD) patterns were collected on an Empyrean instrument
(PANalytical) in the 2y range from 101 to 801 with a step size
of 0.02431. The morphology of the CEs was examined using
scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JEOL6701F), operated at
10 kV. Atomic force microscopy (AFM, Bruker Corp., Multimode
8 with a Nanoscope V controller) in an argon-filled glove box
was employed to measure the topography and modulus images
of the electrolyte or lithium electrode surface with peak force
tapping mode using an insulating triangular silicon nitride
AFM tip (Bruker Corp., k = 26 N m1, f0 = 300 kHz). All samples
mentioned above were sealed in the corresponding container
under oxygen-free atmospheres during all ex situ measurement
processes. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) tests were carried
out from 30 1C to 800 1C at 10.0 1C min1 in a nitrogen
atmosphere (STA449F3, Netzsch, Germany).
3. Results and discussion
The polymer–ceramic composite electrolytes were prepared via
a typical solution casting method, and then utilized in ASSLS
batteries to real time monitor the interfacial processes and
further elucidate performance aging mechanisms during dis-
charging/charging. XRD patterns show that all diffraction
peaks of the as-obtained CEs are nearly well-matched with all
components containing LLZTO, PEO and LiTFSI (Fig. S2, ESI†).
TGA was introduced to evaluate the thermal stability of CE
(Fig. S3, ESI†). The PEO, PEO–LiTFSI and CE membranes are
relatively stable until 350 1C; while with the increase of tem-
perature, PEO decomposes at 355–430 1C. As the temperature
increases to 800 1C, as for the CE membrane, there is still
44.7% of initial weight, thus yielding an extraordinary thermal
tolerance of the remaining LLZTO–ceramic-filler, which suffi-
ciently ensures safety and satisfies the requirement for its
application of lithium–sulfur batteries. SEM was conducted to
characterize the detailed morphology (Fig. S4, ESI†), indicating
that the CE membranes exhibit a smooth surface and LLZTO
particles are able to well disperse in the solid-state CEs. Furthermore,
AFM topography and modulus images were shown to compare the
morphology of PEO/LiTFSI polymer electrolytes and CEs, from
which it can be clearly observed that the rigid LLZTO particles
are intermingled in the PEO matrix, hence resulting in higher
modulus of CEs according to excellent mechanical properties
(Fig. S5, ESI†). Large-scale AFM topography images further show
the PEO-chain agglomeration of a polymer electrolyte, yet CE
seldom stacks together in Fig. S6 (ESI†). The EIS of SS/CE/SS steel
cells evaluated the ionic conductivities of CE membranes from
25 1C to 80 1C in Fig. S7 (ESI†). It approaches 6.18  105 S cm1
at 25 1C and 1.37  104 S cm1 at 60 1C, respectively, which is
comparable with other PEO-based composite electrolytes in ASSLS
batteries (Table S1, ESI†).

To further characterize PS shuttling behaviors, the S2p XPS

The interfacial reaction of ASSLS batteries is thermo-
dynamically favourable owing to the higher ionic conductivity
of CEs and reductive interfacial resistance between the electrode
and electrolyte at an increased temperature. However, an opera-
tional temperature also has a significant influence on the PS
dissolution and shuttling associated with Li–S batteries. Thus, it is
quite critical to investigate the temperature dependence of inter-
facial processes and mechanisms in CE-based ASSLS batteries.
As shown in Fig. S7 and S14 (ESI†), the ionic conductivity of CE
raises and the interfacial resistance of Li–S batteries significantly
decreases with the operational temperature increasing from 45 1C
to 75 1C. Furthermore, the corresponding CV curves were
obtained at a sweep rate of 1 mV s1 in Fig. S15 (ESI†). In the
first cathodic scan, there exist two reduction peaks at all opera-
tional temperature from 45 1C to 75 1C, while it shows two
shoulder peaks at 45 1C. It is most likely associated with a poor
interfacial contact between the electrode and solid-state electro-
lyte for the initial cycle at low temperature, then from the second
cycle two distinct cathodic peaks appear with the progressive
wetting processes of the CE/electrode interface in Fig. S15a (ESI†).
These results indicate that the activity of Li–S electrochemical
reaction increases with increasing temperature.
The real time OM imaging was captured along with the
above CV measurements dependent on varied temperature as
shown in Video S2–S5 (see the ESI†). In Fig. 3, the optical cross-
section images are shown merely before cycling (OCP) and after
the 1st, 2nd and 3rd discharge–charge cycle, respectively. The
following dimensional measurement of layers is marked for a
more detailed comparison (Fig. S16, ESI†), and the corres-
ponding plots also record variations of the layer-thickness in
Fig. S17 (ESI†). As shown in Fig. 3a, at 45 1C, the thickness of
the sulfur-cathode roughly keeps constant after three cycles,
and that of flexible CE membranes shrinks by ca. 2 mm due to
the negligible expansion of lithium. When the temperature
goes up to 55 1C, the solid-state CE presents pale-yellow during
the first cycling process in Fig. 3b, at which temperature the
PS could start to dissolve into the solid-state electrolytes.
Interestingly, upon further cycling, the metallic lithium starts
to expand significantly from 60 mm to 92 mm, thus CE is
contracted under the pressure from the lithium expansion. As a
consequence, an internal short circuit could occur in the following
cycles.
In Fig. 3c, the change of CE colour is particularly visible
at 65 1C, approximately the melting point of PEO, at which
temperature PS dissolves into electrolytes. During the second
cycles, the PS dissolves simultaneously accompanied by the
expansion of CE. As shown in Fig. S16 (ESI†), the CE volume
change is negligible in the cell without active sulfur of a
cathode at 65 1C. It could be speculated that long-chain PSs
dissolve into the solid-state CEs, in which it can disorder
the electrolyte-layer. Thus, the migration and shuttling of PSs
might induce the expansion of CEs. With further cycling, the Li-
film expansion is again visible, and the corrosion rate of
lithium metal obviously increases, as expected. The swelling
lithium anode is quite detrimental to the energy density of the
batteries. At the end of the third cycle, the variable-volume CE
is extruded and removed between the two electrodes of the cell.
As the operational temperature reaches 75 1C, both the color
and volume-expansion of CE during the first cycling present
more obvious change compared with that at 65 1C. Surprisingly,

immobilizing and absorbing PSs (Fig. 5b1). When the potential

slightly disappear and LLZTO nanoparticles stack together

65 1C, as shown in Fig. S18 (ESI†). Therefore, long-chain PSs
dissolve into the solid-state CEs, and then could disorder the
electrolyte-layer, which induces the expansion of the CEs.
Meanwhile, the porous and loose morphology is visible in the
lithium interface, indicating that the corrosion rate of lithium

variable-volume CEs. Thereafter, the deformation at cathode/
electrolyte interface appears during long cycling processes in

conductivity of CEs and reducing interfacial resistance between
the electrode and electrolyte, it is very difficult to avoid PS shuttling.

correlated with the PS shuttling, the irreversible volume-change of
solid-state electrolytes and the metallic lithium expansion.
