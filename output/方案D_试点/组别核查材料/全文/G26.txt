ABSTRACT: All-solid-state lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries have emerged as one of the most promising alternative energy storage
solutions ascribed to their potentials of high energy density, cost-effectiveness, and enhanced safety. Herein, elastomeric polymer-in-
salt electrolytes (PISEs) have been developed by incorporation of highly dielectric and curable cyclic carbonate pendent groups into
the polyether backbone to fabricate sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN)/Li batteries. The PISEs with an intrinsic saturation of the
coordination sites exhibit effective inhibitions of the dissolution of lithium polysulfides and the growth of lithium dendrites and show
favorable compatibility with both the SPAN cathode and lithium metal anode. The robust LiF-rich interphases formed between the
electrodes and the PISEs are capable of effectively passivating the electrodes and accommodating their volume expansion, enabling
the all-solid-sate SPAN/PISE/Li batteries with a high specific capacity of ∼1300 mAh gsulfur
−1 and long-term cycling stability (over 4
months) at ambient temperature. This work provides a strategic framework for the design of high-performance polymer-based all-
solid-state Li−S batteries.
KEYWORDS: lithium−sulfur batteries, all-solid-state, polymer-in-salt electrolytes, lithium metal batteries, sulfurized polyacrylonitrile
1. INTRODUCTION
Li−S batteries are regarded as one of the most promising
energy storage systems due to their exceptional theoretical
capacity, ultrahigh energy density (∼2600 Wh kg−1), abundant
sulfur resources, and environmental sustainability.1−6 Despite
these advantages, the advancement of conventional liquid Li−S
batteries is impeded by the challenges from their complex
interface-related problems, continuous polysulfide shuttling,
enormous volume expansion, electrolyte depletion, and
unconstrained dendrite growth on the metal lithium anode
and associated safety concerns, etc.7−13 Replacing flammable
liquid electrolytes with compatible solid-state electrolytes to
firmly block the cathode and anode presents a highly desirable
strategy to address these issues.
Solid polymer electrolytes (SPEs) have attracted great
attention for the development of safe and high-energy-density
Li−S batteries due to their mechanical flexibility, process-
ability, and interfacial adhesiveness.14−21 For instance, their
inherent mechanical flexibility allows them to accommodate
the large volume expansion of sulfur and lithium electrodes
during charge and discharge cycles. Among SPEs, polyether-
based electrolytes are especially widely studied owing to their
good lithium salt solvation capability and compatibility with
lithium metal.22−24 However, these electrolytes often suffer
from the low lithium-ion conductivities at room temperature
and the strong coupling effect between lithium polysulfides
(LiPSs, Li2Sn, n = 4−8) and polyether chains, which can
potentially lead to LiPSs dissolution and shuttling, consequent
interfacial instability, and short cycle life in Li−S bat-
teries.18,20,25,26 To address these challenges, substantial efforts
have been made to suppress LiPSs dissolution, limit parasitic

employing various types of electrolytes such as self-healing
elastomeric electrolytes,27,28 composite polymer electro-
lytes,29,30 and polymer-in-salt electrolytes.31,32 Salt-in-polymer
is conventionally referred to the dissolution of lithium salts
into the polymer matrix with a low lithium salt content,

solid electrolyte interphase (SEI), thus contributing to the
stable cycling of the cells collectively.33−35
In Li−S batteries, covalent-type sulfurized polyacrylonitrile
(SPAN), as one of the notable sulfur composite cathodes, has
garnered increasing attention due to its solid−solid conversion
mechanism under certain conditions, particularly with
carbonate-based electrolytes.5,7,14,15,20,36 However, the dissolu-
tion of LiPSs may still exist in (poly)ether-based electrolytes
due to their strong interaction, causing rapid capacity decay
and severe self-discharge.20,37,38 However, carbonate-based
electrolytes are unstable when in contact with the lithium
metal anode.39,40 The formation of robust and durable SEI and
cathode electrolyte interphase (CEI) layers on both electrodes
is crucial to the cyclability and the Coulombic efficiency of
batteries.41−43 There emerges an urgent need to develop
optimal polymer electrolytes for solid-state Li−S batteries that
are compatible with both the SPAN cathode and the lithium
metal anode and possess a low solubility of LiPSs.
It has been reported that an increase of the concentration of
salts in small molecular liquid electrolytes may inhibit the
dissolution of LiPSs due to the formation of ionic aggregates of
low solubility.44−47 Whereas, for PISEs, the high concentration
of the lithium salts in the polymer electrolytes is anticipated to
saturate the solvating sites of the polymer segments,48,49 thus
suppressing solvation and shuttling of LiPSs during cycling. In
this work, all-solid-state polymer-based Li−S batteries have
been developed by employing the SPAN cathode and the
lithium metal anode, in combination with the polymer-in-salt
electrolytes cured in situ via the clean and efficient cyclic
carbonate−amine addition reaction. The competitive solvation
mechanism in the PISEs may essentially reduce the loss of the
active substances and alleviate the LiPSs shuttle effect, thereby
enhancing the interfacial stability and the overall performance
of the solid-state Li−S battery system. Additionally, the
formation of robust and durable LiF-rich CEI and SEI layers
derived from the PISEs can also facilitate the prevention of
LiPSs release, limit lithium dendrite growth, and accommodate
volume expansion during cycling. These effects contribute
synergistically to the improved electrochemical stability and
lifetime of the Li−S battery.
2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
2.1. Preparation of the PISEs. The PISEs were prepared in an
argon-filled glovebox (Mikrouna Super, Shanghai, H2O < 0.1 ppm, O2
< 0.1 ppm), as reported previously.48 For example, the PEOEC
polymer and LiTFSI were dissolved in dry tetrahydrofuran to yield a
homogeneous solution, followed by the addition of the cross-linker of
ethylene glycol diamine. The amount of lithium salt content in the
PISE is ∼76 wt %. The resulting solution was then evenly drop-cast
onto the lithium metal plate and first dried at room temperature and
then heated at 80 °C for 2h and further dried under high vacuum for
several hours.
2.2. Preparation of the SPAN Electrode. The SPAN cathode
was prepared by mixing SPAN powder, Super P, and PAA binder in
deionized water at a mass ratio of 8:1:1. The slurry was then coated
on the carbon-coated aluminum foil via the common blade-cast

2.3. General Characterization Methods. Fourier transform
infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was performed on a PerkinElmer
Spectrum 100 instrument. Ultraviolet−visible (UV−vis) absorption

The membrane with dimensions of 15 × 5 × 0.2 mm was obtained in
a rectangular PTFE mold. Cells were disassembled in an argon-filled
glovebox, and the lithium plates and SPAN cathodes were soaked in
anhydrous dimethyl carbonate for 3 days to remove the electrolytes.
The electrodes were transferred and sealed in an airtight box filled
with argon for analysis. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images
were acquired by using a field emission scanning electron microscope
(Nova NanoSEM 450). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)
was carried out on a Talos F200X G2 (Thermo Fisher) instrument
with an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. X-ray photoelectron
spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were performed by an AXIS
ULTRA DLD spectrometer using an Al Kα (1486.6 eV) X-ray source.
Avantage software was applied to fit the XPS peaks, and the C 1s peak
at 284.8 eV serves as the reference for calibrating the binding energy
values of the different species.
2.4. Electrochemical Measurements. Cells (2032 type) were
assembled in an argon-filled glovebox with water and oxygen contents
below 0.01 ppm. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was
conducted on a CHI 650 E electrochemical test system (Shanghai,
China). EIS measurements were performed at a frequency range from
105 to 1 Hz with a voltage amplitude of 5 mV at 30 °C. For the
assembly of SPAN/Li cells, a hollow cellulose ring spacer was applied
to control the electrolyte thickness (∼30 μm). The ionic conductivity
is calculated via the equation of σ = L/(SRb), where L represents the
thickness of the electrolyte, Rb is the bulk resistance, and S stands for
the contact area between the electrode and the electrolyte (∼1.1
cm2). The lithium-ion transference number is estimated via the
Bruce−Vincent−Evans equation: tLi+ = Iss(ΔV −I0R0)/I0(ΔV −
IssRss), where ΔV, R0, RS, I0 and Iss are the voltage polarization applied
to Li/Li cells (10 mV), the initial interfacial impedance, the steady-
state interfacial impedance, the initial current, and the steady-state
current, respectively. The cyclic voltammogram (CV) measurements
were performed at scan rates from 0.2 to 0.9 mV s−1 in a potential
range from 1 to 3 V. Electrochemical performances of the all-solid-
state SPAN/Li batteries were evaluated on the battery test system
(LAND-CT 3002 A, Wuhan, China) in an oven at 30 °C. The current
density was calculated according to the sulfur mass with a theoretical
capacity of 1675 mA gsulfur
−1.
3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
The polymer-in-salt electrolyte used in the all-solid-state
lithium−sulfur batteries was prepared via an in situ curing
process, utilizing PEOEC polymer, LiTFSI, and the ethylene
glycol diamine cross-linker as the starting materials. The
precursor solution in tetrahydrofuran was first cast onto the
lithium metal anode and dried at room temperature. The
SPAN/Li cells were then assembled, cured at 80 °C to
accomplish the cyclic carbonate−amine addition reaction, and
further dried, as illustrated in Figure 1. The aminolysis of cyclic
carbonates proceeds through the nucleophilic addition of a
primary amine and ring-opening of the cyclic carbonate group
into hydroxy urethane.48,50 Figure 1b presents the FTIR
spectra in the CO stretching region of the cured sample of
PEOEC-0.1NH2. The CO absorption bands can be
deconvoluted into four peaks, corresponding to the CO of
cyclic carbonates and the CO of urethane. The deconvo-
lution of the pristine cyclic carbonates can be justified due to
the Fermi resonance, similar to that observed in ethylene
carbonate and propylene carbonate.51 Quantitative analysis
reveals that the molar fraction of CO of urethane is
approximately 12%, agreeing well with the feed molar ratio of
NH2 to cyclic carbonate. The cured PEOEC-0.1NH2 sample
exhibits typical elastomeric behaviors of a chemically cross-
linked network, with a stress at a break of 1.04 MPa (Figures
1c and S1). As shown in Figure S2, the PISE demonstrates a
good ionic conductivity above 10−4 S cm−1 at 30 °C and a high
transference number up to 0.72 when the molar ratio between
the lithium ion and the carbonate moiety is 2:1. Notably, most
of the ether and cyclic carbonate moieties participate in the
coordination with the lithium ions at the high-salt-content
regime,48 which would be beneficial to restrain the complex-
ation and the transport of the LiPSs from the sulfur cathode,
thus inhibiting the notorious shuttle effect.
Figure 2 shows the cyclic voltammogram (CV) curves of the
solid-state SPAN/PISE/Li batteries. The CV curves were
measured at a scan rate of 0.3 mV s−1, where the oxidation and
the reduction peaks appear at approximately 2.3 and 1.7 V,
respectively. Remarkably, only one reduction peak can be
observed, indicative of the direct reduction of the elemental
sulfur to the solid-state Li2S2/Li2S.20,38,52 It is worth noting
that all of the CV curves overlap well, with negligible changes
of the peak position and the peak intensity during the
continuous ten cycles, implying a good electrochemical
stability of the SPAN/PISE/Li cell. In contrast, the SPAN/Li
cell assembled with the low-salt content PEOEC-0.2Li
electrolyte displays weak and asymmetric peaks, suggesting a
less reversible reaction (Figure S3). To further reveal the
kinetics of the redox processes in the battery, the CV profiles at
different scan rates were explored in the range of 0.2−0.9 mV

The electrolyte chemistry and its constitution can
significantly influence the electrochemical behavior of the
SPAN/Li batteries. Typically, the voltage profiles in traditional
(poly)ether-based electrolytes exhibit two distinct pla-
teaus.20,25,39 At the higher initial plateau (∼2.3 V vs Li+/Li),
long-chain LiPSs would form and dissolve in the (poly)ether-
based electrolytes, leading to shuttling of the active species
between the SPAN and the lithium metal electrodes.1,20,38
Figure 3d shows the galvanostatic voltage profiles of the all-
solid-state SPAN/PISE/Li cell at 0.1C. Only a single discharge
voltage plateau at ∼2.0 V is observed, which strongly suggests a
direct conversion between sulfur active species and Li2S2/Li2S
during the charging/discharging process without forming
intermediate long-chain LiPSs by employing this type of
polymer electrolytes in a high salt concentration. As known,
the dissolution of LiPSs would be strongly influenced by their
interaction with polymers. In the PISE, the coordination sites
are predominantly occupied by the high-content lithium
ions;48 LiPSs dissolution is expected to be inhibited
dramatically. This has been further evidenced by the Li2S6
dissolution experiments and UV−vis absorption spectra, as
described in the Supporting Information (Figures S6−S8). The
solid−solid conversion mechanism as well as the saturation of
the solvation sites of the polymer electrolytes would jointly
contribute to the stable cycling of lithium−sulfur batteries.
To unravel the interphase between the PISE and the
electrodes after cycling, EIS was conducted on SPAN/PISE/Li
cells with an open-circuit voltage of ∼2.3 V, as depicted in
Figure 5a. The lithium−sulfur cell with the PISE exhibits a low
initial interfacial resistance of approximately 120 Ω, which is
much lower than that of the low-salt content PEOEC-0.2Li
electrolyte (∼600 Ω; Figure S3b). After 25 cycles at 0.1C and
30 °C, only a slight increase of the interfacial resistance is
observed. The SEM image of the lithium metal electrode
surface reveals a uniform and smooth morphology without
noticeable lithium dendrites after 25 cycles (Figure 5b). On
the cathode side, the SPAN cathode in contact with the PISE
can still maintain a smooth and intact surface morphology after
cycling, despite flower-shaped irregularities on a larger scale
(Figure 5c). In addition, the volume changes of the SPAN
cathode during the discharging/charging process have been
observed by SEM imaging, as illustrated in Figure S9. The top-
view SEM images of the initial, the first discharged, and the
first charged SPAN cathodes show the flat and smooth surface
without noticeable gaps or cracks (Figure S9a,c) and the
changes of the SPAN particle size (Figure S9d−f) during
cycling. The cathode thickness is found to increase from
approximately 15.8 to 19.0 μm after the first discharge and
then decrease to ∼16.7 μm after the first charge (Figure S9g−
i). These results indicate that the elastomeric PISE could
effectively accommodate the volume expansion and enable the
integrity and compaction of the SPAN cathode during cycling.
The TEM image reveals the formation of a homogeneous CEI
layer in a thickness of approximately 5 nm on the surface of the
SPAN particles after cycling (Figure 5d), which should be able
to constrain the active sulfur species in the cathode and may
prevent the leakage of the LiPSs. The above observations
indicate that the lithiation/delithiation on the SPAN cathode
and lithium-ion deposition/stripping on the lithium anode

The detailed component analysis of the SEI and the CEI in
the SPAN/PISE/Li cell was performed by using XPS, as
depicted in Figures 6, S10, and S11. For the lithium metal
anode, the F 1s spectra indicate a minor organic C−F content
(16.5%) with a binding energy of 688.5 eV and a significantly
higher inorganic Li−F content (83.5%) with a binding energy
of 685.4 eV (Figure 6a,b). As previously reported, LiF is an
excellent electronic insulator with high interfacial energy and
mechanical strength, which can facilitate lithium-ion migration
through the SEI and stabilize the interphase of the lithium
anode.55−58 Our observations suggest that a LiF-rich SEI layer
forms on the surface of the cycled lithium anode, which should
be contributed mainly from the decomposition of the high-
concentrated LiTFSI. The S 2p spectrum shows clear signals
for oxidized sulfur species (SO2/SO3) in the range of 165 and
171 eV, along with the splitting peaks ascribed to the reduction
products (Li2S and Li2S2) in the range of 159 and 164 eV. The
peaks of sulfur species in high valence states are associated with
TFSI−and its decomposition products, whereas the inorganic
Li2S and Li2S2 are likely derived from the active components of
the cathode. Figure 6d presents the element ratio of the SEI,
where the signal strength of the S species is less than 0.5%,
suggesting substantial alleviation of the shuttling of the LiPSs
during cycling.
XPS analyses have also been performed to examine the
chemical composition of the SPAN cathode surface after
cycling with the PISE as the electrolyte (Figure 6e−h). As
shown in the F 1s and S 2p spectra, the signals of LiF, C−F,
SO2/SO3, Li2S2, and Li2S have been detected. A notably high
content of LiF was observed in the CEI layer, which can be
attributed to the high concentration of LiTFSI in the PISE.
Sulfur species with chemical valence between −2 and 0 appear
in the low energy range (159−165 eV) in the spectrum (Figure
6g). In addition to the residual Li2S2 and Li2S, the presence of
elemental sulfur (bridging S−S) at 163.5 eV has been detected,
as shown in the S 2p spectrum. It is worth noting that the peak
intensities of the sulfur-containing species are relatively low on
the SPAN cathode surface (Figure 6h), suggesting the
constraint of the majority of the sulfur species in the cathode
by the CEI layer, which can attenuate the XPS signals. The
above XPS results essentially imply that the robust inorganic-
rich SEI and CEI layers formed in the SPAN/PISE/Li cell by
employing the polymer electrolytes with a high-concentrated
salt may effectively alleviate the shuttle effect of the LiPSs,
accommodate the large electrode volume expansion, and
facilitate the uniform deposition of the lithium ions, thereby
enhance the cycling performance of the lithium−sulfur cells.
As reported previously, polyether-based electrolytes can lead
to severe shuttle effects and capacity degradation in the SPAN/
Li batteries due to the strong coordination between ether
segments and LiPSs during the solid−liquid phase tran-
sition.1,20,25,26 In contrast, the PISE with cyclic carbonates and
a high salt concentration can not only drastically reduce the
solubility of LiPSs but also promote the formation of stable
and robust interfacial layers. As a result, a direct transition of
sulfur to solid Li2S2/Li2S is observed, and the long-chain LiPSs
intermediates are dramatically suppressed during cycling,
which may open a promising pathway for the development
of high-energy all-solid-state lithium−sulfur batteries.

4. CONCLUSIONS
In summary, all-solid-state SPAN/PISE/Li batteries, by
employing an in situ curable polymer-in-salt electrolyte, have
been developed, and the distinct roles of the PISEs have been
unraveled in improving the interfacial compatibility, reversible
redox stability with diffusion-controlled electrode reaction
kinetics, and capability of suppressing shuttle effect and self-
discharge. The high-salt-concentration PISE exhibits superior
compatibility with both the SPAN cathode and the lithium
metal anode, promoting the formation of robust, inorganic-rich
SEI and CEI layers. The saturation of the polyelectrolyte
solvation sites in a high-salt-concentration and the solid−solid
conversion mechanism of the SPAN cathode in the PISE as
well as the durability of the interphases can synergistically
contribute to the effective mitigation of the shuttle effect of the
LiPSs intermediates generated during the cycling of the sulfur
cathode, suppression of the growth of lithium dendrites, and
accommodation of the volume expansion of the electrodes.
The lithium−sulfur batteries can achieve a high performance of
discharge capacity exceeding 1300 mAh gsulfur
−1 in all-solid-
state and stable cycling without self-discharge at ambient
temperature.
