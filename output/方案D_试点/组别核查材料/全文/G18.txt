Adsorption or Repulsion: Exploration of Regionally Confined Solid
Polymer Electrolytes for Solid-State Lithium−Sulfur Batteries
Yan Yuan,* Zeyu Li, Yaxin Kong, Jinhaochong Wang, Yan Zhao, and Hai Lu*

ABSTRACT: Dissolution and shuttle of generated intermediates polysulﬁdes
(LiPSs) are intractable challenges for lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries equipped with
solid polymer electrolytes (SPEs). Herein, several polyimide (PI) derivatives char-
acterized by the introduction of additional functional groups were synthesized and
employed as polymer matrices for fabricating regionally conﬁned SPEs. The incor-
porated amide group in the polymer matrix is found to oﬀer enhanced aﬃnity to
both Li+ and LiPSs. Despite improved ionic conductivity, the fabricated adsorp-
tion-type SPE causes severe accumulation of sulfur species in the SPE and subse-
quently partial diﬀusion toward the Li anode. In contrast, inherited sulfonic acid
group can strongly bind with Li+ through electrostatic adsorption and simulta-
neously repel Sn
2– via electrostatic repulsion. The harmonization of the cation coor-
dination property and the anion repulsion ability not only promotes Li+ migration and homogenizes Li deposition but also completely
inhibits the entry of LiPSs into the SPE, facilitating their in situ conversion and eliminating the shuttle eﬀect. Consequently, acceler-
ated reaction kinetics, increased sulfur utilization, and extended cycle lifespan are demonstrated in the Li–S cells adopting the repul-
sion-type SPE coupled with high sulfur loading cathodes.
KEYWORDS: solid polymer electrolyte, polyimide, polysulﬁde adsorption, electrostatic repulsion, lithium sulfur battery
■INTRODUCTION
Lithium–sulfur
(Li–S)
batteries
have
attracted
extensive
research interests owing to high theoretical energy density,
abundant sulfur resources, and good environmental friendli-
ness.1 However, several challenges need to be well addressed
before they are applied in practice, including the ceaseless shut-
tle eﬀect caused by soluble intermediates polysulﬁdes (LiPSs)
and uncontrollable dendrite growth on the Li metal anode,
which are responsible for irreversible loss of active species, rapid
capacity decay, and latent safety hazards of Li–S batteries.2,3 In
essence, both LiPS shuttling and dendritic Li formation are
closely associated with the electrolyte environment. In view of
the intrinsic properties of liquid electrolytes, replacing them
with a solid electrolyte is considered to be a fundamental
approach to tackle the above issues by physically/chemically
blocking dissolution/diﬀusion of LiPSs and mechanically resist-
ing the dendrite growth.4
Solid sulﬁde electrolytes (SSEs) characterized by high ionic
conductivity and superior mechanical strength have been
widely attempted in Li–S batteries.5 A key attraction lies in
the fact that the Li–S batteries with SSEs involve direct con-
version between S and Li2S without intermediate formation.6
However, large interfacial resistances between ceramic grain
boundaries and at electrolyte/electrode interfaces are quite
challenging, making the battery hard to operate without high
pressure.6 Besides, their poor stability against Li metal usually
leads to serious anode corrosion.7 Solid polymer electrolytes
(SPEs) are another promising alternatives by virtue of desir-
able ﬂexibility, good chemical stability, and convenient pro-
cessability.8
Poly(ethylene
oxide)
(PEO)
is
the
most
commonly used polymer matrix in the SPEs and is consid-
ered to be highly compatible with Li–S chemistry because
its chain segment is structurally analogous to those ether
solvent molecules.9 Nevertheless, its semi-crystalline nature
with low segmental mobility below 60 °C leads to low ionic
conductivity and makes the battery operable at higher tem-
peratures,10 which greatly restricts the application potential
of PEO-based SPEs.
Recent reports further reveal that both LiPSs and elemental S
can escape from the cathode and dissolve in the PEO due to the
high donor number (DN) of the ethylene oxide unit.11,12 This
is reﬂected by the typical two-plateau discharge behavior of
the solid-state Li–S batteries involving generated LiPSs during
conversion from sulfur to Li2S. This phenomenon can also be

Therefore, to facilitate complete sulfur conversion while the
capacity fading is delayed, LiPSs generation in the cathode is
considered to be necessary, but their movements from the cath-
ode to anode must be restricted, which necessitates the devel-
opment of regionally conﬁned SPEs.16
Based on the above concept, further enhancements of the
adsorption strength of SPEs toward generated LiPSs have been
attempted by researchers to suppress the redox shuttle, includ-
ing adding polar inorganic ﬁllers into the SPE to chemically
bond LiPSs or introducing high-aﬃnity polymer matrices to
strongly interact with sulfur species.14,17 For instance, Ji et al.
synthesized a polymer of intrinsic microporosity (PIM) which
utilized electrophilic 1,4-dicyanooxanthrene functional groups
to trap LiPSs.18 However, the adsorption-enhancing route is
doubtful since more LiPSs could enter into the SPE based on
the adsorption equilibrium theory (especially at high sulfur
loading on the cathode), accumulating due to low mobility
and continuing to diﬀuse toward the anode under a high con-
centration gradient. Therefore, it is worth further exploring
the pivotal defects of adsorption-type SPEs and developing a
more preferable SPE strategy that can impart desired properties
to practical Li–S batteries.
Polyimides (PIs) have been regarded as suitable volunteers as
polymer matrices of the SPE owing to high mechanical strength
and thermal stabilities.19,20 Moreover, active conjugated car-
bonyl groups in PI chains serve as favorable binding sites to dis-
sociate Li salt and transport Li+.20 In this work, several PI
derivatives were purposefully synthesized by the polymerization
reaction between dianhydride and diamine, followed by the
dehydration process. By adjusting diamine types, high-aﬃnity
amide groups and strong-repulsion sulfonic acid groups were
introduced into the main chains of PI derivatives, aiming at fab-
ricating two kinds of regionally conﬁned SPEs: adsorption-type
and repulsion-type. This study probes the interactions of addi-
tional functional groups with Li+ and sulfur species and investi-
gates their eﬀects on Li+ transport, LiPS conﬁnement, anode
interface stability, and cathode reaction kinetics. It focuses on
elucidating the diﬀerence in regional conﬁnement features of
two kinds of SPEs toward sulfur species and primarily empha-
sizes a feasible design concept of the polymer matrix harmoni-
zing cation solvation property and anion repulsion ability,
which is proved to be applicable for constructing advanced
solid-state Li–S batteries with upgraded performances.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Several PI derivatives were synthesized by multi-step reaction
routes illustrated in Figure 1a. First, dianhydride (e.g., DSDA)
and diamine underwent a dimerization reaction in an aprotic
solvent at room temperature, where the lone pair electrons of
amino groups in diamine attacked carbon atoms of carbonyl
groups in dianhydride to generate a dimeric monomer.21
Then, the monomer experienced a chain reaction to produce
an unstable polymer intermediate with amide (–CO–NH–)
and carboxyl (–COOH) groups. Finally, the resultant amide
group and adjacent carboxyl group in the intermediate under-
went an intramolecular dehydration reaction at high tempera-
tures, forming a stable PI derivative containing imide groups
(–CO–N–CO–).21 According to employed diamines (PPD,
DABA, and DSD), these products were referred to as PI-P,
PI-A, and PI-S, respectively. The types of diamine reactants
result in a distinct diﬀerence in the chain structures of the PI
derivatives. As shown in Figure 1b, compared to PI-P, PI-A
and PI-S are endowed with additional amide (–CO–NH–)
and sulfonic acid (–SO3H) groups, respectively, which were
directly inherited from the pristine diamine molecules.
The accurate chemical structures of as-prepared PI deriva-
tives are identiﬁed by 13C ssNMR, as shown in Figure 2a.
Note that all samples exhibit characteristic peaks corresponding
to carbonyl carbon of the imide group located at 165–167
ppm.22 For PI-A, the signal of quaternary carbon of the amide
group appears at 130.6 ppm. For PI-S, the signal of quaternary
carbon of the benzene ring substituted by –SO3H is detected at
140.5 ppm. The FTIR spectra in Figures 2b and S1 are consis-
tent with the above ssNMR results. Several PI derivatives all
exhibit stretching vibrations of C–N–C at 1720 cm−1 and
C=O at 1780 cm−1, relating to the formation of imide groups.23
Meanwhile, the characteristic peaks of C–O–C stretching
(1650 cm−1) and N–H bending (1550 cm−1) from the two
reactants (DSDA and diamines) almost disappear in these
cases, suggesting their complete reaction and polymerization.
A weak N–H peak (∼3300 cm−1) in the PI-A stands for the
incorporated amide group.24 PI-P and PI-A show vibration
peaks of the S=O bond at 1150 and 1326 cm−1.25 In contrast,

Obviously, the main diﬀerence among several PI derivatives
lies in the additional groups in the PI chain. DFT calculations
were employed for probing into the interaction rules of these
functional groups with Li+ and sulfur species (Li2S6 as an exam-
ple, Figure 2c,d). In PI-P and PI-A, the most stable conﬁgura-
tion corresponds to Li+/Li2S6 binding directly to the doubly
bonded oxygen atom, forming a strong Li–O interaction.28
However, the doubly bonded oxygen in the amide group
exhibits a stronger aﬃnity toward Li+/Li2S6 relative to that in
the imide group. It means that the incorporated amide group
bridged between two benzene rings not only beneﬁts Li salt dis-
sociation and Li+ migration but also oﬀers enhanced adsorption
strength to anchor LiPSs within the PI-A matrix.
In contrast, a much stronger interaction appears between Li+
and singly bonded oxygen atom on the sulfonic acid groups in
the PI-S. The calculated binding energy of the singly-bonded
oxygen atom with Li+ is even higher than that with pristine
H+ (Figure S2). Therefore, ion exchange easily occurs to pro-
duce a lithium sulfonate (–SO3Li) when the sulfonic acid group
is surrounded by a Li+-rich environment (e.g, PI-S was pre-
mixed with Li salt during the membrane preparation process),
which constructs additional Li+ migration channels.29 It should
be noted that the oxygen atom in the S=O bond is chemically
stable with weak aﬃnity, and thus, its interaction with Li2S6 is
negligible. Also, the formed S–O–Li bond could partially shield
the adsorption of the adjacent doubly bonded oxygen atom
toward Li2S6. As a result, a quite low binding energy (only –
0.09 eV) with a long interaction distance exists between PI-S
and Li2S6.

To experimentally conﬁrm the adsorption or repulsion eﬀect
of diﬀerent PI derivatives toward LiPSs, H-type glass cells were
employed for observing the permeability of LiPSs across the
SPEs, in which the Li2S6 solution (left side) and the
DOL/DME mixed solvent (right side) were separated by a
piece of SPE membranes. As shown in Figure 3a, Li2S6 can rap-
idly permeate through the PI-P after only 24 h due to the mod-
erate aﬃnity of the polymer matrix. In contrast, stronger-
aﬃnity PI-A obviously reduces the permeation rate of Li2S6;
however, it is still not enough to restrict LiPS movement in
the SPE in view of the color change after 72 h. It is considered
that the PI-A continuously adsorbs Li2S6 and becomes satu-
rated at a high adsorption equilibrium concentration after
48 h, and then, part of Li2S6 diﬀuses gradually to the right side,
driven by the concentration gradient. Unlike PI-P and PI-A,
LiPSs can be completely conﬁned in the left region of the PI-
S even after 72 h by virtue of electrostatic repulsion of the solid
membrane.
XPS was performed on both sides of the SPEs extracted from
the cycled Li–S cells (Figure 3b,c). In the S2p spectra, a pair of
S(VI) peaks located at both sides of the SPEs are mainly asso-
ciated with the O=S=O group in the pristine PI chains
(Figure S3).30 The additional S(IV) and Li2Sn peaks in the cath-
ode side of PI-A correspond to absorbed long-chain LiPSs, part
of which is further converted into short-chain sulﬁdes (Li2S2/
Li2S) during cycling. However, the anode side of PI-A does
not observe these sulfur species signals. This means that consid-
erable amounts of LiPSs are preserved/immobilized persistently
both on the surface and in the bulk phase of the PI-A by strong
matrix adsorption, thereby forming the distribution of concen-
tration gradient within the SPE (as depicted in Figure 3d).
Despite great restriction for sulfur diﬀusion, these active species
are kinetically sluggish in the subsequent conversion process
and even could become “dead sulfur” diﬃcult to activate revers-
ibly due to electronic isolation. Certainly, longer diﬀusion time
or a higher concentration gradient is still capable of pushing
part of adsorbed LiPSs to reach the anode, as observed in the
H-type cell. This is also conﬁrmed by the existence of deposited
sulfur species on the Li anode after 50 cycles (Figure S4), which
reﬂects the deﬁciency of the adsorption strategy in controlling
the shuttle eﬀect. By comparison, there is no sulfur species-
relating peak on both sides of the PI-S, suggesting great inhibi-
tion toward LiPSs dissolution/diﬀusion. This enables sulfur spe-
cies to be conﬁned entirely in the cathode side (instead of
within the SPE) and then in situ converted (Figure 3d).
Consequently, higher sulfur utilization and conversion kinetics
can be achieved, as discussed later.
In F1s spectra, a strong LiF peak that originated from TFSI-
decomposition appears on the anode side of PI-A.31 Also, rich
LiF species distribute on the surface of cycled Li metal equipped
with PI-A (Figure S4). It is considered that strong interactions
between the amide group and Li+ are conducive to Li salt dis-
sociation, TFSI– migration across the SPE and subsequent
decomposition at the anode side. However, the anode side of
PI-S and the cycled Li surface coupled with PI-S all present a
weak LiF signal. This reﬂects a strong anion repulsion ability
of the PI-S regardless of anion types (Sn
2– or TFSI–).
The as-prepared SPEs adopting diﬀerent PI-derived matrices
were characterized by SEM. It is noteworthy that PVDF was
introduced into the SPE to oﬀset high rigidity of these PI
chains, so as to ensure the formation of the ﬂexible membrane.
As indicated in Figure 4a–c, PI-P presents a porous and loose
morphology consisting of abundant spherical particles. In con-
trast, denser structures with smaller pores are observed in the
PI-A, probably associated with an enhanced connection
between particles owing to the hydrogen bond interaction in
the amide group. The incorporation of sulfonic acid groups
could build a more favorable hydrogen bond network, thereby
contributing to a more compact morphology with scarce pores
and uniform elemental distribution (Figure S5). This accounts
for a higher mechanical strength of the PI-S relative to other
cases, as demonstrated in Figure 4d. It should be noted that
the typical thicknesses of several SPEs are 65–67 μm deter-
mined by the cross-sectional SEM images (Figure S6).
Li+ transport properties of several SPEs were further investi-
gated. First, the Li+ transference number (tLi

evaluate Li+ contribution to the total charge in the ionic con-

method is ∼0.49, which is higher than that of PI-A (0.47) and
PI-P (0.42). Then, the EIS plots of a symmetrical stainless-
steel cell were measured to calculate the ionic conductivity of
the SPE. As shown in Figure 4h, PI-S possesses an ionic con-
ductivity of 1.11 mS cm−1 at 30 °C, which is slightly superior
to PI-A but is greatly advantageous to PI-P. Furthermore, a
comparison

reveals lower energy barriers for Li+ migration in the main
chains of PI-S and PI-A compared to PI-P (Figure 4i). These
results together demonstrate the upgraded Li+ transport capa-
bilities in the PI-S.
In fact, the fabricated PI-S can be regarded as a kind of
sulfonated polymer electrolytes due to the nature of the
sulfonated PI chain. The sulfonated matrices have been widely
employed in fuel cells and some aqueous batteries for proton
conduction (e.g., Naﬁon membrane)32 and are also capable of
conducting other alkaline metal cations such as Li+ by cation
exchange.33 Especially, the sulfonated single-ion conductor can
be formed by covalently ﬁxing the –SO3
– on the polymer back-
bone, which is capable of adequately restricting the anion migra-
tion under an electric ﬁeld and thus has a characteristic of the
cation transference number close to unit (t+= 1).34 At this
moment, the alkali metal cation coordination solely or mainly
depends on the sulfonic group with high electronegativity.
The strong cation-anion interaction usually results in diﬃcult
cation dissociation from the ﬁxed anion and hence low ionic
conductivity.34
In contrast, the PI-S system is diﬀerent from the typical
single-ion conductor. On the one hand, the salt anion (TFSI-)
coexists in the system and its migration partly contributes to
the total charge amount (despite the anion repulsion ability of
the sulfonated PI chain mentioned above), which results in
the deﬁcient tLi
+ in the PI-S. Fortunately, the electronegative
sulfonic acid group still increases the tLi
+ value to some extent.
On the other hand, the sulfonated PI chain in the PI-S can con-
duct the cation by not only the introduced sulfonic acid group
but also the intrinsic imide groups in the PI chain. The former
assists salt dissociation and Li+ migration along the polymer
matrix by strongly interacting with Li+. Meanwhile, the imide

The sulfur redox kinetics in these SPE systems were further
investigated via various electrochemical measurements. The
CV responses of the S/CNT symmetrical cells in Figure 6e
indicate that PI-S contributes to higher redox currents than
PI-P and PI-A, indicating a faster conversion rate of those
regionally conﬁned LiPSs in the cathode. The CV curves of
Li–S cells in Figure 6f illustrate that PI-S oﬀers higher
cathodic/anodic peak currents with smaller potential diﬀerence
relative to other cases, revealing superior redox reversibility. In
addition, the Li-S cell with PI-S exhibits the highest discharge
plateau capacity ratio (QL/QH) and the smallest charge/dis-
charge plateau gap (Figure 6g,h), further reﬂecting the acceler-
ated redox reaction process owing to grafted sulfonic acid
groups.
The enhanced redox reaction kinetics endows the Li–S cell
with superior rate capability. As shown in Figure 6i, the speciﬁc
capacity of the cell with PI-S reaches 1275.1 mA h g−1 at 0.1 C
(∼76.1% of the theoretical limit) and still preserves 560.9 mA h g−1
when the current density was increased to 2C, all far beyond
those with PI-A and PI-S. In most previous cases, the intrinsic
low electronic conductivity of the sulfur cathode and low ionic
conductivity of the SPE will inevitably generate high polariza-
tion at high rates for typical solid-state Li-S batteries.
However, the increased polarization at a discharge rate of 2C
in the PI-S case is acceptable (Figure S11), and the capacity
recovery is exhibited when the discharge current is back to
0.1C.

temperature and the rate was restricted in 0.1C). Furthermore,
remarkable performance diﬀerence between PI-S and PI-A
exists in the cycle test at a high sulfur loading of 4.0 mg
cm−2. As shown in Figure 6k, the adsorption-type PI-A mani-
fests a capacity limit because of considerable quantities of
active species loss by entry into the SPE under the harsh con-
ditions. Furthermore, a higher concentration gradient and
adsorption saturation can be built, pushing part of LiPSs to dif-
fuse to the anode. As a result, the shuttle eﬀect and Li corro-
sion issues are hard to be avoided utterly. In contrast, the
repulsion-type PI-S preserves a high reversible capacity of
770.5 mA h g−1 after 100 cycles and a stable Coulombic eﬃ-
ciency close to 100%, demonstrating its superiority in ade-
quately conﬁning and utilizing sulfur species.
■CONCLUSIONS
In summary, two kinds of regionally conﬁned SPEs were pre-
pared by introducing special functional groups into the PI-
derived polymer matrix. Experimental research and theoretical
calculations jointly reveal that the high-aﬃnity amide group
leads to considerable accumulation of sulfur species in the
SPE and subsequent diﬀusion toward the Li anode, indicating
limited suppression of the shuttle eﬀect. In contrast, electroneg-
ative sulfonic group can strongly coordinate Li+ while repelling
Sn
2– by electrostatic interactions. The resultant “sieving” eﬀect
completely inhibits the entry of LiPSs into the SPE, facilitating
their rapid conversion as well as promoting Li+ conduction and
stabilizing the Li surface. The designed repulsion-type SPE
endows the Li–S cell with high sulfur utilization (1267.3 mA h
g−1 at 0.2C) and excellent cycle stability (up to 300 cycles).
Even at a high sulfur loading of 4.0 mg cm−2, a high reversible
capacity of 770.5 mA h g−1 can be maintained after 100 cycles.
This work provides deep insight into the failure mechanism of
Li-S cells in strong-adsorption SPEs and proposes a preferable
design concept of the SPE, emphasizing electrostatic interaction
and harmony in cation/anion transport for developing next-
generation solid-state Li–S batteries facing practical applications.

Theoretical Calculation
Materials Studio 2020 was used to optimize the geometry structure of
each molecule at the theoretical level of Hybrid B3LYP. DFT-D disper-
sion correction was added to calculate the binding energy (ΔEb) of
each molecule with Li+ or Li2S6 by the following formula:
ΔEb = Etotal −Ep −ELi
where Etotal,Ep, and ELi are the total energy of the system, the
energy of polymer repeating unit, and the energy of Li+/Li2S6,
respectively.
The electrostatic density and electrostatics were calculated by the
DMol3 module, with the electrostatic potential map onto the isosur-
face of electrostatic density to analyze the electronic distribution char-
acteristics of the polymer repeating unit.
Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed by the

cell (AC) module was used to construct the initial electrolyte

a time step of 1.0 fs and a duration of 200 ps and in the NVT
mode with a time step of 2000 ps every 1.0 fs. The electrolyte
model after MD simulations was used for mean square displacement
(MSD) analysis.
