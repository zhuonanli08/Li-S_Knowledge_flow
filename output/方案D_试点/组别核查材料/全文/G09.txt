Strain-coordination strategy enabling long-
cycling all-solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries
Jiaxu Zhang1,2,4, Shengjie Xia1,4, Pushun Lu1,2, Suzhe Liang1,2, Jiamin Fu3,
Zhimin Zhou1, Wenlin Yan1, Guantai Hu1,2, Kaiyong Tuo1,2, Jian Hong1,
Shutao Zhang1, Ziqing Wang1, Xueliang Sun
1
& Changhong Wang

All-solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries offer high energy density and enhanced
safety. However, their practical application is hindered by high external
operating pressure to mitigate mechano-chemical failures at interfaces. Here
we show a strain-coordination strategy that leverages the opposite volume
changes of electrodes during cycling to reduce electrode-level stress evolution
and the external pressure required for stable operation. Using an FeS2 positive
electrode and a prelithiated Si negative electrode as a representative system,
we modulate the Li-to-Si ratio to achieve a near-zero-strain effect, where the
expansion of FeS2 and the contraction of Li2Si partially counterbalance each
other. This self-compensated electrode conﬁguration mitigates mechano-
electrochemical degradation under reduced pressure. As a result, the all-solid-
state lithium-sulfur batteries deliver a discharge capacity of 868.4 mAh g⁻1 at
15 MPa. Under 100 MPa, the cells achieve an areal capacity of 21.7 mAh cm⁻2,
and cycle life 4500 cycles at 1 C (60 min) and 140,000 cycles at 15 C (4 min).
Furthermore, low-pressure all-solid-state pouch cells achieve stable cycling
over 500 cycles at 15 MPa. This strain-coordination strategy provides an
approach for enabling stable operation of all-solid-state batteries under
reduced external pressure.
All-solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) are considered a
promising next-generation energy storage technology due to their
higher theoretical energy density and enhanced safety compared to
conventional lithium-ion batteries (LIBs)1,2. By replacing ﬂammable
liquid electrolytes with solid-state electrolytes (SSEs), ASSLSBs using
high-capacity lithium metal negative electrodes and sulfur-based
positive electrodes mitigate risks associated with polysulﬁde shuttle,
electrolyte leakage, and thermal runaway. Despite these advantages,
the widespread adoption of ASSLSBs remains hindered by several
critical challenges, including interfacial instability, volume-induced
mechanical degradation, and the necessity for high external pressures
to maintain efﬁcient ion transport3,4.
A major challenge of ASSLSBs is the substantial volume change
during cycling, particularly at electrode interfaces. The sulfur positive
electrode undergoes signiﬁcant expansion (~80%) upon lithiation due
to the formation of Li2S, while the lithium metal negative electrode
experiences continuous consumption and volumetric contraction,
leading to uneven interfaces and void formation5. Unlike liquid elec-
trolytes, which adapt to these volumetric changes, SSEs require inti-
mate contact between electrode components to ensure efﬁcient
lithium-ion transport. Loss of contact due to volumetric expansion and
contraction results in increased interfacial resistance, electrochemical
degradation, and premature battery failure6,7. To address these issues,
ASSLSBs are typically operated under hundreds of megapascals to

Results and Discussion
Design Principles of Self-Compensated Zero-Strain ASSLSBs
As a proof-of-concept, a high-capacity and cost-effective FeS2 positive
electrode is paired with either Li or prelithiated Si negative electrodes.
The FeS₂ positive electrode was composed of FeS2 active material,
LPSC solid electrolyte, and conductive carbon, while the negative
electrode consisted of pure Li metal or a prelithiated Si thin ﬁlm. Cell
fabrication details areprovided inthe methods section. During cycling,
the large volume change of FeS₂ generates stress within the composite
positive electrode, which is redistributed throughout the pellet and
accumulates at the electrode/electrolyte interface due to mechanical
mismatch with the dense solid electrolyte separator. By balancing the
opposite volume-change behaviors of the positive electrode and
negative electrode, the strain-coordination strategy reduces the
overall electrode-level stress evolution and stabilizes interfacial con-
tact under reduced external pressure, Figure S1. Typically, in Li | |FeS2
batteries, lithiation proceeds via two consecutive two-electron reac-
tions. Pyrite FeS2 ﬁrst transforms into Li2S and FeS, and then FeS
reduces to Fe and Li2S. Given the molarvolumes of FeS2 (24.1 cm3/mol),
Li (12.9 cm3/mol), Fe (7.1 cm3/mol), and Li2S (27.8 cm3/mol), the total
volume shrinks from 75.7 cm3/mol to 62.7 cm3/mol, a molar volume
change (ΔV) of approximately −17.2% (Fig. 1a). This signiﬁcant volume
change leads to poor solid-solid contact, impairing cycling stability
unless high external pressure is applied.

Stress Analysis of Electrode During Cycling
As different electrochemical systems require different optimal oper-
ating pressures9–11, we ﬁrst determined the optimal operation pressure
of all-solid-state Li2Si | |FeS2 batteries. After applying a fabrication
pressure of 500 MPa, we measured the electrochemical impedance
spectroscopy (EIS) of Li2Si | |FeS2 cells under various operation pres-
sures (Figure S2). Without external pressure, the cell exhibits a high
resistance of 356 Ω (Figure S2a) due to inadequate solid-solid contact.
As pressure increases to 1, 3, 5, and 10 MPa, the total resistance
decreases
signiﬁcantly, indicating
improved
solid-solid
contact.
Between 15 and 100 MPa (Figure S2b and Figure S2c), resistance
remains nearly constant, suggesting that pressure above 15 MPa
ensures stable solid-solid contact for normal battery operation. Thus,
15 MPa is chosen as the optimal operating pressure for subsequent
experiments.
To validate this strain-coordination strategy, we systematically
investigated stress variations of different electrode conﬁgurations. As
shown in Fig. 2a, Li4Ti5O12 (LTO) was used as the counter electrode due
to its minimal volume change (~0.2%) during charge and discharge12.
Thus, observed stress changes primarily originate from the working
electrode. In the LTO | |FeS2 conﬁguration (Fig. 2b), where FeS2 serves
as the positive electrode and lithiated LTO as the negative electrode, Li
ions are extracted from LTO and intercalate into FeS2 during dis-
charge, causing a nonlinear increase in net stress. To compare stress
variations across conﬁgurations, we normalized the maximum stress
change by capacity. The stress change per capacity (σc) for the FeS2
positive electrode is 0.8 MPa mAh−1. It is noted that the result change
with different SSEs, because SSEs may share mechanical load via local
amorphization, grain-boundary sliding, and time-dependent plasticity
under external pressure, thereby helping accommodate conversion-
induced strains. To conﬁrm the reliability of this result, we further
measured
σc
of
common
positive
electrodes,
including
LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 (NCM) and LiCoO2 (LCO), using the zero-strain
LTO negative electrode (Figure S3 and Figure S4). The σc of NCM is
0.213 MPa mAh−1, and the σc of LCO is −0.1558 MPa mAh−1. These
results are consistent with the large volume change (259%) of FeS2
compared to NCM ( ~ 7%) and LCO ( ~ 4%)13,14, conﬁrming the reliability
of this measurement.
Furthermore, the σc of Li-Si alloy with a different molar ratio (1:1,
2:1 or 3:1) was evaluated. As shown in Fig. 2c and Figure S5, the σc of
LiSi, Li2Si, and Li3Si are 0.71, 0.88, and 0.94 MPa mAh−1, respectively. In
contrast, the σc of Li metal is 1.03 MPa mAh−1, signiﬁcantly higher than
that of LixSi (Fig. 2d). These results are consistent with the volume
change of solution-type electrodes15. Given the similar σc values of FeS2
and Li2Si, we paired FeS2 positive electrode with a Li2Si negative
electrode. Interestingly, as shown in Fig. 2e, cell stress initially
decreases during discharge, suggesting Li2Si shrinkage exceeds FeS2
expansion. As discharge continues, stress increases, indicating FeS2
expansion dominates. Compared to the individual stress changes of
FeS2 and Li2Si electrodes, the total stress (max stress value in Fig. 2e)
variation of the Li2Si | |FeS2 cell is reduced by 80%, with a σc of
0.16 MPa mAh−1.To further conﬁrm stress matching, we applied
Lagrange interpolation. As shown in Fig. 2f, the ﬁtted stress curves of
LTO | |FeS2 (y1ﬁt) and LTO | |Li2Si (y2ﬁt) sum closely match the experi-
mental Li2Si | |FeS2 results, conﬁrming minimal overall electrode stress.
Strain Analysis of Electrode During Cycling
Comprehensive strain analysis of all-solid-state Li2Si | |FeS2 batteries
was also conducted. In-situ optical microscope was employed to
directly observe the volume change of electrodes. During discharge,
the FeS2 positive electrode layer expands while the Li2Si negative
electrode layer shrinks (Fig. 3a, b), with the opposite behavior occur-
ring during the charge process (Fig. 3c). This conﬁrms tiny changes in
the cell’s net volume (Supplementary Movie 1, Figure S6). To further
investigate volume changes during lithiation and delithiation, ex-situ
X-ray computed tomography (XCT) imaging was performed on a
2 × 2 mm region at the center of the cell (Figure S7). In the pristine
state, the positive electrode, SSE, and negative electrode exhibit
thicknesses of approximately 56 μm, 268 μm, and 28 μm, respectively
(Fig. 3d), with corresponding X-Y cross-sections shown in Figure S8.
After discharge, the positive electrode expands to ~68 μm, while the
negative electrode contracts to ~13 μm (Fig. 3e, Figure S9). Addition-
ally, thickness variations were in situ recorded under constant pressure
to analyze strain evolution during charge-discharge cycling. As shown
in Fig. 3f, the Li2Si | |FeS2 cell ﬁrst contracts during charge and then
expands during discharge, aligning with the stress behavior in Fig. 2e.
Notably, the total thickness change of the FeS2-based cell was only
~1.4 μm, even smaller than reported values for cells with NCM positive
electrodes10. These strain-related analysis conﬁrms that the self-
compensated electrode volume change enables quasi zero-strain
ASSLSBs.
Dynamic Analysis of Electrodes under Low Pressure
In-situ EIS was employed to investigate the resistance evolution of the
ASSLSBs under 15 MPa. The ﬁrst 1.5 cycles were analyzed considering
the allotropic transformation of FeS2 from cubic to orthorhombic
during the initial lithiation/delithiation process (Fig. 4a). To further
interpret the in-situ EIS results, distribution of relaxation time (DRT)
analysis was used to clearly distinguish the electrode and cell dynamic
behaviors. The relaxation peak at 10−5 ~ 10−6s is associated with particle
contact and current collectors16. Compared to Li | |FeS2 cells, the Li2Si | |
FeS2 cell exhibits a weaker peak in this region, indicating improved
particle contact (Fig. 4b, c, and Figure S21, S22). Additionally, the peaks
in the 10−3 ~ 10−2 s range that emerge at the end of charge/discharge are
attributed to the contact loss caused by the void formation in Li metal
under low pressure17. Notably, the Li2Si | |FeS2 cell exhibits fewer peaks,
suggesting better interfacial contact even under low pressure. Peaks
appearing in the 10⁻2 to 1 s range are linked to the Li diffusion at the
negative electrode/SSE interface18,19. In the Li2Si | |FeS2 cell, weaker
peaks appeared during the ﬁrst discharge, indicating faster interfacial
dynamics. In contrast, the Li | |FeS2 cell exhibits signiﬁcant peaks, likely
due to the formation of a passivation layer between Li metal and
LPSC20,21. Peaks appearing in the longer relaxation times (~10 s) is
generally associated with overall cell polarization. The Li2Si | |FeS2 cell
shows a less pronounced peak compared with the Li | |FeS2 cell, sug-
gesting reduced polarization during cycling. In-situ EIS analysis con-
vincingly demonstrates that the Li2Si | |FeS2 cell with quasi-zero change
exhibits signiﬁcantly enhanced interfacial mechano-chemical stability
and Li-ion transport kinetics.
The galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) was
further performed under low pressure to investigate the mass trans-
port dynamics in Li | |FeS2 and Li2Si | |FeS2 cells. At the initial discharge
state, Li | |FeS2 and Li2Si | |FeS2 cells display the Li-ion diffusion coefﬁ-
cient (DLi) of 4.1 × 10−12 cm2 s−1 and 4.7 × 10−12 cm2 s−1 (Fig. 4d, e). As the
discharge progresses, the Li2Si | |FeS2 cell maintains a higher DLi than
the Li | |FeS2 cell, primarily due to its more mechanically and chemically
stable interfacial contact, which is consistent with the DRT analysis.

Electrochemical Performance of ASSLSBs at low pressure
The electrochemical performance of Li | |FeS2 and Li2Si | |FeS2 cells was
evaluated under a low operation pressure of 15 MPa at 0.1 C (Fig. 5a).
The Li2Si | |FeS2 cell exhibits a high initial discharge capacity of
740.0 mAh g−1, signiﬁcantly outperforming Li | |FeS2 (599.9 mAh g−1). It
also maintains high capacity retention after 30 cycles, whereas Li | |
FeS2 retains only 25% of its capacity after 10 cycles. The superior dis-
charge capacity and cycling stability of Li2Si | |FeS2 are attributed to
enhanced interfacial solid-solid contact and the stable Li2Si/LPSC
interface. Voltage proﬁles further conﬁrm these advantages. The third
discharge curve of the Li2Si | |FeS2 exhibits a stable voltage decrease,
indicating steady ion transport. In contrast, Li | |FeS2 shows voltage
ﬂuctuations at the end of discharge (Fig. 5b), which is caused by void
formation at the Li/SSE interface. The rate performance under low
pressure shows Li2Si | |FeS2 maintaining discharge capacities of 697.0,
617.3, 564.7, 528.4, and 498.9 mAh g⁻¹ at 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4, and 0.5 C,
respectively, with a recovery capacity of 632.0 mAh g⁻¹ upon returning
to 0.1 C (Fig. 5C). In situ stress measurements at different current
densities (Figure S20) reveal a slightly reduced stress response at
higher rates, which can be attributed to the lower delivered capacity
and thus reduced lithium transport. In contrast, Li | |FeS2 shows rapid
capacity fading, delivering only 127.8 mAh g⁻¹ after returning to 0.1 C.
The high-temperature operation further enhances low-pressure
performance25,26. At 55 °C, Li2Si | |FeS2 delivers 868.4 mAh g−1 at 0.05 C
and cycles stably for over 50 cycles at 0.1 C, with capacity increasing to
807 mAh g−1 at the 50th cycle (Fig. 5d, e). This capacity increase is
attributed to LPSC reduction to Li2S by VGCF and Fe particles, enhancing
the Li2S/S conversion reaction27. To demonstrate the practicality, an all-
solid-state Li2Si | |FeS2 pouch cell was fabricated and tested under
15 MPa. The pouch cell successfully powers an LED and delivers
615.8 mAh g−1 at 0.05 C (Figure S10). At 1 C, the pouch cell achieves
442.3 mAh g−1 in the second cycle, corresponding to 619.2 Wh/kg (based
on active material, Table S6), and maintains over 500 cycles (Fig. 5F).
This low-pressure performance is competitive compared to the pre-
viously reported FeS2-based ASSLSBs (Fig. 5G). To further substantiate
the effectiveness of our design, we evaluated Li2Si | |FeS2 cells under a
moderate pressure of 30 MPa at 25 °C (Figure S17). The cell delivers a
capacity retention of 94.3% after 50 cycles. Moreover, the assembled
pouch cell operated at 30 MPa also exhibits markedly improved stability,
further conﬁrming that the Li2Si | |FeS2 conﬁguration is mechanically and
electrochemically robust under accessible pressures.
Encouraged by the excellent low-pressure performance, we fur-
ther evaluated the long-term cycling stability of Li2Si | |FeS2 ASSLSBs
under commonly reported high pressures (e.g., 100 MPa). Although
such elevated pressures are impractical for real-world applications,
they enable an assessment of the intrinsic potential of FeS₂ when
interfacial stress is properly managed. These results further highlight
the effectiveness of the strain-coordination strategy in achieving high-
performance, long-life, and cost-effective ASSLSBs at reduced pressure
levels, offering promising pathways toward scalable and sustainable
energy storage technologies. After activation, the Li2Si | |FeS2 cell deli-
vers a capacity of 411 mAh g−1 after ~4500 cycles at 1 C, corresponding
to 85% capacity retention (Fig. 6a). A comparative evaluation (Fig. 6d)
reveals that the system delivers a signiﬁcantly prolonged cycle life,
despite using commercially available micro-sized FeS2 and cost-
effective Li2Si alloys. (The phase and morphology were characterized
in Figures S11–S14, which are consistent with our previous reports28,29.)

To assess commercial viability, we further tested a high-loading

increased the FeS2 content to 55 and 60 wt% (Figure S18). Reducing the
SSE fraction leads to lower capacity at 25 °C, likely due to limited ionic
transport in the FeS2 composite electrode. This electrochemical per-
formance loss can be partially mitigated at elevated temperature
(Figure S19). Furthermore, a qualitative cost (intrinsic material cost)
estimation highlights the economic advantages of Li2Si | |FeS2 bat-
teries, particularly for grid-scale energy storage applications (Fig. 6e).
These ﬁndings establish new performance benchmarks in the ﬁeld of
ASSLSBs, paving the way for their practical deployment in next-
generation energy storage systems.
To further understand the long-term cycling stability of Li2Si | |
FeS2 ASSLSBs under low pressure, aberration-corrected scanning
transmission electron microscopy (AC-STEM) wasemployed. The high-
resolution bright ﬁeld image of FeS2 composite positive electrodes at
the initial state reveals well-deﬁned lattice fringes with a spacing of
0.31 nm, corresponding to the (111) crystal planes of pyrite FeS2
(Fig. 7a, b), indicating high crystallinity30. After 100 cycles, the dark
ﬁeld STEM image of discharged FeS2 positive electrode shows the
formation of Fe0 and Li2S (Fig. 7c, Figure S16), which aligns with the
expected reaction process (Fig. 7e)31. Atomic-resolution STEM imaging
and elemental mapping further conﬁrms the presence of pure phase
Fe0 nanoparticles, showing a strong matched Fe signal and mis-
matched S signal (Fig. 7d). These Fe0 nanoparticles are uniformly
embedded within the Li2S and LPSC matrix, facilitating a highly
reversible and efﬁcient redox process despite the insulating nature of
Li₂S, thus contributing to the excellent cycling stability of the FeS2
positive electrode. Additionally, interfacial stability between the pre-
lithiated Si negative electrode and LPSC was evaluated through reac-
tion energy calculations (Fig. 7f), showing signiﬁcantly improved
interfacial stability compared to the Li metal-LPSC interface. Taken
together, the high reversibility and stability of both the FeS2 positive
electrode and Li2Si negative electrode contribute to the superior
electrochemical performance and long-term stability of Li2Si | |FeS2
ASSLSBs under low pressure.
In this study, we present a novel strain-coordination strategy
that signiﬁcantly improves the performance and stability of
ASSLSBs, particularly under low operational pressures. By pairing a
high-capacity FeS₂ positive electrode with a prelithiated Si negative
electrode, we exploit their inverse expansion-contraction mechan-
ochemical behavior to achieve a quasi-zero strain effect during
cycling. This strategy effectively mitigates internal mechanical
stress, preserves electrode-electrolyte interfacial contact, and pro-
motes stable electrochemical behavior, even under demanding
operation conditions. The Li2Si | |FeS2 cell demonstrates exceptional
performance, including a high initial discharge capacity, remarkable
cycle life of 4,500 cycles at 1 C and 140,000 cycles at 15 C, and
outstanding rate capability, maintaining 73.8 mAh g−1 at 40 C.
Moreover, the high-loading Li2Si | |FeS2 cells exhibit an high areal

Methods
Preparation of the FeS2 electrode
The FeS2-based electrode consists of commercial micro-sized FeS2
(D50 = 14–18 μm, Macklin, 99.5%), Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 solid electrolytes
(>6 mS/cm, GLABAT), and vapor-grown carbon ﬁbers (VGCF, GLABAT)
in a 20:40:4 or 50:42:8 (wt%) ratio. These materials were placed in a
zirconia jar containing zirconia balls and milled at 300 rpm for 2 h
using a planetary ball milling apparatus. Except for ball milling, all
sample preparation procedures were carried out in an Ar-ﬁlled glove-
box (O₂ <0.1 ppm, H₂O < 0.1 ppm).
Preparation of the LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 and LiCoO2 electrode
The composite positive electrode consisted of LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2
(GLABAT) and LiCoO2 powder (GLABAT), Li3InCl6 solid electrolytes
(>1.5 mS/cm, D50 = 1-3 μm, GLABAT), and VGCF, mixed in a 70:26:4 (wt
%) ratio for 30 min using a mortar and pestle. All the process are
conducted in an Ar-ﬁlled glovebox (O2 < 0.1 ppm, H2O < 0.1 ppm).
Preparation of the Li4Ti5O12 electrode
The Li4Ti5O12 electrode consists of commercial micro-sized Li4Ti5O12
(MTI HeFei), Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 solid electrolytes, and VGCF in a 50:42:8 (wt
%) ratio. These components were placed in a zirconia jar ﬁlled with
zirconia balls and milled at 400 rpm for 2 h using a planetary ball
milling apparatus. Except for ball milling, all sample preparation pro-
cedures were carried out in an Ar-ﬁlled glovebox (O₂ <0.1 ppm,
H₂O < 0.1 ppm).

For the electrochemical tests, pellet cells were assembled using
homemade mold cells consisting of stainless-steel clamping ﬁxtures,
a polyetheretherketone (PEEK) outer sleeve, and an alumina ceramic
inner liner with a diameter of 10 mm. All assembly procedures were
carried out in an Ar-ﬁlled glovebox (O₂ <0.1 ppm, H₂O < 0.1 ppm).
First, ~80 mg of sulﬁde solid electrolyte powder was loaded into the
mold cell and pressed at 125 MPa (1 ton) for 1 min to form a dense
electrolyte pellet. Subsequently, the corresponding positive com-

electrolyte pellet and pressed at 125 MPa for 1 min. A Si ﬁlm was then
placed on the opposite side of the electrolyte pellet, followed by Li
foil on top of the Si ﬁlm. Finally, the multilayer pellet cell was formed
by pressing at 500 MPa (4 tons) for 1 min. The LiₓSi|LPSC|FeS₂ cells
were assembled using the procedure described above. For Li|LPSC|

The pouch cell was fabricated entirely via a solvent-free process.
The FeS2 positive electrode composite was prepared by mixing FeS2,
LPSC, VGCF and PTFE in a weight ratio of 50:42:8:0.5 using a Hummer
Acoustic Mixer (HAM100). The resulting mixture underwent repeated
ﬁberization and roll pressing to form a freestanding ﬁlm, which was
then transferred onto carbon-coated aluminum foil. The negative
electrode was assembled by placing a lithium foil directly onto a pre-
formed silicon ﬁlm, as described above. The solid electrolyte mem-
brane was also prepared via a solvent-free protocol described earlier in
the manuscript. Electrodes and electrolyte membranes were cut to size
using a pneumatic punching machine. The dimensions of the negative
electrode, solid electrolyte, and positive electrode sheets were
50 mm × 60 mm, 50 mm × 60 mm, and 45 mm × 56 mm, respectively.
Tab welding to the current collectors was performed using ultrasonic
welding. The cell was assembled in a stacked conﬁguration and
enclosed in a laminate pouch. After vacuum sealing, the assembled cell
was subjected to a pressure of 300 MPa for 90 s using a BTKF high-
pressure press (Bao Tou KeFa High Pressure Technology Co., Ltd). The
ﬁnal pouch cell had an area of 50 mm × 60 mm and a positive electrode
mass of approximately 112 mg (FeS₂: 56 mg, LPSC: 47.04 mg, VGCF:
8.96 mg). The LPSC separator layer weighed about 253 mg (thickness
~90 μm), and the Li₂Si alloy negative electrode weighed approximately
60 mg. All fabrication steps were conducted in an argon-ﬁlled glove-
box (H₂O < 0.1 ppm, O₂ <0.1 ppm) to prevent moisture and oxygen
exposure.
At least three independent pellet cells were tested for the elec-
trochemical performance measurement. The representative data
shown in the manuscript were selected from cells exhibiting consistent
electrochemical behavior. For mechanistic characterizations, oper-

sentative cells were assembled due to the complexity and time-
consuming nature of the experiments.

Stress measurements and thickness measurements test
Stress measurements during charge/discharge were performed using a
custom-built pressure-measuring device integrated with a force sen-
sor. The assembled cell was tested at 25 °C under an operational
pressure of 15 MPa. All stress changes during the charge and discharge
cycles were monitored using proprietary software. (CHUANGLI
Ningbo) Thickness measurements were conducted with SPFT2000
IESTTECH, and all stress changes were also tracked using the pro-
prietary software.
X-ray computed tomography (XCT) measurements
Micro X-ray computed tomography (XCT) was conducted using an
Carl Zeiss Xradia 610 & 620 Versa system. The cycled cells were dis-
assembled in an Ar-ﬁlled glovebox and encapsulated in plastic ﬁlm to
prevent exposure of the cell to air. The cells were scanned in the
central region (2 × 2 mm) to enhance resolution (~4 µm).
Phase and structure characterization
The phase composition of FeS2, μm-sized Si and Li-Si alloy with a dif-
ferent mole ratio were determined by powder X-ray Diffraction (XRD;
Rigaku). The microstructure of the samples was investigated by ﬁeld
emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM; Hitachi). The STEM
images and mapping were collected using a CEOS probe corrected FEI
Themis TEM (electron accelerating voltage is 300 kV). The interfacial
reaction energies between the negative electrodes and LPSC were
evaluated using the Materials Project database and the Interface
Reactions Calculator tools.
