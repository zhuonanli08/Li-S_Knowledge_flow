Mechanistic Investigation of Polymer-Based All-Solid-State 
Lithium/Sulfur Battery
Yang Liu, Haowen Liu, Yitao Lin, Yuxing Zhao, Hua Yuan, Yipeng Su, Jifang Zhang, 
Shuaiyang Ren, Haiyan Fan, and Yuegang Zhang*
Although employing solid polymer electrolyte (SPE) in all-solid-state lithium/
sulfur (ASSLS) batteries is a promising approach to obtain a power source 
with both high energy density and safety, the actual performance of SPE-
ASSLS batteries still lag behind conventional lithium/sulfur batteries with 
liquid ether electrolyte. In this work, combining characterization methods of 
X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, in situ optical microscopy, and three-elec-
trode measurement, a direct comparison between these two battery systems 
is made to reveal the mechanism behind their performance differences. In 
addition to polysulfides, it is found that the initial elemental sulfur can also 
dissolve into and diffuse through the SPE to reach the anode. Different from 
the shuttle effect that causes uniform corrosion on the anode in a liquid 
electrolyte, dissolved sulfur species in SPE unevenly passivate the anode sur-
face and lead to the inhomogeneous Li+ plating/stripping at the anode/SPE 
solid-solid interface. Such inhomogeneity eventually causes void formation at 
the interface, which leads to the failure of SPE-ASSLS batteries. Based on this 
understanding, a protection interlayer is designed to inhibit the shuttling of 
sulfur species, and the modified SPE-ASSLS batteries show much-improved 
performance in cycle life.

1. Introduction
With the increasing interest in the developing electric trans-
portation and bulk energy storage, batteries with high-areal 
capacity and safety are in great demand.[1] The lithium/sulfur 
(Li/S) battery is very attractive for its high specific energy den-
sity (2567 Wh kg−1), which is about three to five times higher 
than commercial Li-ion batteries.[2] However, there are several 
obstacles that hinder the widespread applications of Li/S bat-
teries, such as the shuttle effect of polysulfides and dendrites 
formation on anode.[3] These drawbacks can not only deterio-
rate the performance of Li/S battery, but lead to severe safety

In this work, a direct comparison of working/failure mech-
anisms between SPE-ASSLS batteries and LELS batteries is 
made to clarify the causes of their performance gap and to 
optimize SPE-ASSLS battery design. A cathode with discharge 
capacity of 1333 mAh g−1 is designed for easy in situ characteri-
zation by optical microscopy, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy 
(XPS), and three-electrode electrochemical measurement. Com-
pared with LELS batteries, we find not only polysulfides but 
sulfur can dissolve into PEO-based solid-state electrolyte. Dif-
ferent from the uniform corrosion on anode caused by soluble 
polysulfides in LELS batteries, dissolved sulfur species in SPE 
unevenly passivate the anode surface and lead to an inhomoge-
neous Li+ plating/stripping at anode/SPE solid–solid interface. 
This inhomogeneity accumulates with cycles and eventually 
causes significant fluctuation of overpotential and cell failure, 
which is not observed in LELS batteries. To further support this 
conclusion and solve this sulfur-anode side reaction problem, a 
freestanding interlayer is designed to protect the Li anode. The 
modified SPE-ASSLS batteries show a notable performance 
improvement in cycle life.

cathode is made by loading sulfur and solid electrolyte on 
cross-stacked super-aligned carbon nanotube (SACNT) films.

In charging process (Figure 1e), SB0 peak appears at 2.25 V, 
which indicates Li2S and Li2S2 oxidates to polysulfides. The 
ST1− and S2− peaks become smaller and finally disappear at the 
end of charging (2.8 V). The similarity between 2.8 V and OCV 
(2.5 V) states proves that the active materials are highly revers-
ible in S@SACNT-SPE cathode.
As polysulfides are important intermediates during dis-
charge, it is necessary to determine whether there exists shuttle 
effect in SPE-ASSLS batteries. Therefore, an in situ optical 
observation system was designed to characterize the mor-
phology change of active materials during discharge. The bat-
tery rested for half an hour at 60 °C before discharged under 
the rate of 0.2 C (Figure  1f). To our surprise, we observed 
shrinkage of the sulfur particle size even before discharge, indi-
cating the dissolution of sulfur into SPE at 60 °C. The Raman 
spectra in Figure S3, Supporting Information, confirmed that 
sulfur remained as S8 rings after it dissolved in SPE, which was 
similar to sulfur dissolved in CS2 solvent.[14] We made a further 
comparative study (Movie S1, Supporting Information) for two 
control samples of S@SACNT cathodes without SPE, one in 
argon at 60 °C (left panel in Movie S1 Supporting Information), 
and the other in liquid ether electrolyte at 30 °C (right panel 
in Movie S1, Supporting Information). The video indicates that 
sulfur particles in both control samples show no obvious mor-
phology change. This result indicates that, without SPE, sulfur 
can neither evaporate at 60 °C nor fully dissolve in liquid ether 
electrolyte at its working temperature of 30 °C. And, the sulfur 
dissolution occurs only in the S@SACNT-SPE sample (middle 
panel in Movie S1, Supporting Information).
Combining the chemical, electrochemical, and morphology 
evolution information of the active materials, the unique voltage 
profile characteristics of SPE-ASSLS batteries in Figure 1b can 
be explained as follows: 1) The dissolved sulfur boosts reaction 
kinetics compared with conventional bulk-type sulfur, resulting 
in a higher upper discharge plateau. 2) The migration speed of 
dissolved polysulfides in SPE is lower than that in liquid ether 
electrolyte, and they cannot return to cathode in time to be 
reduced in further discharge process, which leads to a longer 
slope after upper discharge plateau. The long discharge slope is 
also observed in Li/S batteries with high viscosity electrolyte.[15] 
Note that the dissolution and diffusion of sulfur could lead to 
a self-discharge phenomenon over time, which was recorded 
by the voltage profile in Figure S4a, Supporting Information, 
and the reduction of sulfur was affirmed by the XPS result in 
Figure S4b, Supporting Information.
To identify the electrochemical process on each electrode, 
a three-electrode cell was designed as shown in Figure 2a. To 
make the study more comprehensive, SPE-ASSLS (sample 
1), LELS (sample 2), and the SPE-based Li–Li symmetry cell 
(sample 3) were tested (Figure 2b,c). On the working electrode 
side, both the SPE-ASSLS and LELS cells show stable periodic 
voltage profiles in 10 cycles, indicating well-functioned revers-
ible working electrodes in these cells. On the counter electrode 
side, however, the overpotential of the SPE-ASSLS cell shows 
dramatic increase and severe fluctuation with increasing cycles. 
In marked contrast, the overpotential remains stable in the 
LELS cell; and the minor periodical changes, similar to those 
observed in other reports,[16] may be due to periodical reaction 
between polysulfides and Li during the discharge–charge pro-
cesses. In addition, the overpotential in the SPE-based Li–Li 
symmetry cell remains stable during all cycles, and the whole 
profile is illustrated in Figure S5, Supporting Information.

electrochemical environment in the SPE-ASSLS cell is sim-

Based on the above results, the failure mechanism of 
the SPE-ASSLS cells can be summarized in Figure  3d–f. 
Figure 3d shows the cross-section view of the Li anode-SPE 
interface before discharge. During cycling, the dissolved 
sulfur species diffuse to the anode and form a passivation 
solid electrolyte interface layer.[8b,c,12] Different from the uni-
form anode corrosion in LELS cells, the passivation layer 
in SPE-ASSLS cells is inhomogeneous due to the non-ideal 
contact of solid–solid interface, which further intensifies 
the uneven Li+ plating/stripping at the anode-SPE interface 
(Figure  3e). The accumulation of inhomogeneity reshapes 
the surface morphology of the anode and eventually causes 
large voids between the anode and SPE (Figure  3f). These 
voids directly lead to fluctuation of surface current density 
as well as overpotential, and affect the cell performance. 
Although polysulfides can also react with anode in the LELS 
cell and form porous lithium, there is no contact problem 
with liquid ether electrolyte, which makes its overpotential 
remains stable during cycling.
Based on this understanding, an interlayer composed of 
SACNT film, graphene oxide (GO), and SPE is designed to pre-
vent anode from reacting with polysulfides and prolong SPE-
ASSLS battery life, as shown in Figure 4a. GO membrane is 
widely used in LELS batteries as it contains lots of oxide func-
tional group, which is beneficial to confine polysulfides.[17] The 
freestanding SACNT-GO membrane morphology was revealed 
by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) in Figure 4b. GO 
sheets are well connected with the SACNT network, which is a 
benefit for maintaining robust structure. To guarantee good Li+ 
conduction, SPE is also added into this membrane. The SACNT-
GO-SPE (CGS) interlayer can form intimate contact with the 
SPE electrolyte, as seen in Figure S10, Supporting Information. 
To testify the function of CGS interlayer in prohibition of poly-
sulfides, a cell of polysulfides@SACNT cathode and copper foil 
anode was assembled (inset of Figure 4c). After keeping the cell 
for 24 h at 60 °C, the copper foil maintained its metallic luster 
and much less corrosion was observed than the case without 
CGS interlayer (Figure  4c and Figure S6a, Supporting Infor-
mation). Figure S11, Supporting Information, depicts the XPS 
spectra of copper foils used in cells with or without the poly-
sulfide inhibitor. The spectrum without the protection of CGS 
interlayer shows extra peaks at peak at 931.9 eV, 932.4 eV, cor-
responding to Cu2S and CuS, respectively. These results show 
that the CGS interlayer is effective in suppressing polysulfide 
diffusion.
Hybrid-SPE-ASSLS coin cells were used to evaluate the effect 
of CGS interlayer on their electrochemical performance. The 
hybrid-SPE was made by adding 20 wt% of Li6.4La3Zr1.4Ta0.6O12 
particles as nanofiller in the SPE electrolyte, and its properties 
were illustrated in Figure S12, Supporting Information. The 
galvanostatic charge and discharge were carried out at 60 °C. 
For the cell without CGS interlayer (Figure 4d), although the 
battery shows a high initial discharge capacity of 930 mAh g−1 
(6th cycle in total) at 0.2 C, it rapidly drops to 519 mAh g−1 
at 10th cycle. Then, the cell suffers from severe fluctuation in 
its discharge capacity with an average value of 633 mAh g−1  
(0.2 C). Decline and fluctuation of its coulombic efficiency are 
also observed. From 6th to 50th cycles, the average coulombic 
efficiency is 68.5% and the lowest point is only 12% at 30th 
cycle. In addition, galvanostatic voltage profiles in Figure  4e 
also depict the great overcharge and voltage fluctuation of 
the cell. This is consistent with our observation in the three-
electrode cell experiments. In the contrast, the cell with CGS 
interlayer demonstrates much stable performance as illus-
trated in Figure 4f,g. Due to the insufficient interfacial contact 
and the Li+ blocking effect in the initial CGS interlayer, the cell 
needs an activation process at 0.1 C, which is also observed in 
LELS battery with GO interlayer.[17a] After activation process, 
the battery shows an average discharge capacity of 833 mAh g−1 
at 0.2 C, and remains 799 mAh g−1 after 50 cycles. The cou-
lombic efficiency shows no obvious fluctuation and still 
approach 85% after 50 cycles. The voltage profiles also remain 
stable from 6th to 50th cycles. The comparison between these 
coin cells shows that when the polysulfide shuttle is inhibited 
by the CGS interlayer, the performance of SPE-ASSLS bat-
teries can be greatly improved.

3. Conclusion
In conclusion, this work reveals a key challenge in designing 
long-cycle-life SPE-ASSLS batteries by comparing their working 
and failure mechanism with that of LELS batteries. We find 
that, although sulfur is used as the initial active material in 
both battery systems, there exists a unique sulfur dissolution 
and diffusion behavior in SPE-ASSLS batteries. Unlike the 
LELS batteries in which the polysulfides uniformly corrode 
the lithium metal anode, the dissolved sulfur species unevenly 
passivate the anode surface and lead to the inhomogeneous 
Li+ plating/stripping at the anode/SPE solid-solid interface in 
SPE-ASSLS batteries, which causes significant overpotential 
fluctuation. We further verify this conclusion by designing a

4. Experimental Section
Preparation of SPE and Hybrid-SPE Membrane: To prepare SPE 
membrane, PEO (Aladdin, average molecular weight of 600  000) and 
LiTFSI (Aladdin, 99%) were dissolved in acetonitrile (Innochem, 99% 
H2O < 0.5 ppm) with EO/Li molar ratio of 18:1 at 60 °C for 24 h. Then, 
the obtained solution was cast on a Teflon plate by doctor blading 
method. The prepared membrane was dried in the glove box for 24 h 
and transferred to the vacuum oven at 60 °C for another 24 h. Finally, the 
freestanding SPE was able to be peeled off. Hybrid-SPE membrane was 
obtained by the same method except that 20 wt% Li6.4La3Zr1.4Ta0.6O12 
particle (Kejing) was added in the SPE solution at the first step.
Preparation of Sulfur Cathode and Li/S Batteries: S@SACNT-SPE 
cathode: Preparation process was schematically illustrated in Figure 1a. 
SACNT arrays which can directly pull out 1.5 µg cm−2 continuous SACNT 
film were reported by previous work.[18] A 10 layers cross-stacked free-
standing SACNT film was prepared on an aluminum frame as cathode 
structure. 100 mg sulfur was dissolved in 10 mL toluene (Aladdin, 99%) 
to produce a transparent sulfur solution. 100  µL sulfur solution was 
uniformly dropped on a 10 cm2 above-mentioned 10 layers SACNT film, 
and the S@SACNT cathode formed after evaporation of the solvent. 
Total sulfur loading of S@SACNT cathode is around 0.1 mg cm−2 with 
a high S/C ratio of more than 6.6:1. SPE solution was further added into 
S@SACNT cathode to prepare S@SACNT-SPE cathode.
Polysulfides@SACNT cathode: The Li2S8 catholyte (0.05 mol L−1) was 
prepared by mixing elemental sulfur (112 mg) with Li2S (23 mg) in 10 mL 
1,2-dimethoxyethane/1.3-dioxolane (DME/DOL, 1:1 by volume) solvent at 
50 °C for 24 h. 100 µL Li2S8 catholyte was added on a 10 cm2 SACNT 
substrate, and further dried in an argon-filled glove box for 12 h to get 
the polysulfides cathode. This cathode was used with copper anode to 
evaluate the reaction between polysulfides and anode.
S@C cathode: Sulfur and KJblack were mixed uniformly by ball milling 
for 10 h at the mass ratio of mS:mC = 70:30, to yield a black mixture. The 
black mixture was sealed in an air-tight container and heated at 155 °C 
for 24 h to form a sulfur-carbon composite. The cathode was prepared 
by mixing an 80 wt% sulfur-carbon composite, 10 wt% carbon black, 
and 10 wt% polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF; Alfa Aesar) in N-methyl-
2-pyrrolidone (NMP; Aldrich) to form a slurry. The resultant slurry was 
coated on a carbon-coated Al foil and dried at 55 °C for 4 h. Total sulfur 
loading of S@C cathode was around 0.5 mg cm−2, and this cathode was 
used for coin cell SPE-ASSLS batteries.
Li/S batteries assembly: The ether liquid electrolyte for LELS batteries 
was prepared by dissolving an appropriate amount of LiTFSI (1 mol L−1) 
into a DOL/DME (1:1 by volume) solvent. Lithium foil with 450  µm 
was used as anode, and the Celgard 2400 separator (LELS batteries) 
or SPE (SPE-ASSLS batteries) was employed to separate electrodes. 
Two-electrode cell was tested in 2025-type coin cells. The tested three-
electrode cells, including the SPE-ASSLS cell, LELS cell, and SPE-based 
Li-Li symmetry cell, were assembled in a Swagelok three-electrode 
type cell set-up as shown in Figure 2a. Lithium metal was used as the 
counter electrodes and reference electrodes for all these cells. The 
working electrodes of the SPE-ASSLS cell, LELS cell, and SPE-based 
Li–Li symmetry cell were S@SACNT-SPE, S@SACNT, and Li metal, 
respectively. All the procedure was processed in an argon-filled glove box.
Preparation of SACNT-GO-SPE Membrane: To prepare SACNT-GO 
membrane, 5  mg GO powder was dispersed in ethyl alcohol and 
effectively sonicated for 15 min to get a uniform suspension. A 5 layers 
cross-stacked free-standing SACNT network with the area of 50 cm2 was 
prepared on aluminum frame and then GO suspension was uniformly 
deposited on it. After evaporation of the solution, another 5 layers 
cross-stacked CNT network were covered on it to get a sandwich-
structured SACNT-GO membrane with GO loading of 0.1  mg cm−2. 
After SACNT-GO membrane was prepared, SPE solution was uniformly 
dropped on the surface of CNT-GO membrane and the solution would 
soak into this membrane. SACNT-GO-SPE membrane was prepared 
after evaporation of the solution at 60 °C for 24 h in vacuum oven.
Characterization: The morphology and structure characterization were 
carried out on an SEM (Sirion 200, FEI), a TEM (Tecnai G2F20, FEI), 
and an optical microscope (LCM-DIM, OLS OLYMPUS). XPS data were 
collected at the Catalysis and surface Science Endstation at the BL11U 
beamline in the National Synchrotron Radiation Laboratory (NSRL) in 
Hefei, China. All the electrochemical measurements were carried out 
at 60 or 30 °C within the range of 1.6–2.8 V. The galvanostatic charge–
discharge profile was collected by using a Land CT2001 automatic 
battery tester. CV was recorded on an Autolab PGSTAT204 at a scan rate 
of 0.2 mV s−1. Their electrode cell was tested on a VMP-3 electrochemical 
working station under 0.2 C.
