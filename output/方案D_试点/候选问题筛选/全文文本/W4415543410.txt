Operando Neutron Imaging of Lithium Flux and Gradient
Cathode Design for Enhanced Kinetics in High-Loading
All-Solid-State Li─S Batteries
Cheng Xu, Yuxuan Zhang, James Torres, Aleksandar S. Mijailovic, Selim Halacoglu,
Jiwei Wang, Brian W. Sheldon, and Hongli Zhu*
All-solid-state Li–sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) are considered promising
candidates for next-generation energy storage owing to their
inherent safety, high energy density, and abundant sulfur resources. However,
slow redox kinetics greatly limit sulfur utilization during solid-solid sulfur
reactions, leading to signiﬁcant challenges to achieve eﬃcient performance
in high-mass-loading ASSLSBs. Here, operando neutron image is employed
to directly visualize, for the ﬁrst time, that sluggish Li+ transport kinetics
and the uneven distribution of Li+ during cathodic reactions are critical
factors limiting sulfur conversion. To address this issue, gradient cathode
architectures comprising three and ﬁve layers are designed, in which catholyte
concentrations are strategically varied to optimize Li-ion ﬂux and enhance
ionic conductivity of the whole composite cathode electrode. Operando
neutron imaging distinctly visualizes and conﬁrms that three-layer gradient
approach signiﬁcantly enhances Li-ion mobility, resulting in more uniform
redox reactions and greatly improved sulfur utilization compared to traditional
non-gradient structures. Consequently, the three-layer gradient cathode
achieves superior rate performance and reduced electrode polarization at high
sulfur mass loadings of 4.5 and 6.0 mg cm−2. Furthermore, the applicability
and scalability of this design are demonstrated in a ﬁve-layer gradient cathode
architecture, achieving an impressive discharge speciﬁc capacity increase from
656 mAh g−1 (three-layer gradient) to 1232 mAh g−1 at 1/20 C for ultra-high
sulfur loading of 7.5 mg cm−2. This innovative gradient cathode design oﬀers
substantial advancements in understanding and overcoming Li-ion transport
limitations, paving the way toward practical, high-energy-density ASSLSBs.

To overcome this fundamental limitation, in this work, we in-
troduced a constructive gradient cathode design speciﬁcally en-
gineered to regulate Li-ion distribution and balance the charge
transport across the entire thick cathode, as represented in
Figure 1B. The key mechanism underlying this gradient design
involves creating a continuous and directional Li-ion concentra-
tion gradient within the cathode, optimizing the ionic pathway
from the solid electrolyte interface through the cathode bulk to-
ward the current collector side. By carefully adjusting catholyte
concentrations to form regions of varying ionic conductivities,
this gradient structure eﬀectively mitigates internal transport bot-
tlenecks, promotes uniform ionic ﬂux, and ensures balanced re-
dox reactions throughout the cathode layers. This precise con-
trol of Li-ion distribution signiﬁcantly enhances sulfur utiliza-
tion, as ions eﬃciently access and react uniformly with sulfur
active sites across the cathode. Consequently, redox reaction ki-
netics are accelerated, and electrodes polarization is substantially
reduced. Such advancements result in notable improvements in
rate capability and polarization reduction, addressing critical bar-
riers for practical implementation.

toward red (Trt/Tr0 < 1) reveal localized increases in Li-ion con-

leaving deeper cathode regions signiﬁcantly underutilized.
This uneven reaction distribution is fundamentally attributed
to the intrinsic limitations of Li-ion transport capacity within the
non-gradient electrode. Consequently, such transport constraints
markedly restrict the eﬀective utilization of sulfur, as evidenced
by minimal Li-ion depletion from the anode (Figure S4, Support-
ing Information) and negligible ionic exchange reactions within
the SE (Figure S5, Supporting Information). The limited trans-
port eﬀectively conﬁnes electrochemical activity to a narrow re-
gion adjacent to the interface, exacerbating the ineﬃciencies of
sulfur utilization.
To overcome the inherent challenges associated with high-
mass-loading sulfur electrodes, a strategically engineered gra-
dient cathode architecture is designed. This innovative design
utilizes a systematic concentration gradient (50 catholyte|45
catholyte|40 catholyte, named as 3 gradients) across the sulfur
cathode, with catholyte-rich layers toward the SE side, deliber-
ately creating controlled pathways that enhance Li-ion transport
and redox kinetics. The underlying mechanism of this design
capitalizes on creating a deliberate ionic concentration gradient,
establishing driving forces that direct uniform Li-ion ﬂux across
the entire electrode. Consequently, this promotes homogeneous
utilization of active materials and alleviates local polarization, sig-
niﬁcantly improving the electrochemical reaction uniformity and
eﬃciency, thus marking a substantial step forward for the practi-
cal implementation of ASSLSBs.
To further validate and elucidate this mechanism, both elec-
tronic conductivity (EC) and ionic conductivity (IC) of the cathode
electrode were evaluated. As illustrated in Figure 3A,C, two sym-
metric cell conﬁgurations employing electro-blocking and ion-
blocking structures were utilized to independently characterize
the electronic and ionic transport properties, respectively. More
detailed equations and calculation parameters are provided in
equations (1) of the Experimental Section. The gradient struc-
ture demonstrated an eﬀective EC of ≈9 mS cm−1 (Figure 3B;
Figure S6, Supporting Information), closely aligning with homo-
geneous catholytes (50, 45, and 40 catholyte). This similarity in
electronic transport arises from the constant PPCF content of
15% across the samples, aﬃrming uniform electronic pathways.
Conversely, signiﬁcant diﬀerences emerged in IC measure-
ments. As presented in Figure 3D and Figure S7 (Supporting In-
formation), IC decreases progressively with lower catholyte con-
centrations. Notably, the highest IC (0.59 mS cm−1) is observed
in 50 catholyte due to the enhanced presence of catholyte facili-
tating rapid Li-ion conduction pathways. Importantly, the 3 gra-
dients cathode exhibited an impressive 16% increase in IC (0.51
mS cm−1) relative to 45 catholyte (0.44 mS cm−1).
Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) under diﬀer-
ent DOD and states of charge (SOC) provided additional insights
into the underlying transport and interfacial kinetics facilitated
by the gradient structure. Figure 3F,I depict Nyquist plots for
both 3 gradients and non-gradient cathodes, complemented by
their respective distribution of relaxation time (DRT) analyses
(Figure 3G,J). Within these analyses, distinct resistance compo-
nents were identiﬁed: bulk resistance (R1), solid electrolyte inter-
phase layer (R2, R3), and charge transfer (R4 and R5).[26] While
bulk resistances were comparable between gradient and non-
gradient structures, reﬂecting their shared PPCF content and
similar EC values, signiﬁcant reductions were observed in charge
transfer (R4 and R5) within the batteries with gradient struc-
ture, indicating improved charge transport kinetics. The galvano-
static intermittent titration technique (GITT) further evaluated
the lithium-ion diﬀusion coeﬃcient (DLi+)[27,28] at various states
(Figure S8, Supporting Information).
Further mechanistic validation of improved sulfur redox kinet-
ics was obtained through cyclic voltammetry (CV).[29] CV anal-
yses (Figure S9, Supporting Information) revealed distinct ca-
thodic (C′-C″) and anodic peaks (A′-A″) corresponding to sulfur
reduction and oxidation under scanning rates from 0.05 to 1 mV
s−1, respectively. The gradient structure consistently exhibited en-
larged redox peaks compared to the non-gradient counterpart,
conﬁrming superior sulfur utilization and conversion kinetics.

3. Conclusion
The sluggish reaction kinetics inherent to high-mass-loading all-
solid-state Li-sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) critically limit sulfur uti-
lization. Operando neutron imaging reveals that these slow re-
action kinetics stem from limited Li-ion transport and uneven
distribution within the sulfur cathode. To overcome these fun-
damental kinetic challenges, we engineered innovative three-
layer and ﬁve-layer gradient cathode architectures. This strategic
three-layer gradient structure enhanced ionic conductivity (IC)
by nearly 16%, from 0.44 mS cm−1 in conventional non-gradient
cathodes to 0.51 mS cm−1. In the ﬁve-layer gradients, the IC fur-
ther increased to 0.57 mS cm−1, representing an improvement
of up to 30%. The catholyte-rich layers particularly excelled at
promoting uniform Li-ion distribution throughout the cathode,
dramatically improving sulfur reaction kinetics. Operando neu-
tron imaging conclusively demonstrated that our gradient design
achieved signiﬁcantly more homogeneous Li-ion ﬂux and sulfur
utilization patterns, directly translating to superior electrochem-
ical performance at challenging sulfur mass loadings. The three-
layer gradient cathode exhibited exceptional rate capability and
reduced polarization at high loadings of 4.5 and 6.0 mg cm−2.
The ﬁve-layer gradient cathode accommodated an ultra-high sul-
fur loading of 7.5 mg cm−2, delivering an outstanding discharge
capacity of 1232 mAh g−1 at 1/20 C—nearly double the capac-
ity (656 mAh g−1) achieved by the three-layer design. This work
deﬁnitively establishes gradient-based structural engineering as
a transformative approach to addressing the intrinsic kinetic
limitations in high-mass-loading sulfur cathodes, opening

4. Experimental Section
Synthesis
of
Li5.4PS4.4Cl1.6:
The
sulﬁde
solid
electrolyte
(SE),
Li5.4PS4.4Cl1.6, was synthesized using a conventional ball milling and
annealing method. 1.310 g lithium sulﬁde (Li2S, 99.98%, Sigma-Aldrich),
1.665 g phosphorus pentasulﬁde (P2S5, 99%, Sigma–Aldrich), and 1.017
g lithium chloride (LiCl, >99.98%, Sigma-Aldrich) were mixed within
a stainless-steel jar under a protective argon atmosphere. Ball milling
was conducted with a 15-min milling/resting procedure for 40 cycles at
a rotating speed of 500 rpm. The weight ratio between ZrO2 balls and
precursors was 50:1. Following ball milling, the mixture was sealed in a
glass tube and annealed at 510 °C for 2 h, and Li5.4PS4.4Cl1.6 was then
obtained.
Preparation of Polyacrylonitrile-Based Porous Carbon Fibers (PPCF):
The
synthesis of PPCF involved multiple steps,[19] starting with the dissolu-
tion of 15 wt.% polyacrylonitrile (PAN, MW 15 K g mol−1, Sigma–Aldrich)
in N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.9%, Fisher Scientiﬁc) to obtain a
homogeneous solution. Subsequently, nanoﬁber mesh was obtained in a
nanoﬁber electrospinning unit with an applied voltage of 15 kV. The ﬁbers
were then thermostabilized at 250 °C and carbonized at 1000 °C to ob-
tain carbon nanoﬁbers (CNs). CNs were activated by potassium hydrox-
ide (KOH, 85%, Fisher Scientiﬁc) at 1000 °C to create porous structures.
PPCF was collected after undergoing dialysis and freeze-drying.
Preparation of Sulfur Cathodes (x Catholyte):
The cathode materi-
als were prepared via mixing catholyte (Li5.4PS4.4Cl1.6), sulfur (99.98%,
Sigma) and PPCF with weight ratios of x:(85-x):15 (x = 55, 50, 45, 40, 35,
labeled as x catholyte) through ball milling and melting-inﬁltration meth-
ods. The mixture together with ZrO2 balls in a weight ratio of 1:20 were
sealed in a stainless-steel jar under a protective argon atmosphere milling
at 400 rpm for 10 h. Next, the mixture was sealed within a glass tube and
heated at 155 °C for 12 h, and x catholyte were collected.
Fabrication of All-Solid-State Li─S batteries (ASSLSBs):
All cells were
assembled inside the Ar-ﬁlled glovebox (O2 < 0.1 ppm, H2O < 0.1 ppm).
First, 126 mg SE powders were pressed under 20 MPa to form a pellet
with a diameter of 12.8 mm. Then, x catholyte cathode materials together
with aluminum current collector were applied on one side and pressed
at 400 MPa for 3-5 min. Finally, a piece of Li/In foil as the anode with
copper current collector was attached to the other side at 50 MPa (testing
pressure). The sulfur content of x catholyte and gradient was 40%. The
mass loadings of sulfur were 4.5, 6, and 7.5 mg cm−2.
Electrochemical Performance and Characterization:
All ASSLSBs were
tested at room temperature (24–26 °C) via an electrochemical station (Bio-
Logic SP-150). The rate capabilities were performed with a potential lim-
itation protocol between the cutoﬀvoltages of 0.7 and 2.7 V (vs. Li/In),
unless otherwise speciﬁed. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was conducted be-
tween 0.7 and 2.4 V (vs. Li/In) with diﬀerent sweep rates from 0.05 to 1 mV
s−1. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed with
an amplitude of 10 mV in the frequency range of 1 MHz to 100 mHz. The
distribution of relaxation time (DRT) was analyzed based on EIS results
by Bayesian calculations in MATLAB. GITT was carried out at 1/20 C with
1 h current pulse followed by 6 h relaxation period. TGA was conducted
under a nitrogen atmosphere from 25 to 600 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C
min−1. SEM morphologies of the cathodes and energy-dispersive X-ray
spectroscopy (EDS) analyses were obtained on a Hitachi S4800 scanning
electron microscope operated at an accelerating voltage of 3 kV.
Electronic conductivities (EC) of non-gradient and gradient cathodes
were measured using an ion-blocking symmetric cell (Figure 2a).[33] 60
mg x catholyte or gradient designed materials were pressed under 400
MPa for 3–5 min to form a pellet. A piece of aluminum foil was applied to
each side of the pellet. The cells were tested with the applied potentials at
10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 mV for 15 min in total.
Ionic conductivities (IC) were performed using an electron-blocking
symmetric cell (Figure 2c).[33] First, 45 mg x catholyte or gradient designed
cathode materials were pressed into a pellet under 20 MPa for one min.
Then, 90 mg SE was spread uniformly on both sides of cathode pellet un-
der 400 Mpa for 3–5 min. After that, Li/In foil and Cu foil were attached
on both sides of the above pellet. The cells were tested with an amplitude
of 10 mV in the frequency range of 1 MHz to 100 mHz.

Operando Neutron Imaging:
The operando neutron imaging was taken
on the Multimodal Advanced Radiography Station (MARS), HFIR beam-
line CG-1D, at Oak Ridge National Laboratory. The detector-to-pinhole dis-
tance was 6.59 m, and the pinhole diameter was 11 mm. The neutron cells
used for operando neutron imaging were assembled within a transparent
quartz tube in the Ar-ﬁlled glovebox (O2 < 0.1 ppm, H2O < 0.1 ppm). 15
mg of SE were ﬁrst prepressed to form a pellet with a diameter of 4 mm un-
der 20 MPa within the quartz tube. Then, the sulfur cathode powders were
spread uniformly on one side of SE pellet with a pressure of 200 MPa. Next,
one piece of In and 6Li foil (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, Inc., 95%)
were sequentially attached to the other side of the SE pellet. Aluminum
and copper foil were used as the current collector at the cathode and an-
ode side, respectively. Finally, a testing pressure of 10 MPa was applied on
the neutron cell for the neutron imaging. All gaps on the cell were further
sealed by hot melted glue. For the operando neutron imaging collecting,
the cell was placed in front of a scientiﬁc Complementary Metal-Oxide-
Semiconductor (sCMOS) camera system (QHY600 PRO, Light Speed Vi-
sion (Beijing) Co., Ltd., (QHYCCD)) with a 20 μm thick scintillator screen
with Gd2O2S:Tb and 6LiF mixed as 4:1 by weight. With a lens magniﬁca-
tion of ≈0.5x, the eﬀective pixel size was 7.75 μm, corresponding to a
spatial resolution of 25 to 30 μm, including the geometric blurring eﬀect.
The standard deviation was 2.9% and 1.6% between sulfur cathode and
air area from neutron transmission and neutron transmission ratio im-
ages, respectively. The exposure time for each image was 5 min under the
neutron beam with a wavelength range from 0.8 to 6 Å and a peak ﬂux of
2.2 × 106 n cm−2 s−1 at 2.6 Å. Open beam and dark current images were
collected before and after the operando neutron imaging measurement.
The ambient temperature was around 20 °C. The neutron imaging Jupyter
Notebook developed by ORNL,[35] Fiji-image and ImageJ software were
used for the data processing of the operando neutron images.
