M AT E R I A L S S C I E N C E
Regulate transportation of ions and polysulfides in 
all–solid-­state Li-­S batteries using ordered-­MOF 
composite solid electrolyte
Jia Li1†, Fangxi Xie2†, Weiwei Pang3, Qingyou Liang4, Xianfeng Yang4, Lei Zhang1*
A dilemma arises when striving to balance the maximum desired ion conductivity and minimize the undesired 
lithium polysulfide shuttling effect for all–solid-­state lithium-­sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs). Here, we introduce a 
strategy of using ordered MIL-­125–NH2 as fillers for poly(ethylene oxide)–based electrolytes to simultaneously 
regulate the transportation of lithium ions and polysulfides. When compared to electrolytes lacking metal-­organic 
frameworks (MOFs) and those containing disordered MOFs, the electrolyte featuring an ordered-­MOF structure, 
denoted as three-­dimensional (3D) MPPL composite solid electrolyte (CSE), exhibits the highest ion conductivity 
of 8.3 × 10−4 siemens per centimeter at 60°C. As a result, pouch-­type ASSLSBs with 3D MPPL CSE maintains stable 
cycling for 400 cycles at 0.5 C at 60°C, showcasing the successful implementation of this strategy in simultane-
ously regulating ion and polysulfide transport. This approach opens up alternative avenues to achieve high-­
performance ASSLSBs with exceptional energy density.
INTRODUCTION
Lithium-­sulfur (Li-­S) batteries are considered one of the most prom-
ising candidates to satisfy the emerging market demands for energy 
storage (1, 2). On the basis of the S/S2− and Li+/Li redox couples, Li-­S 
batteries can offer a high theoretical capacity (1675 mAh g−1) and, 
consequently, a high energy density (3–6). Furthermore, the utiliza-
tion of elemental sulfur, which has abundant reserves on Earth, offers 
a much lower cost (~$100 per ton) than conventional layered cath-
odes with precious transition metal elements (for example, $75,000 
per ton for Co) (7). In the realm of Li-­S battery research, all–solid-­
state Li-­S batteries (ASSLSBs) have emerged as a highly promising 
avenue for their practical realization (8–10). Specifically, the incorpo-
ration of solid-­state electrolytes (SSEs) in ASSLSBs, characterized by 
their intriguing physicochemical attributes, such as nonflammability, 
noncorrosiveness, nonvolatility, and leak-­proof qualities, provides a 
level of safety superior to that of conventional Li-­S batteries using liq-
uid electrolytes (11). Furthermore, the high Young’s modulus of SSEs 
enhances resilience against lithium dendrite formation, thereby bol-
stering overall safety (12). Furthermore, ASSLSBs eliminate the need 
for liquid electrolytes, rendering them more straightforward to man-
ufacture compared to traditional Li-­S batteries while also endowing 
them with superior energy density as energy storage devices. Al-
though ASSLSBs hold great promise, they are not without any chal-
lenges (12). A notable hurdle lies in the rigid interaction between 
inorganic solid electrolytes and active materials in the solid-­solid inter-
face. The innate rigidity and brittleness of inorganic solid electrolytes 
can lead to increased interfacial impedance with the electrodes (7, 13). 
What exacerbates this issue is the substantial volumetric expansion of 
79% during the lithiation process of sulfur, as it transforms from S8 to 
Li2S. This expansion can result in contact loss and the formation of 
irreversible Li2S, leading to the chemomechanical failure of ASSLSBs (9, 
14, 15). Therefore, developing SSEs for ASSLSBs with optimized 
transportation and prolonged cycle life is desired but remains a 
challenge.
Solid polymer electrolytes (SPEs), such as poly(ethylene oxide) 
(PEO), polyacrylonitrile (PAN), and poly(vinylidene fluoride), are 
highly flexible. They can offer intimate interfacial contact with 
electrodes and avoid the fragile issue of their inorganic analogs, 
thus extending the cycle life of ASSLSBs (16). Unfortunately, de-
spite their superiorities, SPEs always exhibit low ion conductivity 
(10−5 to 10−10 S cm−1) at room temperature (17, 18). Therefore, 
maximizing ionic conductivity stands as a notable focal point in 
the study of SPEs (9). On the other hand, plenty of long-­/mid-­
chain polysulfides (LiPSs) (Li2Sn, n ≥ 4) are formed because of the 
reduction of S in the cycling process of Li-­S batteries (3–6). The 
similar molecular structure between LiPSs and polymer electro-
lytes, particularly PEO-­based electrolytes, results in the dissolu-
tion of LiPSs, which is also known as the “shuttle effect” of LiPSs 
(19, 20). This has been considered as the key reason of irreversible 
loss of active materials and rapid capacity degradation of Li-­S bat-
teries (21). Thus, minimizing the undesired LiPSs shuttle effect 
remains one of the foremost concerns in the field of Li–S batteries 
(19, 20). The simultaneous pursuit of both maximum ion conduc-
tivity and minimum LiPSs shuttle presents a dilemma for ASSLSBs, 
hence demanding a feasible strategy to selectively optimize trans-
portation in SPEs.
Metal-­organic frameworks (MOFs), porous crystalline inorganic-­
organic materials with tunable pore sizes, offer a unique capability 
to selectively separate various molecules (12, 22–26). This remark-
able capability of MOFs for selective separation can serve as a po-
tential approach to regulate the transportation of ions and LiPSs 
simultaneously if MOFs are incorporated to ASSLSBs as inorganic 
fillers into polymer matrices (12, 27–34). However, when using the 
conventional solvent casting method for MOFs–polymer composite 
solid electrolytes (CSEs), MOF particles are often distributed in a 
disordered and discontinuous manner within the polymer matrix

(35, 36). The disordered and discontinuous distribution of MOFs 
may result in nonuniform Li+ deposition and disruption of Li+ trans-
portation, thus consequently promoting lithium dendrite growth 
and reducing Li+ conductivity, respectively (37). This hinders the ef-
fective utilization of the selective regulating capability of MOFs (36, 
38). Despite the selective regulating capability that MOFs can offer, 
the implantation of such a capability in MOF-­related CSEs for 
ASSLSBs remains a challenge.
Herein, we propose a strategy of using ordered MOF as fillers to 
prepare MOF-­related CSEs for ASSLSBs, enabling simultaneously 
regulated transportation of ions and LiPSs. As a proof of concept, 
ordered distribution of MIL-­125–NH2 within a PEO-­based electro-
lyte (denoted as 3D MPPL CSE) was successfully prepared through 
electrospinning and the following calendaring approach. Three-­
dimensional (3D) MPPL CSE, featured with an ordered distributed 
MIL-­125–NH2 structure, provides highly ordered channels, facili-
tating the strong dissociation of lithium salt and inducing uniform 
Li+ transport. As a result, the ionic conductivity of 3D MPPL CSE is 
as high as 8.3 × 10−4 S cm−1 at 60°C. In addition, it also induces the 
formation of Li3N, providing even lithium deposition. Consequently, 
the cycle life span of a lithium symmetric cell using 3D MPPL CSE is 
almost 15 times longer than that of cells using its analogs without 
MOF filler and with disordered-­MOF filler, respectively. Further-
more, 3D MPPL CSE with an ordered distributed MIL-­125–NH2 pro-
vides multiple barriers, preventing the shuttle of LiPSs and allowing 
prolonged cycle life. Specifically, the pouch-­type ASSLSBs with 3D 
MPPL CSE cycles stably for 400 cycles at 0.5 C at 60°C. The high ion 
conductivity and excellent cycle life demonstrate the successful im-
plementation of using an ordered-­MOF structure to simultaneously 
regulate ion and polysulfide transportation. This strategy opens up a 
previously unidentified way to achieve high-­performance ASSLSBs, 
paving the way to achieve ASSLSB with high energy density for prac-
tical application.
RESULTS
Synthesis and structure of 3D MPPL CSE
The 3D MPPL CSE was synthesized using an electrospinning 
process coupled with a calendering process, followed by a subse-
quent pouring of a PEO/LiTFSI homogeneous solution, as illus-
trated in Fig. 1A. As determined by the N2 adsorption-­desorption 
isotherms of the MIL-­125–NH2 precursors (fig. S1), the MIL-­125–NH2 
particles show an average pore size of 4.5 Å, which is notably 
smaller than the diameters of LiPSs (Li2Sn, 4 < n ≤ 8, 5.2 to 8.4 Å). 
Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron 
microscopy (TEM) images of MIL-­125–NH2 particles demon-
strate that they have a tetragonal plate morphology with an aver-
age diameter of 1 μm (Fig.  1B and fig.  S2). X-­ray diffraction 
(XRD) pattern (fig. S3) confirms that the prepared product is a 
MIL-­125–NH2 precursor (39). Afterward, taking advantage of the 
good compatibility between MIL-­125–NH2 and PAN, we used an 
electrospinning technique to synthesize a spinning film with uni-
form distribution of MIL-­125–NH2.As shown in fig.  S4A, the 
large size of the 3D MIL-­125–NH2@PAN film (denoted as 3D 
MP) suggests its potential for large-­scale preparation. The SEM 
images (fig. S4, B and C) show that it exhibits a microstructure 
with a necklace-­like morphology but has a loosened structure. The 
XRD pattern (fig. S4D) indicates the presence of MIL-­125–NH2 
in 3D MP. The TEM images (fig. S5, A and B) further exhibit a 
microstructure with a necklace-­like morphology of 3D MP mem-
brane, and the MIL-­125–NH2 particles are completely embedded 
in the PAN fibers (fig. S5B, inset). A calendering process was ad-
opted to increase the density of 3D MP membrane. The SEM im-
age (Fig. 1C) of the calendered 3D MP membrane shows that the 
overall structure of material inherits a necklace–like morphology. 
In particular, the 3D MP membrane has a 3D network of nanofi-
brous MIL-­125–NH2@PAN structures with the MIL-­125–NH2 
completely embedded in the PAN fibers. Specifically, as shown 
in Fig. 1D and fig. S5 (C and D), the fibers intertwine to create 
an almost dense structure with a symmetrical distribution of 
MIL-­125–NH2, revealing the ordered distribution of MOF parti-
cles in the fiber and the membrane. XRD patterns demonstrate 
that the crystal structure of MIL-­125–NH2 in the 3D MP mem-
brane before and after calendering remains unchanged (figs. S3 
and S6). Compared with fig. S4, the results of the calendered 3D 
MP membrane indicate that the calendering process increases 
the density of 3D MP, leading to a reduction in pore size and an 
enhancement of interfacial contact areas with the electrode (40). 
However, there are still a few gaps in the 3D MP membrane. Last, 
to completely eliminate the pores in the 3D MP membrane, the 
infiltration of PEO/LiTFSI in the 3D MP membrane to form the 
final 3D MPPL CSE was realized. The SEM image (Fig. 1E) re-
veals that 3D MPPL CSE exhibits a smooth and clear surface, 
along with a homogeneous film. This also indicates that there is 
no phase separation between the 3D MP membrane and PEO, 
suggesting that the 3D MP membrane is well distributed in the 
PEO polymer. In addition, the cross-­sectional SEM image of 3D 
MPPL CSE (Fig. 1F) shows an average thickness of around 76 μm, 
agreeing with the thickness measured from thickness gauge (fig. S7). 
The optical photographs of 3D MPPL CSE show its self-­standing, 
transparent, and flexible nature, allowing it to be mass-­prepared 
and making it suitable for use as electrolytes in both coin and 
pouch cells (Fig. 1G and fig. S8). The corresponding elemental 
mapping images (Ti, N, F, O, and S, as shown in Fig. 1H) of 3D 
MPPL CSE reveal the uniform spatial distribution of MIL-­125–NH2 
in 3D MPPL CSE. The results prove that MIL-­125–NH2 particles 
are uniformly dispersed in 3D MPPL CSE. XRD patterns also 
show that the diffraction peak at 9.8°, which can be attributed to 
MIL-­125–NH2, is presented at 3D MPPL CSE, agreeing with the 
XRD peaks of pristine MIL-­125–NH2 (fig. S9). This indicates that 
the crystal structure of MIL-­125–NH2 remains unchanged dur-
ing the whole preparation process (39). Furthermore, the peaks 
at 19.1° and 23.2° corresponding to PEO weaken upon the intro-
duction of the 3D MP membrane. This observation suggests a 
reduction in the crystallinity degree of PEO (41). For compari-
son, PEO/LiTFSI CSE (denoted as PL CSE) and MIL-­125–NH2/
PEO/LiTFSI (denoted as MPL CSE) were prepared by a typical 
solution casting method. The SEM images, energy-­dispersive x-­
ray (EDX) mapping images, and optical photographs (figs. S10 to 
S13) of MPL CSE and PL CSE demonstrate that their morpholo-
gies and physical characteristics are similar to those of 3D MPPL 
CSE. These similar properties allow them to be used as control 
samples in the following experiments. The SEM images and EDS 
elemental mapping images of 3D MPPL CSE and MPL CSE re-
veal that, when compared to the conventional solvent casting 
method, electrospinning and the following calendering ap-
proach can achieve a more uniform dispersion of MOFs in SSEs 
(figs. S14 and S15).

Physicochemical characterizations of 3D MPPL CSE
The ordered distribution of MIL-­125–NH2 in 3D MPPL CSE was 
evaluated by small-­angle x-­ray scattering (SAXS). As displayed in 
Fig. 2A, there is no indication of secondary structures in the scat-
tering profile, suggesting the uniform distribution of charges in the 
polymer and the high uniformity of 3D MPPL CSE, MPL CSE, and 
PL CSE (18). The radius of gyration (Rg), calculated by Guinier’s 
law, is defined as the radius of rotation of the scatterer. Its physical 
meaning is similar to the radius of inertia, which is used to analyze 
the dispersion of scatterers (42). Furthermore, the Guinier curve 
of 3D MPPL CSE presents two linear regions, with correspond-
ing Rg values of 4.5 and 16.5 nm, respectively. These values are 
apparently larger than the Rg values (4.0 and 14.6 nm) of the Guinier 
curve of MPL CSE (Fig. 2B). The larger value of Rg indicates 
that MIL-­125–NH2 particles in 3D MPPL CSE are more evenly dis-
tributed than those in MPL CSE (19). In summary, the results 
from SAXS and Guinier curves reveal a higher degree of order in 
MIL-­125–NH2 within the as-­prepared 3D MPPL CSE compared to 
that in MPL CSE.

variations arising from the inclusion of MOFs and the ordered-­
MOF structure. Specifically, we conducted Fourier transform infra-

We investigated the impact of different forms of MIL-­125–NH2 
on the overall crystallinity of the composite system using differential 
scanning calorimetry (DSC). Furthermore, the glass transition tem-
perature (Tg) calculated by DSC can be used to directly reveal the 
transfer temperature from the crystalline to the amorphous state 
(47). As determined by DSC results (Fig. 2E), the Tg of 3D MPPL 
CSE is −53.2°C, which is lower than those of MPL CSE (−53.1°C) 
and PL CSE (−52.3°C). In addition, the Tg values of both 3D MPPL 
CSE and MPL CSE are nearly identical. This indicates that the pres-
ence of MIL-­125–NH2, whether in a disordered or ordered form, 
exerts a similar influence on reducing the overall crystallinity of 
PEO (48). The melting temperatures (Tm) of PL CSE and MPL CSE 
are 51.0 and 50.4°C, respectively, whereas 3D MPPL CSE shows a 
lower Tm of 49.0°C. These results suggest that the inclusion of 
MIL-­125–NH2 can effectively hinder the organized alignment of 
PEO chains. Furthermore, XRD patterns were also measured to de-
termine the crystallinity of PEO (fig. S22). When MIL-­125–NH2 is 
incorporated, both MPL CSE and 3D MPPL CSE show a decreased 
XRD peak intensity of PEO, indicating the successful inhabitation of 
the crystallization process of PEO chains due to the presence of 
MOF particles. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) plots of 3D 
MPPL CSE, MPL CSE, and PL CSE are shown in fig. S23. The de-
composition of PL CSE begins at 350°C, with a carbon residue of 
approximately 9.4% observed above 600°C. In comparison to PL 
CSE, both MPL CSE and 3D MPPL CSE exhibit lower decomposi-
tion temperatures (336° and 327°C, respectively), suggesting that 
more amorphous regions of PEO are present in the 3D MPPL CSE 
and MPL CSE. This subsequently reduces the crystalline nature of 
PEO, potentially enhancing the σ of the electrolyte. In addition, 
when subjected to thermal shock conditions (200°C, 0.5 hours), the 
MPL CSE and PL CSE melted, while the size and morphology of 3D 
MPPL CSE remain substantially unchanged (fig. S24). Overall, the 
DSC results suggest that the ordered feature might lower the Tm and 
Tg of PEO, which have a positive impact on σ. The results above 
demonstrate that the ordered arrangement of MIL-­125–NH2 in 3D 
MPPL CSE reduces the crystallinity of the PEO matrix by prevent-
ing the crystallization of PEO molecular chains. As a result, such an 
ordered arrangement of MIL-­125–NH2 promotes the conduction of 
lithium ions on the surface of the filler particles and thus improves 
the σ. Meanwhile, the strong Lewis acid–base interaction between 
the surface chemical groups of the MIL-­125–NH2 and the cations/
anions from Li salts could promote the dissociation of Li salts and, 
consequently, enhance Li+ transport, which have a positive im-
pact on σ.
Considering the positive effect of the 3D MPPL CSE structure on 
LiTFSI dissociation, we examined the influence of the ordered-­
MOF structure on the σ of the CSE samples using electrochemical 
impedance spectroscopy (EIS), as shown in figs. S25 and S26. The 
EIS curves of stainless steel (SS)|CSE|SS at 60°C are shown in 
Fig. 2F. Among the three CSEs, 3D MPPL CSE exhibits the lowest 
bulk resistance, suggesting that its σ is higher than those of MPL 
CSE and PL CSE. Figure 2G shows Arrhenius plots of 3D MPPL 
CSE, MPL CSE, and PL CSE. According to Eq. 1, at 30°C, the σ val-
ues for 3D MPPL CSE, MPL CSE, and PL CSE are 2.1  ×  10−5, 
8.6 × 10−6, and 7.4 × 10−6 S cm−1, respectively (figs. S25 and S26). 
Meanwhile, at 60°C, the corresponding values are 8.3 × 10−4, 4.6 × 10−4, 
and 3.8 × 10−4 S cm−1, respectively. The values of σ of the 3D MPPL 
CSE are meaningfully higher than those of MPL CSE and PL CSE at 
all temperatures. This substantiates that, apart from reducing crys-
tallinity, the ordered-­MOF structure in 3D MPPL CSE can further 
augment the ion transport capability of the electrolyte (47). In com-
parison to MPL CSE, the CSE with the disordered-­MOF structure 
prepared by the conventional solution casting method, 3D MPPL 
CSE with the ordered-­MOF structure substantially enhances its σ. 
Although the disordered-­MOF structure in MPL CSE can reduce 
the crystallinity of PEO, they also tend to aggregate, forming discon-
tinuous Li+ pathways. Consequently, the aggregation might block 
the Li+ transport, leading to reduced σ. As suggested in Fig. 2H, it is 
postulated that the presence of the ordered-­MOF structure within 
the electrolyte not only diminishes the crystallinity of the composite 
system but also provides 3D continuous Li+ conduction pathways 
with a large number of organic-­inorganic amorphous interphases, 
thereby augmenting Li+ transport in the electrolyte and offering a 
higher σ than the one with the disordered-­MOF structure. With a 
combination of chronoamperometry and EIS, the Li+ transference 
number (tLi+) of 3D MPPL CSE is calculated to be 0.81 according to 
Eq. 2 (fig. S27), which is much higher than those of MPL CSE (0.64) 
and PL CSE (0.39) (fig. S28 and table S3).
Mechanism of stability of the 3D MPPL CSE/Li interface
To investigate the influence of the ordered-­MOF structure on the 
cycling stability of the electrolytes, symmetrical lithium metal cells 
were assembled using the three prepared electrolytes and subjected 
to galvanostatic tests. As shown in fig. S29, the linear sweep voltam-
metry (LSV) curve of 3D MPPL CSE exhibits an increase at voltages 
above 5.0 V, signifying the composite system’s stability and its resis-
tance to decomposition at lower voltages. The electrochemical sta-
bility window of the 3D MPPL CSE is apparently higher than those 
of MPL CSE and PL CSE. As disclosed in Fig. 3A, the 3D MPPL 
CSE cell exhibits a substantially improved stability than other elec-
trolytes. Specifically, it enables a stable operation for more than 
1500 hours at 0.1 mA cm−2, and its voltage-­time curve remains 
smooth during operation. After 1500 hours, the overpotential of the 
Li|3D MPPL CSE|Li cell is 60 mV. In contrast, the Li|MPL CSE|Li 
cell short-­circuits after 124 hours, while the Li|PL CSE|Li cell short-­
circuits after 98 hours. This phenomenon is likely to be attributed to 
the instable solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer, causing the 
growth of Li dendrites that eventually penetrated the electrolytes 
(40). The Li|3D MPPL CSE|Li symmetrical cell exhibits a low initial 
voltage, and the overpotential progressively increases during opera-
tion. The continuous test results of the lithium symmetrical cells of 
3D MPPL CSE, MPL CSE, and PL CSE at different current densities 
are shown in Fig. 3B. The 3D MPPL CSE cell can stably operate at a 
large current density of 0.5 mA cm−2. In contrast, the symmetrical 
cells of MPL CSE and PL CSE suffer from short circuits at 0.2 and 
0.5 mA cm−2, respectively. This phenomenon suggests that 3D 
MPPL CSE exhibits a lower interface impendence under high cur-
rent densities. In addition, when the current density went back to 
0.1 mA cm−2, the Li|3D MPPL CSE|Li cell enables a stable opera-
tion for more than 1000 hours (fig. S30). The results indicate that 
the implementation of 3D MPPL CSE confers improved cycling sta-

We evaluated the stability between the CSEs and Li metal by

chemistry between the 3D MPPL CSE and Li metal, as illustrated in 
Fig. 3 (J to L). The formation of uniform, inorganic, and stable Li3N

CSE and Li metal (51, 52). The ordered-­MOF structure in 3D MPPL 
CSE facilitates the formation of Li3N in the interface between Li 
metal and CSE, compared to that of MPL CSE, the CSE with the 
disordered-­MOF structure. This highlights the crucial impact of the 
presence of MOF and the distinctive ordered-­MOF structure in 3D 
MPPL CSE on facilitating Li3N formation and consequently pro-
longing cycle life.
In situ Raman analysis of CSE-­based ASSLSBs
To delve deeper into the impact of the ordered-­MOF structure in 
mitigating the shuttle effect of LiPSs, we investigate the LiPSs per-
meation behavior through two experiments. First, the LiPSs perme-
ation behavior through barrier layers was investigated in an H-­typed 
glass apparatus (fig. S34). Apparently, the right chamber of PL CSE 
turns from colorless to brownish red after 90 min, suggesting that 
the PL CSE experiences serious LiPSs penetration. Comparatively, 
brownish red can be observed in the right chamber of the MPL CSE 
after 300 min, suggesting that the migration of LiPSs across the MPL 
CSE is partially restrained. Furthermore, when the 3D MPPL CSE 
was used as the barrier layer, only a slight yellow color can be per-
ceived for the solution in the right chamber of it after 420 min. It 
indicates that the 3D MPPL CSE can effectively block the perme-
ation of LiPSs from the cathode to the anode, even at high concen-
trations of LiPSs. The prevention of LiPSs of 3D MPPL CSE might 
originate from the physical confinement and its strong chemical im-
mobilization layer. Another grievous problem in Li-­S batteries is the 
self-­discharge behavior, which results in the decrease of open-­circuit 
voltage (OCV) and the poor cyclability (53). A stable OCV indicates 
great advantage in blocking the shuttle of LiPSs, showcasing a prom-
ising potential for future practical application and storage (54). As 
shown in fig. S35, notable OCV drops are observed for the PL CSE 
and MPL CSE, suggesting their weak ability of blocking shuttle of 
LiPSs. In contrast, the 3D MPPL CSE sustains a stable OCV over 
200-­hour standing, suggesting its superiority in blocking the shut-
tle of LiPSs.

DISCUSSION
From the results above, we propose that the improved performance 
of 3D MPPL CSE compared to its counterparts (PL CSE and MPL 
CSE) originates from three aspects. First, the FTIR and Raman re-
sults demonstrate that the incorporation of MIL-­125–NH2 provides 
additional Ti sites to form a chemical attachment with TFSI−, allow-
ing a more effective dissociation of LiTFSI and offering additional 
free Li+ ions. The presence of MOF improves the σ of as-­prepared 
CSEs. In addition, the incorporation of MOFs in CSEs reduces the 
crystalline nature of PEO, potentially enhancing the σ of electro-
lytes. Moreover, the ordered-­MOF structure of 3D MPPL CSE offers 
additional ordered 3D channels for Li+ transportation, further fa-
cilitating the σ of CSEs.
Second, the ex situ XPS results reveal that the incorporation of 
MOFs in CSEs induces the formation of Li3N on the electrolyte/Li 
interface. Li3N serves as both a superionic Li+ conductor and a pro-
moter of good wettability with lithium metal, thereby suppressing 
localized Li+ deposition, promoting interfacial stability, and extend-
ing the cycle life. Moreover, the ordered MIL-­125–NH2 structure of 
3D MPPL CSE facilitates the formation of these two compounds, 
offering further extended cycle life of 3D MPPL CSE than those 
of pristine PL CSE and the MPL CSE with the disordered-­MOF 
structure.
Third, because highly ordered micropores (approximately 4.5 Å) 
of MIL-­125–NH2 are notably smaller than the diameters of LiPSs 
(Li2Sn, 4 < n ≤ 8) (57), the CSEs with MIL-­125–NH2 restrict the 
shuttle of LiPSs, offering enhanced cycle life compared to PL CSE,

LiPSs, uneven Li+ flux, and low lithium dissociation. When it comes 
to the case of MPL CSE (Fig. 6C), the introduction of MIL-­125–NH2 
shows promise in restraining the shuttle effect of LiPSs. However, it 
fails to address the issues of disparate distribution of MIL-­125–NH2 
within the CSEs, leading to the inhomogeneous Li+ flux and low 
LiTFSI dissociation. Therefore, through the construction of a compact 
3D MP membrane with an ordered distribution of MIL-­125–NH2, 
the resulting interconnected 3D MPPL CSE not only exhibits aug-
mented uniform Li+ flux and enhanced LiTFSI dissociation but also 
effectively suppresses the shuttle effect of LiPSs.
In summary, we have successfully demonstrated a strategy of us-
ing an ordered-­MOF structure to simultaneously regulate transpor-
tation of ions and LiPSs in ASSLSBs. An ordered distribution of 
MOFs, in this case, MIL-­125–NH2, can be easily obtained through 
an electrospinning and a subsequent calendering process. The elec-
trolyte, named 3D MPPL CSE, with an ordered-­MOF structure of-
fers a more effective dissociation of LiTFSI in the electrolyte as well 
as the continuous structure of MOFs, facilitating the ion transporta-
tion and enhancing the rate performance of ASSLSBs. Simultane-
ously, the ordered-­MOF structure in 3D MPPL CSE offers highly 
ordered micropores and multiple barriers to restrict the shuttle of 
LiPSs, offering an enhanced cycle life of ASSLSBs. This study pres-
ents a strategy using an ordered-­MOF structure to concurrently 
regulate ion and LiPSs transportation in ASSLSBs, thereby opening 
the door to realizing ASSLSBs with notably improved energy den-
sity, making them viable for practical applications.

and long-­term cycling performance tests were carried out at a con-
stant current mode between 1.6 and 2.6 V at 60°C using Neware 
battery testers. Meanwhile, the EIS spectra of the cells were collected 
over the frequencies from 105 to 10−2 Hz using the GAMRY 1000E.
In situ Raman spectroscopy measurements
The in situ optical cells were assembled in an argon-­filled glovebox. 
We used the bare KB@S composite as the working electrode and 
cold-­pressed lithium metal on a copper foil as the counter electrode. 
The PL CSE, MPL CSE, 3D MPPL CSE acted as the electrolyte to 
connect two electrodes. An optical glass slide was placed on top of 
the electrodes, and the cell was then sealed by a polytetrafluoroeth-
ylene high-­temperature film. A hole (1 to 2 mm) was punched on Li 
metal anode to allow the laser to shine on the CSEs directly. The 
in situ electrochemical reaction was performed with GAMRY 1000E, 
and Raman signals were recorded on a Raman microscope (HJY 
LabRAM Aramis) by a 785-­nm laser.
