SUMMARY
All-solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) offer a promising solution for safe, high-energy density, and 
cost-effective energy storage. However, their practical performance is limited by low-density triple-phase- 
boundaries (TPBs) and tortuous ionic/electronic paths. Here, we report a two-length-scale architectural design 
for a solid sulfur cathode. By decoupling host particle size (electrode-scale transport) from pore size (particle- 
scale kinetics) and tracking processing-induced sulfur redistribution, we show that microporous, micron-scale 
carbon hosts can satisfy these competing constraints. Micropores confine sulfur, increase TPB density, and 
suppress sulfur relocation during milling, while micron-scale particles reduce ionic tortuosity and mitigate 
chemo-mechanically driven contact loss during reactions. The resulting cathodes deliver extremely high sulfur 
utilization (>1,500 mAh gS
−1), areal capacity (>9 mAh cm−2) with high sulfur content (∼50 wt %), and 94.2% 
capacity retention over 250 cycles at 6 mAh cm−2 in lithium-metal full cell. This multi-scale carbon-host strat­
egy provides a practical route to high-loading, high-performance sulfur cathodes for all-solid-state batteries.
INTRODUCTION
Electrification of transport and grid-scale storage demands 
batteries that combine high energy density, low cost, and 
intrinsic safety. Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries (LSBs) are prom­
ising alternatives because of their high theoretical capacity 
(1,672 mAh g−1), low cathode cost, and environmental friend­
liness.1,2
However, conventional LSBs employing liquid 
electrolytes suffer from dissolution and migration of Li polysul­
fides, which drive sulfur loss, electrolyte consumption, and 
Li corrosion, limiting cell lifetime3,4
and raising safety 
concerns.5,6
CONTEXT & SCALE
All-solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) are promising next-generation energy- 
storage technologies because of their high energy density, enhanced safety, and low-cost advantages. Real­
izing practical high-sulfur-content cathodes, however, requires navigating a fundamental trade-off, i.e., 
achieving dense sulfur/carbon/solid-electrolyte triple-phase boundaries (TPBs) while maintaining low-tortu­
osity ion and electron transport through thick electrodes. 
This work establishes a two-length-scale carbon-host design principle that decouples the roles of carbon- 
host particle size and pore size. We show that particle size primarily dictates electrode-level ionic transport, 
whereas pore size determines TPB density. Incorporating microporosity further restricts sulfur redistribution 
during electrode processing and preserves a continuous reaction network throughout cycling. 
Guided by this framework, we identify microporous, micron-scale carbon hosts as an optimal architecture for 
simultaneously maximizing sulfur utilization, transport efficiency, and long-term cycling stability. These cath­
odes deliver high areal capacities exceeding 9 mAh cm−2 at ∼50 wt % sulfur content, and full cell paired with 
a Li-metal anode retain 94.2% of their initial capacity over 250 cycles at 6 mAh cm−2. This multi-scale carbon- 
host strategy provides a practical route to high-loading, high-performance sulfur cathodes for all-solid-state 
batteries.

All-solid-state Li-S batteries (ASSLSBs) address these issues 
by eliminating liquid electrolytes.7,8 However, practical deploy­
ment is still limited by short cycle life, the need for high operating 
pressure, and practically low energy. For long cycling, the stabil­
ity of Li metal against the solid electrolyte presents a major sys­
tems-level challenge. In particular, thiophosphate solid electro­
lytes are vulnerable to Li creeping along grain boundaries 
under high stack pressures and when ultrathin (<30 μm) sepa­
rator layers are used, which accelerates filament formation and 
leads to internal shorting.9,10 For cell-level energy improvement, 
performance is primarily constrained by cathode limitations.11–14
In the absence of soluble intermediates, the sulfur conversion re­
action is largely confined to the triple-phase boundaries (TPBs), 
where sulfur (S), electronic conductor (carbon [C]), and solid- 
state electrolyte (SSE) simultaneously contact. Achieving high 
sulfur utilization at practical sulfur loading requires a high TPB 
density, yet thick composite electrodes require continuous, 
low-tortuosity ionic and electronic transport pathways. This 
challenge becomes even more acute when targeting high 
C-rate performance at practical sulfur fractions. As the sulfur 
fraction increases to levels necessary for high energy density, 
the effective S/C/SSE TPB density decreases and ionic transport 
pathways become increasingly tortuous, leading to severe rate 
limitations. These constraints highlight the need for cathode ar­
chitectures that can simultaneously sustain high sulfur utilization 
and efficient ion/electron transport under realistic loading condi­
tions. However, these requirements are intrinsically coupled and 
often conflicting; architectures that locally maximize TPBs can 
increase tortuosity and isolate SSE percolation globally, while ar­
chitectures optimized for transport may reduce TPB density and 
slow conversion kinetics.15–18
Porous carbons are widely used as sulfur hosts, but design 
rules established for liquid-electrolyte Li-S batteries do not 
translate directly to solid-state cathodes. In liquid-electrolyte 
Li-S batteries, microporous carbons can confine polysulfides 
through nanoscale adsorption arising from overlapping 
Lennard-Jones potentials, whereas mesoporous carbons pri­
marily provide sufficient pore volume for sulfur infiltration and 
accessible pathways for electron and ion transport.1,19,20
Various porous carbon structures have been tested in 
ASSLSBs with notable progress.21–23 Impregnating sulfur into 
porous carbon hosts has shown greater efficacy than merely 
mixing sulfur and carbon materials.24,25 For instance, mesopo­
rous carbons have been proven effective in improving cell perfor­
mance.22,26–28 Recent studies by Kaskel and colleagues have 
highlighted the influence of pore size on sulfur utilization.29 Addi­
tionally, compared with that of their mesoporous counterparts, 
the performance of microporous carbons tends to be less 
consistent across different research groups.21,30 Furthermore, 
debates concerning performance enhancements are ongoing, 
due to different carbon synthesis methods. While template- 
based mesoporous carbon typically excel, certain studies sug­
gest that activated mesoporous carbons can achieve similar re­
sults under optimized conditions.31,32
Here, we establish a two-length-scale design principle for 
solid sulfur cathodes by disentangling (1) electrode-scale trans­
port governed by composite tortuosity and percolation, (2) parti­
cle-scale kinetics governed by TPB density, and (3) processing- 
and cycling-induced microstructural evolution. Using mesoscale 
transport simulations, effective ionic/electronic conductivity 
measurements, computed TPB metrics, and operando chemo- 
mechanical diagnostics, we find that microporous carbon hosts 
with micron-scale secondary particles best reconcile these con­
straints. Implementing this principle enables high sulfur content 
(∼50 wt %), high sulfur utilization (>1,500 mAh gS
−1), and high 
electrode-level capacity (>750 mAh gelectrode
−1; >9 mAh cm−2) 
at a practical loading (6 mg cm−2), together with durable Li-metal 
full-cell cycling.
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Electrode-scale transport governed by host particle size
In solid sulfur cathodes, ionic and electronic transport are estab­
lished through percolating networks of SSE and carbon, respec­
tively, while sulfur conversion proceeds at TPBs.33 When SSE 
and carbon fractions are constrained to increase cell-level en­
ergy, the composite microstructure becomes highly sensitive 
to the particle size of the sulfur/carbon (S/C) composite. We first 
isolated this electrode-scale effect using mesoscale simulations 
of coupled ionic/electronic conduction in composite electrodes 
with a fixed S/C:SSE mass ratio and varied S/C particle size. 
The simulations reveal a trade-off—smaller S/C particles pro­
mote a more homogeneous electronic network, whereas larger 
S/C particles produce more continuous ionic current pathways 
through the SSE phase (Figures 1A–1C, S1A, and S1B). Parti­
cle-size-dependent conductivity modeling (Figure S1C) further 
defines an optimal host particle window of 2.5–7 μm, which bal­
ances robust electronic percolation with continuous ionic path­
ways. These findings underscore the crucial role of carbon hosts 
in affecting electrode-level transport properties. To validate 
these trends experimentally, we selected four porous carbons 
spanning two particle-size regimes and two pore-size regimes. 
The small-particle group consists of microporous BP-2000 
(Mic-S) and mesoporous KB-600 (Mes-S), while the large-parti­
cle group includes microporous YP-80F (Mic-L) and mesopo­
rous CMK-3 (Mes-L). This classification is corroborated by scan­
ning electron microscopy (SEM) (Figure 1D) and particle-size 
analysis (Figure S2), which distinguish a micron-scale regime 
for Mic-L and Mes-L (D50 = 5.89, 3.62 μm, respectively) from a 
nanoscale regime for Mic-S and Mes-S (D50 = 140, 150 nm, 
respectively). Based on the measured pore volumes and corre­
sponding calculation (Figure S3), 70 wt % sulfur was infiltrated 
into all S/C composites, representing the maximum loading 
that can be accommodated within the available pore volume of 
all four carbon hosts. Complete sulfur impregnation into the 
pores was verified by X-ray diffraction (XRD), Brunauer-Em­
mett-Teller (BET) analyses (Figure S4), and transmission electron 
microscopy-energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (TEM-EDS) 
(Figure S5).
The obtained S/C composites were then mixed with SSE at a 
weight ratio of S/C:SSE = 70:30, yielding composite electrodes 
with a sulfur content of 49 wt %. Effective ionic (σeff,ion) and elec­
tronic (σeff,elec) conductivities were measured by direct current 
(DC) polarization (methods; Figure S6). The small-particle group 
exhibited higher σeff,elec (0.104–0.108 S cm−1) than the large-par­
ticle group (0.015–0.019 S cm−1), consistent with improved

Micropores maximize TPB density for efficient sulfur 
conversion
In solid-state cathodes, sulfur utilization is controlled by the 
availability and stability of TPBs and by short-range ion transport 
to conversion fronts. At particle level, the pore size of the carbon 
hosts dictates the grain size of the infiltrated sulfur. Nitrogen 
sorption and TEM analyses show that Mic-S and Mic-L are domi­
nated by micropores (<2 nm), whereas Mes-S and Mes-L 
contain predominantly mesopores (2–10 nm) (Figures 2C and 
S8; Table S1). At comparable particle size, microporous hosts 
confine sulfur into smaller domains and increase the interfacial 
contact area available to the SSE, thereby increasing TPB den­
sity (schematic in Figure 2A). This trend is captured by computed 
TPB densities derived from synthetic three-dimensional (3D) mi­
crostructures (Figure 2B; computational details).
Analyzing the correlations of rate capability with particle size 
and pore class further clarifies the distinct roles played by these 
structural scales. As shown in Figure 2D, although the specific 
capacity shows no clear trend with particle size, the capacity 
fading at high rates remains strongly dependent on particle 
size. In contrast, the specific capacity exhibits a clear depen­
dence on pore architecture (Figure 2E)—microporous hosts 
consistently deliver higher sulfur utilization than mesoporous 
hosts across all C rates. This capacity gap becomes even 
more pronounced at a high rate, reaching nearly a 3-fold differ­
ence (1,070 mAh gS
−1 for Mic-L and 338 mAh gS
−1 for Mes-S 
at 2C). These results indicate that microporosity predominantly 
governs high-rate kinetics by increasing TPB density, minimizing 
ion-transport distances, and ensuring electronic accessibility at 
the particle scale.
Although the four commercial carbon hosts were selected to 
represent distinct structural length scales, they also exhibit var­
iations in other intrinsic material properties, as revealed by 
Raman and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analyses 
(Figure S10). However, correlations between electrochemical 
performance and these physicochemical descriptors (ID/IG ratio, 
O/C ratio, surface area, and total pore volume) are far less pro­
nounced than those associated with pore size and particle size 
(Figure S11). Accordingly, the observed electrochemical 
behavior is most consistently explained by these two primary 
structural length scales.
Prevented processing-induced sulfur relocation by 
micron-scale microporous hosts
Cathode architectures in ASSLSBs are not defined solely by 
pristine powders; processing can modify sulfur distribution and 
composite connectivity. For example, the ball milling process 
not only mixes solid components but also involves particle 
breakdown and heat generation, potentially affecting material 
properties, electrode architecture, and overall cell perfor­
mance.35,36 To directly probe microstructural changes during 
electrode processing, we removed the SSE from as-milled com­
posites by water washing and examined the remaining S/C 
structure. Surprisingly, small-particle hosts displayed severe 
post-milling agglomeration, whereas large-particle hosts largely 
preserved their secondary-particle morphology (Figures 3A, 3C, 
and S12). Figure 3A depicts the morphologies of the four S/C 
composites after SSE removal. Following ball milling, the D50 
values of small S/C composites (Mic-S and Mes-S) increased 
dramatically from their original sizes of 0.14 and 0.15 μm to 
10.7 (∼80-fold) and 22.3 μm (∼150-fold), respectively. 
Conversely, large S/C composites such as Mic-L and Mes-L 
maintained their individual particle sizes without notable 
agglomeration.
We then tested whether sulfur mobility during milling contrib­
utes to agglomeration and interfacial passivation by preparing 
sulfur-free C/SSE composites under identical milling conditions. 
In the absence of sulfur, agglomeration was substantially 
reduced for all hosts (Figures S12B and S12C), indicating that 
sulfur participates in the aggregation process. Figures 3C and 
S13 show that the D50 value of Mes-S decreased from 
22.3 μm with sulfur to 3.7 μm without sulfur. Meanwhile, Mic-S, 
with a similar particle size to Mes-S but smaller pores, showed 
a less drastic change from 10.7 μm (with sulfur) to 4.5 μm (without 
sulfur). A similar trend was observed in the large-particle group. 
To further elucidate sulfur distribution within the agglomerated 
secondary structures, we conducted scanning TEM (STEM) im­
aging and EDS elemental mapping. As shown in Figure 3B, the 
sulfur and carbon signals in Mic-S and Mes-S exhibit incomplete 
spatial overlap, indicating non-uniform sulfur distribution. This 
suggests that sulfur partially redistributes from the carbon 
framework and forms sulfur-rich domains during milling. In 
Mes-L, sulfur relocation is less pronounced but sulfur-rich do­
mains or discrete sulfur particles remain visible. In contrast, 
Mic-L largely preserves its secondary-particle morphology and 
displays a relatively uniform sulfur distribution without obvious 
sulfur agglomeration. The individual carbon and sulfur mapping 
in Figure S14 further clarify this spatial variation.
XRD analysis provides direct evidence that milling can release 
sulfur from porous hosts; although as-prepared S/C composites 
show no crystalline sulfur (Figure S4B), crystalline sulfur peaks 
appear after milling for all samples except Mic-L (Figure 3D). 
Together with TGA (Figure 3E), these data support a processing 
mechanism in which local heating and mechanical agitation 
mobilize infiltrated sulfur; the mobilized sulfur migrates and rede­
posits as a crystalline, bridge-forming phase that promotes S/C 
agglomeration and can passivate carbon/SSE contact. Impor­
tantly, the extent of sulfur relocation depends on both pore struc­
ture and diffusion length (i.e., particle size), which could be ex­
plained by capillary effects. The calculated capillary pressures 
based on the Young-Laplace equation are summarized in 
Figure S15. The microporous hosts exert a higher capillary pres­
sure (151–198 MPa) on liquid sulfur than mesoporous hosts (59– 
64 MPa), corresponding to a 2.5- to 3.4-times stronger confine­
ment effect.37,38 This stronger capillary pressure, coupled with 
increased diffusion paths, renders microporous large-particle 
(Mic-L) hosts the most resistant to sulfur redistribution, consis­
tent with the delayed and more gradual sulfur loss (Figure 3E).

explore the impact of particle size on electrode stability. To 
isolate cathode evolution from Li-metal anode effects, we 
compared Mic-S and Mic-L in all-solid-state cells using Li-In 
anode.

most previously reported ASSLSBs, Mic-L cathodes deliver both 
high electrode-based and areal capacities with long-term 
cycling stability under practical conditions. It should be noted

practical sulfide-based ASSLSBs. The relatively high pressures

densification of the composite cathode and to maintain intimate

anode. However, practical systems must ultimately operate at 
significantly lower pressures. This will require complementary

Conclusions
This study establishes a processing-robust, two-length-scale 
design rule for high-energy all-solid-state sulfur cathodes. 
Microporosity increases S/C/SSE TPB density and sustains 
high-rate sulfur conversion, while micron-scale particles reduce 
electrode tortuosity and mitigate chemo-mechanically driven 
contact loss in thick composite electrodes. By explicitly ac­
counting for processing-induced sulfur relocation during 
ball milling, we further show why pore-size trends can appear 
inconsistent across studies; sulfur redistribution can passivate 
interfaces and drive agglomeration, masking intrinsic TPB 
advantages. Implementing the combined principle with a micro­
porous micron-scale carbon host enables ∼50 wt % sulfur cath­
odes with >1,500 mAh g−1 utilization, >750 mAh gelectrode
−1 
at 6 mg cm−2. Additionally, the high loading electrodes 
(4 mg cm−2) showed impressive rate capabilities, delivering 
1,071 mAh g−1 at a high current density of 13.4 mA cm−2 (2C). 
Promisingly, when paired with a metallic Li anode, the solid-state 
Li-S cell retains a capacity retention of 94.24% for over 250 cy­
cles, marking among the best performances under practical 
conditions. Together, these findings provide cathode-architec­
ture guidelines for advancing ASSLSBs toward high-loading, 
high-active-fraction cell designs. Looking ahead, it is important 
to note that the use of pellet-based solid electrolytes remains a 
key barrier for practical implementation. Such pelletized cells 
require relatively high stack pressures to maintain intimate con­
tact between the cathode, separator, and anode. Ultimately, 
achieving the high-energy potential of all-solid-state Li-S pouch 
cells will depend on progress in scalable fabrication of ultrathin, 
highly conductive solid-electrolyte separators, along with inter­
face-engineering strategies that preserve intimate contact 
without reliance on large external pressures.
METHODS
Preparation of SSEs
To synthesize glass-ceramic Li7P2S8Br0.5I0.5 (LPSBI) solid elec­
trolyte, stoichiometric amounts of Li2S (Sigma-Aldrich, anhy­
drous, 99%), P2S5 (Sigma-Aldrich, 99%), LiBr (Sigma-Aldrich, 
99.99%), and LiI (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.99%) were carefully hand- 
ground in an agate mortar inside a glove box. The resulting 
mixture was ball-milled using cyclohexane as a solvent at 
600 rpm for 40 h using a planetary ball mill (PM 100, RETSCH) 
with zirconia balls at a ball-to-powder ratio of 10:1. A sealed 
and argon (Ar)-filled milling vessel was used to prevent contam­
ination from air and moisture during the milling process. The 
milled powders were dried and then annealed at 160◦C for 1 h 
under Ar atmosphere. The synthesized LPSBI exhibited an ionic 
conductivity of 5.2 mS cm−1 at room temperature for a cold 
pressed pellet.
Composite electrode
The S/C composites were prepared using four different commer­
cial carbon hosts: BP-2000 (Black Pearls, Cabot Corporation), 
KB (Ketjen Black EC-600JD, Nouryon), YP-80F (Kuraray), and 
CMK-3 (ACS Material). Prior to use, all carbon materials were 
dried under vacuum at 150◦C for 12 h. Sulfur (Sigma-Aldrich, 
99.998%) and each carbon host were manually mixed in a weight 
ratio of 70:30 (S:C, w/w) using an agate mortar. The mixture was 
heat treated at 155◦C for 12 h to infiltrate sulfur into the host ma­
terial. The resulting S/C composite were dried at 55◦C for 12 h 
before further use. For the composite electrode, the resulting 
S/C composite and LPSBI solid electrolyte were mixed in a 
weight ratio of 7:3 (S/C:SSE), with a total mass of 1 g. The mixture 
was transferred to a 50-mL zirconia jar containing 3 mm ZrO2 
balls (50 g) and ball milled at 450 rpm for 48 cycles (12 h) using

Cell fabrication
To fabricate all-solid-state Li-S cells, 80 mg of LPSBI powder 
was loaded into a PEEK mold (10-mm diameter) with Ti rods 
as current collectors and pelletized under 100 MPa. The com­
posite electrode was evenly spread on one side of the pellet 
and compressed at 700 MPa for 3 min. The sulfur loading in 
the composite electrodes was controlled at 1.5–6 mg cm−2. 
For cells with Li metal as anode, a 9-mm Li foil (100 μm thick) 
was placed on the opposite side of the SSE pellet and pressed 
at 100 MPa. For Li-In alloy anode, a 6-mm Li foil was placed 
beneath a 9-mm In foil and gently pressed to form Li0.5In. 
The amount of Li was adjusted based on the sulfur loading 
to maintain an N/P ratio = 2. The In foil side was placed in 
direct contact with the SSE pellet, and the anode was pressed 
at 100 MPa.
For both ionic and electronic conductivity measurement of the 
composite electrode, 50 mg of the composite materials was 
loaded into a mold cell. For ion-blocking cells, the pellet was 
compressed at 700 MPa. For electron-blocking cells, the pellet 
was compressed at 200 MPa, followed by pressing 100 mg of 
LPSBI onto both sides at 700 MPa. Li-In alloy anodes were 
attached to both sides of the cells under a pressure of 100 
MPa. All assembly steps were carried out in a dry Ar-filled glove 
box (H2O < 0.1 ppm, O2 < 0.1 ppm). All cells were tested in con­
stant-gap mode, with the gap adjusted to apply a stack pressure 
of 50 MPa.
Electrochemical measurement
The effective ionic and electronic conductivity of the composite 
electrodes was evaluated by DC polarization using a VMP-3 po­
tentiogalvanostat (BioLogic instrument). Constant voltages from 
−50 to 50 mV were applied and held for 1 h to ensure that the cell 
polarization reaches a steady state. Resistance was determined 
by Ohm’s law (R = U/I), and conductivity (σ) was calculated using 
σ = L/(R × A), where A and L represent the electrode area and 
thickness, respectively. Rate performance was conducted using 
Li-metal anode and cycled at 60◦C between 1.3 and 3.1 V (vs. Li/ 
Li⁺) with a sulfur loading of 4 mg cm−2. Discharge current density 
was stepwise increased from 0.1C to 2C, while the charge cur­
rent was fixed at 0.1C (1C = 1,672 mA g−1). To study cathode 
structural stability, cells with Li-In alloy anodes and a sulfur 
loading of 1.5 mg cm−2 were operated at room temperature at 
1.25 mA cm−2 (0.5C). High areal mass loading tests were con­
ducted with sulfur loading of 2, 4, and 6 mg cm−2. To exclude 
the influence from the anode, Li-In alloy was initially used as 
the anode. The cell was cycled at 60◦C in a voltage range 0.7– 
2.5 V (vs. Li-In/Li+), with a C rate of 0.2C–1C. To further examine 
the practical applicability in realistic cell configurations, cells 
were assembled with Li metal as the anode and tested at 0.2C 
under identical conditions. Before cycling test, cells underwent 
activation cycles at 0.05C to enhance solid-solid contact within 
the composite electrode and Li/SSE interfacial stability. The 
EIS test was performed before and after cycling within a fre­
quency range of 7 MHz to 0.05 Hz. For operando electrochemi­
cal pressure measurements, an RSB 2 load cell (Loadstar Sen­
sors) was placed beneath the cell stack to monitor pressure 
evolution during cycling. Real-time data were acquired using 
LoadVUE Pro software.
Material characterization
The morphology of the carbon hosts and composites was 
examined by FE-SEM (JSM-IT200, JEOL). For morphology 
analysis after SSE removal, samples were washed by immer­
sion in deionized water for 24 h, followed by centrifugation at 
2,000 rpm for 10 min and vacuum drying at room temperature. 
While electrode cross-sections were obtained by mechanically 
breaking assembled cells, for high-resolution cross-sectional 
analysis, samples were prepared using an Ar-ion beam cross- 
section polisher (IB-19520CCP, JEOL) operated under cryo­
genic conditions (−100◦C) to prevent mechanical/thermal arti­
facts. Additionally, an air-tight transfer vessel was employed 
to prevent any environmental degradation. Internal voids and 
cracks were segmented using the Trainable Weka Segmenta­
tion plug-in in ImageJ/Fiji, in which representative regions 
were manually labeled to train the pixel classifier. The trained 
classifier was then applied to the full cross-sectional images, 
and the segmented void/crack regions were overlaid onto the 
corresponding SE images for visualization. Particle-size distri­
bution was measured using a Malvern Mastersizer 3000E. 
The samples were dispersed in mixed solvent of isopropyl 
alcohol (IPA) and water (7:3 v/v) and ultrasonicated for 3 min 
prior to measurement. Pore structure was analyzed via N2 sorp­
tion at 77 K using a gas sorption system (Quadrasorb EVO). 
Carbon hosts and composite cathodes were degassed under 
vacuum at 100◦C and 30◦C, respectively, for 12 h. Total pore 
volume, and pore-size distribution were calculated by the den­
sity functional theory (DFT) method. Pore structure was further 
examined by TEM (300kV FEI Titan). XRD patterns were 
collected on a Rigaku MiniFlex II diffractometer with Cu Kα ra­
diation (λ = 1.5418 A˚ ) over a 2θ range of 10◦–70◦. The sulfur 
content in the S/C composite was determined by thermogravi­
metric analysis (STA 449, Netzsch) under N2 flow, with a heat­
ing rate of 10◦C min−1 up to 600◦C.
Computational details
The generation of synthetic 3D microstructures of the cathode 
was based on the random particle generation algorithm42 in 
the PoreSpy package.43 The packing of S/C composite particles 
with SSE particles was generated according to the carbon parti­

pore size in carbon particles is mostly below 10 nm (Figure S9), it 
is impossible to generate the cathode structure that can capture 
the micron-scale particles and nanoscale pores in a single ge­
ometry. Therefore, the multi-scale packing method was used 
for estimating the TPB. The micron-scale packed structure is 
used to calculate the interface area between S/C composite 
and SSE particles with unit of area per volume, called the SSE- 
carbon interface. The nanoscale packed structures were gener­
ated to mimic the internal structures of different carbon particles
