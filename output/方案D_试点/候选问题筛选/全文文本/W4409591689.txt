Halide Chemistry Boosts All-Solid-State Li-S Batteries
Feipeng Zhao* and Yanguang Li*
All-solid-state Li-S batteries (ASSLSBs) are emerging as a promising energy
storage solution due to their low cost and high energy density. Their
solid-state conﬁguration eﬀectively eliminates the notorious shuttle eﬀect
caused by soluble polysulﬁdes in conventional liquid electrolytes. However,
the heterogeneous solid-to-solid interfaces introduce signiﬁcant challenges,
including sluggish ion/electron transport and interfacial instability among
electrode materials, conductive additives, and solid electrolytes (SEs).
Recently, halide-based strategies have gained attention for enabling
high-performance ASSLSBs. This perspective highlights these strategies,
emphasizing the role of halide chemistry in enhancing ASSLSB kinetics. It is
contended that halides (e.g., iodides) in sulfur-based cathode
composites—such as Li2S and transition metal sulﬁdes—can activate S/Li2S
redox reactions, improving both ionic and electronic conductivities. This
“catalytic eﬀect” of halides accelerates the reversible transition, even in the
absence of conductive additives like SEs or conductive carbons. Moreover,
halides at the anode interface play a crucial role in preventing Li dendrite
formation and SE degradation, owing to their large polarizability and high
interfacial energy. This perspective provides a timely and insightful summary
of halide chemistry’s impact on ASSLSB kinetics, oﬀering inspiration for
further research and broader adoption of halide-based strategies in
next-generation solid-state Li-S batteries.

development of halide chemistry in ASSLSBs.
2. Halides Promoting Dual Conduction Properties
First, the semiconducting I2 can melt into the crystal structure
of S and form S9.3I cathode materials with a high electronic con-
ductivity of 5.9 × 10−7 S cm−1, showing an 11-order-of-magnitude
increase over the pristine S. The low melting point of the S9.3I
(65 °C) additionally enables self-healing ability of the S-based
cathode (by periodical re-melting) in full cells.[1] Second, intro-
ducing LiI to the S-based cathode materials is a general approach
to improve the ionic conductivity of Li2S. This is rooted in the
large radius and high polarizability of I−ions,[42,43] which has
also been employed to design I-incorporated sulﬁde and halide-
based SEs.[44,45] Through a mechanical milling method, the opti-
mal 20 mol% of LiI was incorporated into the crystal structure of
Li2S and formed solid solutions. The ionic conductivity of 4Li2S-
LiI (in molar ratio) reached 2.2 × 10−6 S cm−1, showing a two-
magnitude-of-order increase compared to the pristine Li2S.[29] In
this way, a high utilization of electrochemically active Li2S up to
95% was demonstrated.[28,29] Co-incorporating I−and other metal
elements (e.g., Al3+, Cu1+) further improved the ionic conductiv-
ity. For example, the ionic conductivity of Al/I co-doped Li2S ma-
terials could be signiﬁcantly improved to 6.0 × 10−5 S cm−1.[32]
More importantly, the electronic conductivity of Cu/I co-doped
Li2S increased by ﬁve orders of magnitude. The increase of dual
conduction enables the excellent reversibility of S/Li2S redox and
durability of the ASSLSBs up to 6200 cycles.[33] It is important to
note that the role of halides in enhancing electronic conductivity
has primarily been observed in iodine-doped sulﬁde materials.
This phenomenon can be attributed to the narrow band gap of
LiI and the semiconducting properties of I2.[1,46]

4. Halides Protecting Li Metal Anodes
Using Li metal as the anode is one of the most antecedent con-
ditions that enable high-energy density for ASSLSBs.[50] How-
ever, like the typical all-solid-state Li-metal batteries, employing
Li metal as the anode in ASSLSBs is still challenging.[18,51,52] Li
metal is extremely active and able to react with most SEs spon-
taneously. A few metal-free and fully reduced SEs (e.g., Li11P3S,
Li9N2Cl3, Li2.5N0.5S0.5, etc) show high chemical stability with Li
metal,[53–55] but Li dendrites could be generated at the Li/SEs in-
terface with high asperity and penetrate through the voids/grain
boundaries of the SE layer. Under these circumstances, interfa-
cial modulation has been recognized as necessary to improve the
interface stability of Li/SEs.[56] Among various interface strate-
gies (including artiﬁcial SEI or in situ formed interphases), in-
volving halide is popular as the surface mobility of lithium is
signiﬁcantly enhanced in the presence of LiX salts (X═F, Cl, Br,
and I).[57–60] In addition, the low electronic conductivity of LiX

layer to over 2.0 eV by the second, demonstrating LiF is specif-
ically eﬀective in blocking electron transport and preventing in-
terfacial reactions.
From the perspective of Li metal-compatible SEs, iodide-based
SEs have emerged as promising candidates for enhancing re-

et al.[39] employed AIMD simulations to establish theoretical in-

Through comparative analysis of pre- and post-simulation snap-

undergo reduction and migrate into adjacent Li layers, ultimately

contribution.[65] LiI-incorporated sulﬁdes or mixed-halogen argy-

5. Summary and Outlook
In conclusion, we elucidate the functionality of halide chem-
istry performed on both the cathode and the anode for high-
performance ASSLSBs. At the cathode, halogen can be incorpo-
rated into the S-based cathode active materials (S, Li2S, metal
sulﬁdes, etc.) to promote the dual (electron and ion) conduction
properties, which ﬁnally evolves into a “catalytic eﬀect” on the
rapid reversible S/Li2S redox reaction with high S utilizations.
In addition, based on the high reactivity between halides (e.g.,
LiI for Li2S-LiI solid solution, metal chlorides as pre-reactant)
and S-based cathode materials, redox mediates can be formed
as reaction sites with abundant percolation pathways for dual
conduction. This highlights the signiﬁcance of achieving spe-
ciﬁc cathode structures or all-electrochemical-active cathodes for
ASSLSBs. At Li metal anode, halide chemistry demonstrates a
pivotal role in reducing the interfacial reaction and preventing
the growth of Li dendrites. LiF, as the outstanding representa-
tive, has been widely used as the necessary SEI component to
regulate smooth Li plating/stripping and suppress the penetra-
tion of Li dendrites. Iodide SEs promise good reduction stability
and Li-dendrite-suppression capability at the same time.
Even so, we consider that halide chemistry is still underex-
plored in the ﬁeld of ASSLSBs. Therefore, we appeal for more
work about halide chemistry to facilitate the development of high-
performance ASSLSBs. The outlook is proposed as follows:
5.1. Developing Single-Phase Halogenated S-Based Cathode for
ASSLSBs
Building on the unique dual-conduction properties of halides in
Li2S cathodes, as discussed in this work, we propose the develop-
ment of single-phase halogenated sulfur-based cathodes without
the need for conductive carbon or solid electrolytes. As illustrated
in Figure 5, conventional ASSLSBs rely on a three-phase inter-
face system to ensure suﬃcient ionic and electronic conductivi-
ties. However, the sluggish S/S2−redox kinetics at these three-
phase boundaries remain unresolved. By leveraging the dual-
conduction capabilities of halides, we aim to design halogenated
sulfur-based cathodes that enable a single-phase reaction mech-
anism, thereby eliminating the challenges associated with three-
phase interfaces. This strategy diﬀers from previously reported
Li2S-LiI-MSx (M═V, Mo) and related composite materials,[30,69,70]
which inherently compromise speciﬁc capacity through transi-
tion metal incorporation, despite their beneﬁcial reduction in
conductive additive requirements. Actually, improving the redox
kinetics of the S/S2−redox couple has been demonstrated by
Pang et al.,[2] who successfully simpliﬁed the reaction mecha-
nism from a three-phase to a two-phase process by designing a
surface redox mediator on the sulfur-based cathode. Our strat-
egy, which aims to further reduce the reaction to a single-phase
process at the cathode, is anticipated to represent a signiﬁcant
advancement in this ﬁeld.
5.2. Exploring the Amorphization Eﬀect of Halogenation Strategy
in ASSLSBs
Amorphous components have emerged as a critical factor in
enhancing ionic conductivity and deformability. For instance,
recent studies on oxyhalide solid electrolytes (SEs), which ex-
hibit signiﬁcant amorphous phases, have demonstrated break-
throughs in achieving superionic conductivities exceeding 10
mS cm−1,[19] and enabling pressure-less operation in solid-state
batteries.[71] Given that sulfur (S) belongs to the same group as
oxygen (O), amorphization is expected to play a similarly im-
portant role in halogenated sulﬁde materials for ASSLSBs. To
advance this understanding, resolving the atomic structure of
amorphous halogenated-sulﬁde materials is essential. Beyond
conventional structural characterization techniques, we propose
the use of synchrotron radiation (SR) combined with Reverse
Monte Carlo (RMC) modeling analyses[72] to probe the material

5.3. Probing the Evolution of Halogenated Materials During
Electrochemical Cycling
As discussed in this work, the reactivity between sulfur-based
materials and halides complicates the evaluation of intermediate
stability during battery cycling. These intermediates, which may
arise from catalytic transitions (S/S2−) or the formation of redox
mediators, remain poorly understood, hindering detailed inves-
tigation into the dynamic mechanisms that enhance S/S2−redox
reactions in sulfur-based cathodes. A deeper understanding of
these processes is essential for developing targeted halogenation
strategies and halogenated catalysts to achieve highly reversible
S/Li2S transitions, such as LiVS2 redox mediates preprepared by
VCl4-activated reaction with Li2S.[36] To uncover more interme-

physicochemical characterization techniques, such as Raman
spectroscopy, secondary ion mass spectrometry (SIMS), X-ray ab-
sorption spectroscopy (XAS), and cryogenic electron microscopy
(cryo-EM), to monitor phase composition changes and hierar-
chical morphological transformations during cycling. These ad-
vanced techniques will provide critical insights into the evolu-
tion of halogenated sulﬁde materials and their role in improving
ASSLSB performance.
