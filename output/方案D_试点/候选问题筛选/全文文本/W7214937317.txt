ABSTRACT
All‐solid‐state lithium–sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) are promising for high‐energy and safe energy storage, yet their practical 
operation remains constrained by the reliance on external stack pressure (usually > 80 MPa, even exceeding 120 MPa). While 
pressure can improve solid–solid contact, it primarily acts as a compensatory component rather than a fundamental solution, 
and introduces substantial penalties in cell‐level energy density and engineering complexity. Here, we present a pressure‐ 
centered perspective and show that the degradation of ASSLSBs under low‐pressure (< 0.1 MPa) conditions originates from a 
chemo‐mechanical mismatch among reaction‐induced volume change, interfacial chemistry, charge transport, and mechanical 
response. This mismatch is commonly reflected as cathode contact loss, interphase growth, and lithium anode instability. On 
this basis, we outline key challenges and design principles for low‐pressure ASSLSBs, focusing on maintaining reaction 
accessibility, constructing adaptive ion‐conducting phases, and stabilizing solid–solid interfaces. Eliminating pressure depen­
dence, rather than accommodating it, is essential for translating ASSLSBs into practical energy storage systems.

Introduction 
Lithium–sulfur batteries offer a promising route toward next‐ 
generation energy storage [1–4]. However, translating their high 
theoretical energy density into practical performance remains a 
persistent challenge. This gap is further amplified in all‐solid‐ 
state lithium–sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs), where the absence of 
liquid electrolyte eliminates the self‐adjusting nature of inter­
facial contact and exposes fundamental limitations in solid– 
solid reaction systems [5–8].
To compensate for the lack of interfacial adaptability, stack 
pressure (typically > 80 MPa and in some cases exceeding 
120 MPa) has been widely adopted as an operational parameter 
in ASSLSBs [9–12]. High pressure can improve solid–solid 
contact and reduce interfacial resistance, enabling considerable 
battery performance under laboratory conditions [13–16]. 
However, this strategy fundamentally relies on external 
mechanical constraint rather than intrinsic material and inter­
facial stability. As a result, performance achieved under high 
pressure does not necessarily reflect the intrinsic stability of the 
system. More importantly, from a practical perspective, such 
pressure dependence introduces substantial penalties in system‐ 
level energy density, mechanical complexity, and scalability. 
Considering the mechanical constraints of practical battery 
modules, we define application‐oriented low‐pressure ASSLSBs

The Role of Stack Pressure in ASSLSBs 
2.1
|
Solid–Solid Contact and Its Reliance on 
Pressure 
In ASSLSBs, stack pressure primarily acts by improving 
physical contact among solid particles and across electrode/ 
electrolyte interfaces [19–22]. In sulfur composite cathodes, ion 
transport relies on the solid electrolyte network, whereas elec­
tron transport is provided by conductive carbon [23, 24]. Sulfur 
conversion can proceed efficiently only when active sulfur 
species remain simultaneously connected to both pathways. 
Because sulfur and Li2S are intrinsically poor ion/electron 
conductors [25, 26], any local loss of contact can rapidly isolate 
active material from the electrochemical reaction.
Applying stack pressure compacts the composite cathode and 
reduces unfilled voids between particles. For relatively soft 
sulfide electrolytes, pressure can also promote local deforma­
tion, thereby increasing the true contact area among sulfur 
particles, carbon additives, and electrolyte particles [27, 28]. 
This improves initial sulfur utilization by lowering contact 
resistance and establishing more continuous ion/electron 
transport pathways. In this sense, pressure is effective because 
the reaction topology in conventional ASSLSB cathodes is 
inherently contact‐dependent [29–31].
However, this pressure‐induced contact is intrinsically 
unstable during cycling. The sulfur cathode undergoes large 
volume changes during repeated S8/Li2S conversion, which 
continuously perturbs the originally compacted particle 
network [32–34]. For this reason, mechanically enforced 
contact cannot be reliably maintained once the system 
evolves beyond its initial state. If the applied pressure is 
insufficient, newly formed gaps, particle separation, and 
local delamination progressively reduce the triple‐phase 
contact area [35, 36]. As a result, part of the sulfur may 
become electron‐accessible but ion‐inaccessible, or ion‐ 
accessible but electron‐inaccessible. Therefore, the role of 
stack pressure is not simply to increase electrode density, 
but to maintain reaction‐accessible contact during dynamic 
volume evolution. More fundamentally, it acts as a tempo­
rary means of sustaining contact, reflecting the intrinsic 
fragility of contact‐dependent reaction networks rather than 
providing a robust solution for practical operation.
2.2
|
Pressure‐Modulated Interfacial Impedance 
and Reaction Boundaries 
Beyond particle‐level contact, stack pressure also influences 
interfacial impedance and the spatial distribution of reaction 
boundaries. In ASSLSBs, charge transfer occurs mainly at solid– 
solid interfaces, including the internal interfaces of the com­
posite cathode (sulfur/carbon, sulfur/electrolyte, carbon/ 
electrolyte), cathode/electrolyte, electrolyte layers, and Li/solid‐ 
state electrolyte (SSE) interfaces [37–39]. These interfaces 
are fundamentally different from liquid–electrolyte systems 
because they cannot be continuously wetted or repaired by 
electrolyte infiltration [40, 41]. Their electrochemical behavior, 
therefore, depends strongly on the real contact area established 
by mechanical compression [42, 43].
Stack pressure can increase the true contact area at these in­
terfaces and reduce local constriction resistance [44]. This helps 
distribute current more uniformly, mitigate local polarization, 
and improve interfacial charge‐transfer kinetics. At the 
cathode/electrolyte and Li/SSE interfaces, better mechanical 
contact can facilitate Li+ transport [45, 46]. Within the cathode, 
improved particle contact can expand the effective reaction 
region from a few isolated contact points to a more connected 
interfacial network [47]. On the anode side, enhanced Li/SSE 
contact can also homogenize Li+ flux and reduce local current 
concentration, thereby mitigating void formation during 
Li stripping and unstable Li deposition during subsequent 
plating [48, 49].
Nevertheless, stack pressure does not eliminate interfacial 
instability, but only mechanically suppresses its expression. If 
the interfacial chemistry is unstable or if the electrode under­
goes repeated volume change, high pressure may temporarily 
mask contact degradation rather than solve it. In addition, since 
each solid material has its own pressure tolerance limit, overly 
constrained interfaces may accumulate local stress, which 
can promote microcracking, delamination, or heterogeneous 
current distribution during cycling [50, 51]. Thus, the interfacial 
role of pressure should be understood as a trade‐off, 
where reduced impedance is achieved at the expense of mask­
ing intrinsic instability. More fundamentally, the apparent 
improvement in interfacial kinetics under pressure arises from 
externally enforced contact rather than intrinsically stable in­
terfaces, and therefore cannot be sustained under practical low‐ 
pressure operation.

2.3
|
The Effect of Stack Pressure on Sulfur 
Conversion 
The influence of stack pressure on sulfur conversion is pri­
marily kinetic and interfacial rather than thermodynamic. Stack 
pressure is not expected to fundamentally redefine the intrinsic 
sulfur redox sequence. Instead, it regulates the mechanical 
and transport environment in which successive conversion 
reactions proceed. By increasing the effective contact area, 
homogenizing the local overpotential distribution, and reducing 
interfacial resistance, appropriate pressure can lower polariza­
tion and improve the practical utilization of sulfur [52, 53]. This 
is especially important in solid‐state systems, where sulfur 
conversion is restricted by sluggish Li+ transport, limited elec­
tron access, and narrow solid–solid reaction boundaries [54].
Current evidence suggests that solid‐state sulfur conversion can 
involve complex discharge products, including mixed Li2S2/Li2S 
phases [9, 55, 56]. However, this behavior may not be universal, 
as the sulfur‐conversion pathway depends on the electrolyte 
chemistry, interface structure, and operating conditions 
[57–59]. The distribution of these products is strongly influ­
enced by factors such as cutoff voltage, catalytic environment, 
and interfacial chemistry [60–62]. The extent to which stack 
pressure selects a fundamentally different sulfur reaction 
pathway nevertheless remains unclear. Nevertheless, stack 
pressure may influence the apparent reaction pathway by al­
tering the continuity of solid–solid reaction boundaries, the 
spatial distribution of local overpotentials, and the electro­
chemical accessibility of intermediate sulfur species.
Accordingly, reducing the stack pressure from a high value, 
such as 100 MPa, to below 0.1 MPa may increase reaction het­
erogeneity and restrict the completion of successive reduction 
steps. This could decrease the overall conversion depth and 
increase the apparent fraction of kinetically trapped Li2S2‐like 
species relative to fully reduced Li2S. However, direct pressure‐ 
resolved measurements of the Li2S2/Li2S ratio under otherwise 
identical cell conditions remain scarce. This possibility should 
therefore be regarded as a plausible pressure‐dependent kinetic 
effect rather than an established universal trend.
This pressure‐dependent kinetic effect is particularly relevant 
for Li2S‐based cathodes. During charging, the conversion of Li2S 
to S is accompanied by volume shrinkage [63–65]. When the 
stack pressure is insufficient, volume shrinkage during charging 
can generate gaps within the cathode or at the cathode/current‐ 
collector interface, leading to disrupted electronic percolation 
and reduced reversible capacity [66, 67]. This again highlights 
that the apparent improvement in sulfur utilization under 
pressure originates from mechanically maintained contact and 
transport pathways, rather than from a direct alteration of the 
intrinsic thermodynamics of sulfur redox chemistry. Conse­
quently, sulfur‐conversion behavior observed under high stack 
pressure may partly reflect pressure‐assisted interfacial contact 
and transport kinetics and may not fully represent the apparent 
redox pathway under application‐oriented low‐pressure condi­
tions. Overall, stack pressure primarily regulates the accessi­
bility and completeness of successive solid‐state conversion 
steps rather than fundamentally changing the intrinsic sulfur 
redox sequence.
2.4
|
Pressure Trade‐Off Between Sulfur Cathode 
and Li‐Metal Anode 
Although stack pressure is beneficial for maintaining cathode 
contact and promoting sulfur utilization, its effect on the Li‐ 
metal anode is more complex and can even become detrimental. 
In sulfur cathodes, external pressure compensates for the loss of 
solid–solid contact caused by conversion‐induced volume 
changes. However, at the Li/SSE interface, excessive pressure 
may intensify Li creep, interfacial deformation, and local stress 
concentration [68, 69]. Because Li metal is mechanically soft, 
sustained pressure can drive plastic flow of Li into defects, 
pores, grain boundaries, or mechanically weak regions of the 
solid electrolyte [70, 71]. This behavior may increase the risk of 
short circuits, especially when local current concentration and 
interfacial flaws coexist.
This creates an intrinsic pressure dilemma in ASSLSBs. The 
sulfur cathode requires sufficient pressure to preserve reaction‐ 
accessible contact, whereas the Li‐metal anode requires limited 
and homogeneous pressure to avoid pressure‐driven deforma­
tion and interfacial instability. Therefore, the optimal stack 
pressure cannot be determined solely from cathode utilization 
or initial impedance reduction. It must reflect a full‐cell com­
promise among cathode contact retention, Li/SSE interfacial 
stability, Li creep resistance, and system‐level energy density. 
Increasing pressure may improve the cathode‐side reaction 
environment but simultaneously narrow the mechanical safety 
margin of the Li‐metal anode. This trade‐off further supports 
the necessity of low‐pressure ASSLSBs.
2.5
|
The Hidden Costs and Defects of Excessive 
Pressure 
Although high stack pressure can improve solid–solid contact 
and reduce interfacial resistance in the short term, it may also 
lead to an overestimation of the intrinsic stability of ASSLSBs. A 
cell that delivers higher capacity under high pressure does not 
necessarily possess robust materials or stable interfaces, but 
rather reflects the effectiveness of external mechanical con­
straint, thereby introducing hidden mechanistic costs. External 
pressure can artificially suppress pore expansion, particle sep­
aration, and localized contact loss, thereby concealing the un­
derlying chemo‐mechanical incompatibility between electrodes 
and solid electrolytes [72–74]. Once the pressure is reduced or 
once cycling‐induced volume changes accumulate during long‐ 
term operation, these masked failure processes can re‐emerge. 
Therefore, performance obtained only under high stack pres­
sure should be interpreted with caution, as it may reflect the 
effectiveness of external mechanical constraint rather than the 
true durability of the cell chemistry and architecture.
From a practical perspective, this pressure dependence also 
introduces a substantial system‐level penalty. In ideal labora­
tory configurations, stack pressure is often treated as a simple 
operating parameter for compacting composite cathodes, 
maintaining interparticle contact and sustaining sulfur con­
version (Figure 1A). However, in pouch cells or modules, 
pressure is not a free parameter, meaning the existence of 
hidden engineering‐related costs. Maintaining a constant load

external mechanical constraint. The applied pressure forces solid

mechanical compatibility is poor. Under low‐pressure conditions, 
this external compensation is weakened. As a result, the ASSLSBs 
must rely entirely on intrinsic structural adaptability and interfacial 
stability. To avoid treating chemo‐mechanical mismatch as an 
overly generalized explanation, it should be resolved across mea­
surable length scales. At the material level, relevant descriptors 
include reaction‐induced volumetric strain, elastic or shear modu­
lus, yield behavior, and fracture toughness, which determine 
whether deformation is accommodated or released through crack­

evolution of contact and chemical stability. At the cell level,

mismatch.
Once reaction‐induced volume evolution cannot be accommo­
dated by the solid framework, stress accumulation, contact loss, 
void formation, interphase growth, and current‐density local­
ization emerge in a coupled manner. Therefore, low pressure is 
not the root cause of degradation. It reveals and amplifies the 
intrinsic mismatch between electrochemical conversion, inter­
facial chemistry, and mechanical response.
From this perspective, the major failure mechanisms in low‐ 
pressure ASSLSBs can be unified within a multiscale and ex­
perimentally testable framework. On the cathode side, chemo‐ 
mechanical mismatch manifests as contact loss and sulfur 
underutilization. At reactive interfaces, it appears as interphase 
growth and kinetic polarization. On the anode side, it is ex­
pressed as void formation, unstable Li stripping/deposition, and 
dendrite‐related risks. These processes are strongly inter­
dependent (Figure 2), but each can be associated with specific 
mechanical, structural, and electrochemical observables. Con­
tact loss constricts the available transport pathways, leading to 
intensified local polarization and accelerated interphase 
growth. The resulting interphase further impedes Li+ transport 
and deteriorates interfacial contact, thereby driving a self‐ 
reinforcing degradation loop.
3.1
|
Contact Loss and Sulfur Underutilization 
On the cathode side, chemo‐mechanical mismatch first appears 
as the inability of the composite sulfur electrode to maintain 
continuous solid–solid contact during repeated conversion 
reactions. Sulfur and Li2S are both poor ionic and electronic 
conductors, so their reversible utilization strongly depends on 
continuous triple‐phase contact among active material, solid 
electrolyte, and conductive carbon [80, 81]. During cycling, the 
large volume change associated with sulfur conversion contin­
uously perturbs this contact network. Whether this deformation 
can be accommodated depends on the effective modulus, yield 
behavior, and fracture toughness of the composite cathode. If 
the cathode framework lacks sufficient compliance, particle 
separation and interfacial delamination occur more readily. 
Once the ion or electron pathway is locally disrupted, the cor­
responding sulfur species become electrochemically isolated, 
even if they remain physically present in the electrode.
This issue becomes more severe in high‐sulfur‐loading and 
high‐areal‐capacity cathodes. Thicker electrodes create longer 
ion‐transport pathways and more uneven pressure distribution 
[82]. Under low pressure, surface regions may still remain 
partially active, whereas the inner regions are more prone to ion 
depletion, stress accumulation, and contact loss. Therefore, the 
decrease in sulfur utilization is not only related to sluggish 
Li2S2/Li2S conversion, but also to the growing fraction of 
inactive sulfur caused by insufficient triple‐phase contact. The 
severity of this degradation can be evaluated through changes in 
electrode thickness, porosity, true contact‐area fraction, inter­
facial gap or void fraction, ionic and electronic conductivity, 
and cell impedance. Moreover, contact loss exhibits a self‐ 
reinforcing behavior. Once local contact is degraded, current 
and electrochemical reactions are redistributed to the remain­
ing connected regions, leading to increased local reaction het­
erogeneity and stress concentration. This further accelerates 
delamination and pore growth, thereby amplifying structural 
instability during cycling. Thus, contact loss is not merely a 
static mechanical defect. It is the cathode‐side expression of 
chemo‐mechanical mismatch. This can be verified by the pro­
gressive loss of contact area, growth of internal gaps, increased 
transport resistance, and spatially heterogeneous sulfur utili­
zation. For low‐pressure ASSLSBs, the key design target should 
shift from maximizing initial sulfur utilization to maintaining a 
cathode topology that can preserve ion, electron, and active‐ 
material connectivity throughout cycling.

Interphase Growth and Kinetic Polarization 
The same mismatch also appears at reactive solid–solid inter­
faces as interphase growth and kinetic polarization. Sulfide 
solid electrolytes are widely used in ASSLSBs because of their 
high Li+ conductivity and good compactability [83–85]. How­
ever, their electrochemical stability window is limited. When 
sulfide electrolytes are mixed with sulfur species and conduc­
tive carbon, numerous chemically active interfaces are formed 
[86]. These interfaces are exposed to local potential gradients, 
nonuniform current distributions, and repeated mechanical 
disturbance during cycling. Consequently, interfacial reactions 
become strongly coupled with mechanical instability.

local electrochemical inhomogeneity. The true contact‐area

As charge transfer becomes confined to fewer active contact

local polarization can accelerate electrolyte decomposition and

thickness and composition, impedance growth, and changes in

Therefore, interphase growth under low pressure should not be 
regarded solely as a consequence of the limited electrochemical 
stability of sulfide electrolytes. Rather, it represents a chemical 
manifestation of chemo‐mechanical mismatch. Contact loss 
induces current constriction, thereby intensifying local polariza­

interphase subsequently impedes ion transport and further dete­
riorates interfacial contact. This self‐reinforcing feedback loop 
couples chemical degradation with mechanical instability and 
explains why rapid capacity decay under low pressure cannot be 
attributed simply to insufficient external pressure. Experimentally, 
this coupling can be examined by correlating impedance evolution 
and polarization with cross‐sectional imaging of contact loss and 
spectroscopic analysis of interphase growth. Accordingly, a stable 
low‐pressure sulfur cathode must not only suppress parasitic 
reactions but also maintain a mechanically robust and chemically 
compatible reaction interface.

removed from the Li/SSE interface. If interfacial adhesion is 
insufficient or Li replenishment from the bulk metal is too slow, 
voids form at the interface [87–89]. These voids sharply reduce 
the true contact area between the Li anode and SSE. The degree 
of anode‐side mismatch can therefore be described using the

This issue becomes more critical as ASSLSBs move toward 
high‐energy‐density designs, including thin Li, Li‐deficient, 
alloyed, or anode‐free configurations. These configurations 
represent distinct design routes and differ in their technological 
maturity in ASSLSBs. Thin Li and Li‐deficient designs pri­
marily reduce the excess lithium inventory. Alloy anodes rely 
on alternative lithium‐storage mechanisms, and anode‐free 
cells depend on reversible in situ lithium plating, which 
imposes particularly stringent requirements on interfacial sta­
bility and cycling reversibility. However, all of them reduce 
inactive Li inventory but make the anode more sensitive to void 
formation and irreversible Li loss [90–92]. Increasing stack 
pressure can temporarily improve physical contact, but it 
cannot fundamentally eliminate void formation. The critical 
conditions are governed by interfacial adhesion, Li vacancy 
migration, Li bulk diffusion, local current density, and the 
mechanical compliance of the interfacial layer. These factors 
can be assessed through adhesion or interfacial‐fracture mea­

ance, void‐fraction evolution, and plating/stripping efficiency. 
Therefore, alloyed anodes, Li‐affinitive coatings, artificial SEI 
layers, and multilayer functional interlayers are essential not 
only for suppressing side reactions but also for mitigating the 
mismatch between Li volume evolution, interfacial mechanics, 
and Li+ transport. The preferred stack pressure is system‐ 
dependent and should be optimized according to the specific 
cell chemistry, architecture, and operating conditions.

Design Principles for Low‐Pressure ASSLSBs 
via Regulating Chemo‐Mechanical Mismatch 
4.1
|
Preserving Reaction Accessibility in Sulfur 
Cathodes 
The first failure under low pressure typically manifests in the 
sulfur cathode, where chemo‐mechanical mismatch reduces 
reaction accessibility. In this work, reaction accessibility refers 
to the ability of sulfur species to maintain continuous ionic and 
electronic transport pathways throughout cycling. It can be 
evaluated through sulfur utilization, dQ/dV peak retention, 
impedance evolution, and the spatial homogeneity of sulfur‐ 
conversion products.
Conventional S/C/SSE composite cathodes rely heavily on dis­
crete triple‐phase contact points among sulfur, conductive car­
bon, and solid electrolyte, making the reaction topology 
intrinsically fragile [93]. The preservation of triple‐phase con­
tact can be assessed through conductivity measurements, in­
terfacial impedance, and structural characterization of contact‐ 
area evolution. During cycling, sulfur conversion induces large 
volume changes, and even minor particle separation can disrupt 
either ion or electron transport pathways. Once this occurs, 
sulfur may remain physically present but becomes electro­
chemically isolated. Therefore, the key design principle is not 
merely to increase conductive carbon or solid electrolyte con­
tent, but to preserve continuous ion/electron accessibility 
within a mechanically stable reaction network throughout cy­
cling. Here, a mechanically stable reaction network denotes a 
cathode architecture that accommodates reaction‐induced 
deformation without disrupting transport continuity. Its effec­
tiveness can be evaluated through electrode‐thickness variation, 
contact retention, internal‐gap formation, and long‐term elec­
trochemical performance.
Porous and confined cathode architectures address this failure 
by stabilizing sulfur within a structurally resilient conductive 
framework [94]. When sulfur or Li2S is embedded within me­
soporous hosts, the electron‐transport distance is shortened, 
and the active material is more likely to remain physically 
integrated with the conductive framework during volume evo­
lution [95–97]. Such designs effectively couple transport path­
ways with mechanical confinement, reducing the probability of 
active‐material isolation. Consequently, preserved reaction 
accessibility should be reflected not only by high sulfur utili­
zation but also by limited impedance growth and sustained 
electrochemical signatures during prolonged cycling. This 
concept is exemplified by the Li2S‐LiI composite infiltrated into 
a mesoporous carbon framework reported by Jiang et al. [98] 
(Figure 3A), where ion transport, electron conduction, and 
structural retention are integrated within a three‐dimensional 
mixed‐conductive network. Importantly, the value of this 
approach lies not in maximizing initial capacity, but in main­
taining reaction accessibility under repeated deformation.
However, preserving reaction accessibility cannot be achieved 
by maximizing electronic conductivity alone. Highly conductive 
carbon improves electron percolation, but can also accelerate 
sulfide electrolyte decomposition at reactive interfaces. Ex­
cessive electronic connectivity enlarges electrochemically active 
carbon/sulfide contact areas and facilitates electron leakage into 
the sulfide electrolyte, thereby promoting parasitic reduction 
reactions, interphase growth, and local polarization. The work 
by Jin et al. [99], employing polar yet electronically insulating 
mesoporous hosts, demonstrates that chemical affinity toward 
S/Li2S is equally critical (Figure 3B). This highlights that cath­
ode design must balance transport accessibility, interfacial 
chemical compatibility, and structural stability against contact 
loss, rather than optimizing conductivity in isolation.
A more fundamental route is to directly reduce the reliance on 
the narrow triple‐phase boundaries of the cathode. Mixed ion‐ 
electron conductor (MIEC), as demonstrated by Wang et al. 
[93], extends sulfur conversion from isolated S/C/SSE junctions 
to broader sulfur‐MIEC interfaces (Figure 3C). This shifts the

Building Adaptive Ion‐Conducting Phases to 
Prevent Deformation‐Induced Transport Failure 
Electrolyte‐related failure under low pressure is not simply caused 
by insufficient bulk ionic conductivity [100]. More fundamentally, 
it arises from the inability of the ion‐conducting phase to maintain 
continuous contact with deforming electrodes and evolving solid– 
solid interfaces [101–103]. During sulfur conversion, the cathode 
experiences large and heterogeneous volume changes, while Li 
stripping/deposition on the anode side also alters local interfacial 
pressure [104]. If the solid electrolyte is too rigid, these dimen­
sional changes can be converted into cracks, voids, delamination, 
and discontinuous Li+ pathways. Therefore, the design principle 
of the electrolyte in low‐pressure ASSLSBs is that it should not be 
regarded only as a Li+ conductor. It must also function as a 
mechanical buffer and interfacial adaptor that preserves transport 
continuity during cycling.
This requirement explains why sulfide electrolytes are widely 
used in ASSLSBs. Compared with oxide electrolytes, sulfides are 
softer and more easily compacted, which helps establish better 
solid–solid contact during cell assembly [105, 106]. However, 
low modulus alone does not guarantee low‐pressure stability 
[107]. The deformation of both the sulfur cathode and Li‐metal 
anode is dynamic, spatially heterogeneous, and cyclically 
accumulated. As a result, an electrolyte that forms good initial 
contact may still progressively lose adhesion during operation. 
Thus, an effective electrolyte under low pressure must combine 
sufficient room‐temperature Li+ conductivity with local de­
formability, interfacial adhesion, and chemical compatibility 
toward sulfur species and discharge products.
Polymer‐modified sulfide electrolytes represent one route to 
this design principle. The disulfide‐linked Li3PS4 polymer 
electrolyte developed by Kato et al. [108] illustrates that sulfide‐ 
based ion conductors can be structurally modified to introduce 
flexibility without completely sacrificing Li+ transport. More 
importantly, this demonstrates that ion‐conducting networks 
can be designed to accommodate interfacial deformation 
rather than acting as brittle, pressure‐dependent components 
(Figure 4A). Though it shows a Li‐In/Li3PS4/NMC cell rather 
than an ASSLSB, it is included to illustrate how polymer 
modification can enhance the mechanical compliance and in­
terfacial adaptability of sulfide electrolytes, offering a transfer­
able design concept for low‐pressure ASSLSBs.
Halide electrolytes and halide‐based composites provide a 
complementary approach. Although their greatest advantages 
in low‐pressure ASSLSBs lie in their use as localized functional 
phases at present, recent developments suggest that broader

Stabilizing Solid–Solid Interfaces for 
Sustained Interfacial Reactions 
At low stack pressure, solid–solid interfaces are no longer 
passively held together by external compression, making in­
terfacial stability the primary determinant of sustained 
electrochemical reactions. Once adhesion is weakened or 
resistive products accumulate at local interfaces, the reaction 
is forced to proceed through fewer remaining contact points, 
leading to current constriction, increased local current 
density, and accelerated polarization [118–120]. Therefore, 
the key design principle is to stabilize and maintain 
continuous interfacial contact so that ion transport, chemical 
compatibility, and mechanical integrity are preserved 
throughout cycling.
On the cathode side, this principle first applies to the active 
material/SSE interface. In mechanically mixed sulfur cathodes, 
sulfur or Li2S particles contact the solid electrolyte through 
discontinuous junctions, which are highly susceptible to dis­
ruption under conversion‐induced volume changes. Peng et al. 
[121] addressed this issue by forming an in‐situ Li3PS4 layer on 
Li2S particles through the interfacial reaction between Li2S and 
P2S5 (Figure 5A). This surface layer provides a more continuous 
Li+‐transport pathway around the active particles and trans­
forms random particle‐level contacts into a more integrated 
Li2S/SSE interface. The key function of this design is to preserve 
reaction continuity after structural perturbation, rather than 
merely reducing initial impedance.
Another useful strategy is to introduce reconfigurable inter­
facial phases that can recover after contact damage. Liu's sulfur 
iodide cathode provides such an example [122]. Because of its 
relatively low melting point, the sulfur iodide phase can par­
tially repair damaged interfaces through periodic melting

Outlook 
Overall, research on low‐pressure ASSLSBs is moving from 
electrochemical feasibility demonstration toward practical 
chemo‐mechanical design. Early studies often relied on high 
stack pressure to maintain contact, reduce polarization, and 
improve sulfur utilization [13, 126, 127]. However, such 
pressure‐assisted performance does not necessarily prove 
that the cell architecture is intrinsically compatible with 
low‐pressure operation. In fact, it may obscure the very failure 
mechanisms that determine whether an ASSLSB can operate 
under practical conditions. The key limitation is not a single 
material property, but the chemo‐mechanical mismatch among 
reaction‐induced volume evolution, solid–solid contact, charge 
transport, interphase chemistry, and cell‐level mechanical 
constraints. Under reduced pressure, these factors no longer 
remain independently limiting, but instead reinforce one 
another, leading to cathode contact loss, sulfur underutilization, 
interphase 
growth, 
kinetic 
polarization, 
and 
Li‐anode 
instability. Therefore, future research should move beyond 
optimizing isolated materials under externally constrained 
conditions. Low‐pressure operation should instead be treated as 
a defining engineering constraint that guides material selection, 
interfacial regulation, and electrode construction. Only by 
designing materials and architectures around this constraint 
can ASSLSBs move from pressure‐assisted laboratory perform­
ance toward intrinsically sustainable cell operation. Based on 
this perspective, several key directions deserve focused atten­
tion (Figure 6).

Spatially Regulated Sulfur Conversion for 
Reducing Local Chemo‐Mechanical Strain 
An advanced future direction is to regulate not only the 
chemical identity of sulfur discharge products, but also their 
spatial distribution within thick composite cathodes. Previous 
work has demonstrated that the discharge product of ASSLSBs 
is a Li2S2/Li2S mixture rather than pure Li2S, and that pro­
moting a Li2S2‐dominant product can improve reversibility and 
cycling stability [9, 55, 128]. This finding provides an important 
mechanistic basis while raising a new question that is highly 
relevant to low‐pressure operation: Is the desired Li2S2/Li2S 
distribution maintained uniformly across a thick, high‐loading 
sulfur cathode?
Under low‐pressure conditions, sulfur conversion is unlikely to 
proceed homogeneously throughout the electrode. In thick 
cathodes, Li+/electron transport and local contact pressure are 
unevenly distributed across the electrode thickness [129, 130]. 
Regions close to the solid electrolyte layer may experience faster 
lithiation, while regions close to the current collector may suffer 
from ion limitation. Localized current constriction and inter­
facial polarization can further promote nonuniform Li2S2/Li2S 
formation. Therefore, even if the overall discharge product is 
Li2S2‐dominant, local regions may still undergo excessive con­
version to Li2S, generating larger local volume expansion and 
stronger chemo‐mechanical strain. These local Li2S‐rich 
domains may become the initiation sites for contact loss, 
interphase thickening, and irreversible sulfur isolation. There­
fore, controlling the spatial distribution of sulfur conversion 
may be more important than optimizing the average redox 
pathway alone.
Future research should thus move from average redox‐pathway 
control to spatially resolved conversion management. The key 
objective is to construct sulfur cathodes in which the depth of 
conversion and the Li2S2/Li2S ratio are spatially regulated to 
minimize local strain concentration. This can be implemented 
by designing cathodes with gradient catalytic activity, gradient

mechanical stress regulates interphase chemistry inside sulfur 
composite cathodes. In sulfide‐based ASSLSBs, interphase for­

pressure operation, these interfaces never evolve under 
mechanically neutral conditions. Local pressure, particle 
rearrangement, contact loss, and current constriction continu­

[15, 133, 134]. Therefore, interphase growth should be regarded 
as a stress‐dependent interfacial reaction, rather than a purely 
chemical decomposition reaction.

optimization. The key question is not only whether sulfide 
electrolytes decompose in contact with sulfur or carbon, but 
how their decomposition pathways and products are altered by 
local mechanical states. Highly compressed contact points may

Full‐Cell‐Coupled Li Anode Design Based on 
Vacancy Flux and Stress Fluctuation 
The Li anode in ASSLSBs should no longer be studied as an 
isolated Li/SSE interface. Under low‐pressure conditions, anode 
failure is strongly coupled with full‐cell volume evolution and 
polarization. During discharge, sulfur lithiation causes cathode 
expansion, whereas Li stripping leads to an even more pro­
nounced volume loss at the Li‐metal anode. Although the exact 
efficiency of stress transfer between the two electrodes remains 
unclear, the overall stack pressure often decreases during dis­
charge, indicating that cathode breathing and Li consumption 
do not mechanically compensate each other in a simple 
manner [135, 136]. Instead, their asynchronous volume changes 
redistribute stress throughout the cell stack and generate local 
pressure heterogeneity at both electrode/electrolyte interfaces. 
Meanwhile, cathode contact loss and interphase growth 
increase full‐cell polarization, further amplifying current 
inhomogeneity at the Li anode. As a result, void formation at 
the Li/SSE interface is not simply an anode‐local problem, nor 
is it solely induced by cathode‐side degradation. It should be 
viewed as the anode‐side manifestation of full‐cell chemo‐ 
mechanical mismatch, where coupled cathode expansion, Li‐ 
metal volume loss, interfacial stress redistribution, and polar­
ization jointly destabilize Li stripping/deposition.
A key future direction is therefore to regulate Li vacancy flux 
under realistic full‐cell operating conditions. During Li strip­
ping, voids form as Li removal at the interface is faster than Li 
replenishment from the bulk metal, or when the Li/SSE inter­
face lacks sufficient adhesion. The critical parameters include 
interfacial adhesion, Li vacancy migration and aggregation, Li 
self‐diffusion, local current density, and interlayer compliance. 
Although these factors have been individually studied, they are 
rarely considered under coupled conditions of sulfur cathode 
volume fluctuation and low‐pressure operation, which ulti­
mately define practical performance.
Several experimentally feasible strategies can be considered. 
First, ultrathin lithiophilic interfacial layers (e.g., Ag, Zn, Mg, or 
Sn nanolayers) should be designed as flux‐regulation layers 
rather than bulk alloy anodes, enabling uniform Li stripping/ 
deposition without introducing excessive voltage penalty or 
inactive mass. Second, adhesive ion‐conducting interlayers with 
moderate mechanical compliance can stabilize Li/SSE contact 
during stripping while avoiding brittle fracture. Third, light­
weight three‐dimensional Li‐host architectures may provide 
lateral Li redistribution pathways, thereby reducing local 
vacancy accumulation and current constriction. Crucially, these 
strategies must be evaluated under limited Li inventory rather 
than thick‐Li conditions to reflect realistic cell operation.
As ASSLSBs' designs move toward thin Li, Li‐deficient, or 
anode‐free configurations, the tolerance for void formation 
becomes much lower. In such systems, stable operation cannot 
rely on excess Li inventory or high external pressure. The anode 
must intrinsically maintain interfacial contact, homogeneous Li 
flux, and sufficient Li replenishment during stripping. From the 
perspective of chemo‐mechanical mismatch, future Li anode 
design should shift from static interfacial stabilization to 
dynamic vacancy‐flux regulation under full‐cell stress coupling.

Internal Stress‐Compensating Composites to 
Replace External Pressure Fixtures 
Building on the above discussion of external pressure fixtures, a 
more targeted future direction is to develop cell architectures 
with intrinsic stress‐compensating capability. The aim is not 
simply to reduce the nominal stack pressure, but to internalize 
pressure regulation within the cell architecture itself. Unlike 
externally applied pressure, which provides a global constraint, 
internal stress‐compensating components could respond more 
locally to cathode shrinkage/expansion, Li stripping‐induced 
void formation, and heterogeneous stress evolution during cy­
cling. Such an approach enables pressure to be dynamically 
redistributed within the cell, rather than externally imposed. 
Therefore, lightweight elastic or recoverable components inte­
grated into the cell stack may offer a more practical route to 
maintaining interfacial contact under low stack pressure.

to introduce a thin, lightweight, ionically inactive but mechani­

such as a porous polymer film, elastic carbon framework, or metal

of the pouch cell. In this case, the role of the component is not to 
enhance electrochemical activity but to stabilize the mechanical 
boundary conditions of the cell.
This strategy differs from conventional interlayer or coating

advantage of ASSLSBs. From the perspective of chemo‐

external constraint imposed during operation.

Conclusion 
In summary, stack pressure plays an important role in 
ASSLSBs, but should be regarded as a compensatory parameter 
rather than a fundamental solution for sustaining cell opera­
tion. Recent advances indicate that ASSLSBs are transitioning 
from pressure‐assisted laboratory demonstrations toward 
intrinsically stable chemo‐mechanical systems capable of 
operating under low pressure with high areal capacity and 
practical cell configurations.
The key to low‐pressure operation does not lie in simply reducing 
external pressure, but in designing materials, interfaces, and cell 
architectures that can maintain reaction accessibility and inter­
facial integrity under minimal mechanical constraint. Perform­
ance degradation under low pressure originates from the coupled 
effects of cathode contact loss, interphase growth, and lithium 
anode void formation, all governed by chemo‐mechanical mis­
match. Accordingly, contact‐preserving cathodes, adaptive ion‐ 
conducting phases, stabilized interfaces, and stress‐regulated cell 
architectures represent the core design directions.
Importantly, the viability of ASSLSBs should no longer be 
evaluated by capacity alone, but by their ability to deliver stable 
cycling at room temperature under low stack pressure with 
realistic electrode loading and cell configurations. Looking 
forward, progress will depend on integrated design frameworks 
that couple materials, interfaces, and mechanics. Ultimately, 
eliminating the reliance on external pressure, rather than 
accommodating it, will be essential for realizing practical 
ASSLSBs.
