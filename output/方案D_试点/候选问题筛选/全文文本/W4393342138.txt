Construction of Elastic and Conductive Channels for
High-Rate and High-Areal-Capacity Sulfur Cathodes in
All-Solid-State Lithium–Sulfur Batteries
Ruqin Ma, Yuteng Fan, Yanting Jin, Siyuan Pan, Haoyue Zhong, Yu Luo, Jiabao Gu,
Mingzeng Luo, Yuqi Wu, Wenxuan Hu, Pengzhan Chen, Yu Su, Guanyu Wu, Jiawei Yan,
Jun Gao, Zhengliang Gong, and Yong Yang*
All solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) have attracted signiﬁcant
attention due to their enhanced safety and superior energy density. However,
the considerable volume change during cycling poses a challenge, resulting in
electrochemical-mechanical degradation. To address this issue, polypyrrole
(PPy) is coated on a nitrogen-doped carbon nanotube (NCNT) network to
exhibit suitable conductivity and compatibility with sulﬁde electrolyte.
Functionally, PPy@NCNT provides a continuous and conductive pathway,
reducing the tortuosity of charge transport. Mechanically, operando pressure
measurements and atomic force microscopy (AFM) tests are conducted,
demonstrating that the ﬂexible and viscoelastic structure of PPy@NCNT can
alleviate local stress. The axial pressure on the cathode can be reduced by
11% with a sulfur area capacity of 3 mg cm−2 at 30 °C. At a high areal mass
loading of 4.5 mg cm−2, a high areal capacity of 6 mAh cm−2 (1348 mAh g−1,
1C = 1675 mA g−1) is achieved at 0.1C, maintaining stability over 300 cycles
at 0.2C. Remarkably, it can achieve an areal capacity of over 8.8 mAh cm−2
and stable cycling even at 60 °C. This study, being the ﬁrst to regulate
mechano-electrochemical action in ASSLSBs with ﬂexible conducting polymer,
highlights the signiﬁcance of integrating a continuous and elastic network
into solid-state conversion-type cathodes.

Therefore, it is necessary to design mechanical buﬀering com-
ponents. Furthermore, despite the excellent properties of sulﬁde
SSE, they have some critical drawbacks: chemical instability and
unsatisﬁed mechanical properties. The poor compatibility of sul-

tional consideration in the selection of additives and the process-
ing method of the composite cathode. Presently, there is limited
research on integrating conductive ﬂexible components into sul-
fur cathode with sulﬁde SSE.[22,23] (Here the “conductive ﬂexible
components” do not include polymer electrolytes, as they still in-
duce the dissolution and shuttling of lithium polysulﬁdes, in the
same way as liquid systems.[24]) Only ﬂexible binder has been re-
ported, the addition of which helps to maintain the integrity of the
cathode with the cost of a compromised electronic transport.[25,26]
Overall, the main challenges lie in 1) ﬁnding the compatible ﬂex-
ible component with sulﬁdes SSE, 2) achieving homogenous dis-
persion of conductive agent, and 3) using suitable dispersion pro-
cess to minimize side reaction. The interfacial stability between
the conductive agent and sulﬁdes SSE during electrode prepara-
tion and electrochemical cycling also plays an important role in
battery performance.[22,27]
To fulﬁll these criteria, we have adopted a strategy by coat-
ing polypyrrole (PPy) on hollow nitrogen-doped carbon nan-
otube (NCNT) through a liquid-phase polymerization process.
As shown in Figure 1, this process results in the formation of
a continuous and ﬂexible conductive pathway, which exhibits
excellent chemical compatibility with sulﬁde SSE. Through an-
ionic doping and precise control of polymerization conditions,
PPy@NCNT achieves an optimal level of conductivity while mit-
igating the electrode volumetric variation during cycling. The hi-
erarchical control of PPy@NCNT to sulfur composite cathodes
enables high capacity and long cycling stability, even under high-
loading conditions.
2. Results and Discussion
2.1. Synthesis and Characterization of Composite Cathodes
Figure 2a illustrates the process of preparing the composite cath-
ode labelled as A19P5 (detailed compositions reported in Table 1).
The coating of the mixed conductive and ﬂexible PPy layer onto
NCNT substrate was achieved via in-situ polymerization and an-
ionic doping, leading to the formation of PPy@NCNT composite.
Subsequently, the composite cathode was fabricated using one-
step ball-milling method. The choice of PPy is attributed to its
facile synthesis, unique electronic conductivity, and pronounced
tunability, as highlighted in previous studies.[28,29] Within this
context, an important factor of using PPy is its absence of strongly
polar functional groups such as hydroxyl and carboxylic acid
groups which makes it more compatible with SSE, thus avoid-
ing potential interfacial reactions that could escalate resistance
issues.[30] Moreover, previous study has shown that PPy is struc-
turally stable within the 2–4 V versus Li/Li+, which also aligns
well with the electrochemical window of sulfur.[31] Transmis-
sion electron microscope (TEM) analysis (Figure 2b) shows a
conformal coating via the liquid-phase polymerization process,
yielding a layer thickness of around 5 nm. After the post-
polymerization process, the presence of PPy on the NCNT sur-
face introduces electrostatic repulsion and steric hindrance, ef-
fectively reducing the tendency of NCNTs to clump together.[31]
Therefore, as shown in Figure 2c, PPy@NCNT exhibits excel-
lent dispersion under the compression and friction of the milling
balls. This enables it to serve as a uniform, continuous and con-
ductive pathway within the electrode, thereby minimizing the in-
ﬂuence of solvents on SSE during dispersion. Additionally, the
coating of ﬂexible PPy enhances the mechanical properties of the

2.2. Improved Electrochemical Performance
Composite cathodes were assembled into batteries to investi-
gate the eﬀect of PPy@NCNT incorporation on electrochemi-
cal performance. The initial discharge speciﬁc capacity and ini-
tial Coulombic eﬃciency (CE) at 0.1C are compared across vari-
ous systems (Figure 3a, detailed electrochemical curves in Figure
S6, Supporting Information). Increasing the amount of acety-
lene black (AB) in primary particles (A24) compared to A20
does enhance the initial discharge capacity. This is attributed to
adding more primary conductive particles during high-energy
ball milling, enhancing greater triple-phase contact at the pri-
mary particle level.[47-49] However, this improvement appears lim-
ited in the cycling data (Figure 3b), with no noticeable alteration
in the cycling trend. In contract, incorporating continuous net-
works on the electrode side (A19N5/A19P5) resulted in a higher
initial CE. Particularly, the A19P5 cathode achieves an initial dis-
charge capacity of 1546 mAh g−1, close to the theoretical capacity
of S (1675 mAh g−1), along with an impressive initial CE reach-
ing up to 93.7%. A19P5 exhibits stable cycling within the initial
20 cycles, signifying the favorable inﬂuence of PPy@NCNT on
early-cycle capacity realization. This trend highlights the eﬀec-
tiveness of hierarchical design within the electrode, to be dis-
cussed in Section 2.3. However, the NCNT samples did not ex-
hibit a higher initial speciﬁc capacity. As for long-cycle perfor-
mance, A19N5 displays pronounced decay during the initial 100
cycles (detailed cycling data in Figure S7, Supporting Informa-
tion). This could be attributed partly to the poor mechanical prop-
erties of NCNT after ball milling and pressure. Additionally, for
highly conductive carbon additives,[50] attention is needed for the
electrochemical degradation issue. We also compared the eﬀect
of conductive agents on the electrochemical decomposition of
LPS. Compared to NCNTs, the electrochemical compatibility be-
tween PPy@NCNT and LPS is improved, resulting in less de-
composition of LPS (Figure S8, Supporting Information). Later
in this work, we focus the comparison between A24 and A19P5,
highlighting the signiﬁcance of a proper-conductive continuous
network alongside the role of mechanical relief.

numbers (Figure S9, Supporting Information), an interesting
phenomenon was observed. Here, the diﬀerence in midpoint
voltage (voltage at SOC = 50%) is used to reﬂect the diﬀerence
in overpotential, as shown in Table S1 (Supporting Information).

2.3. Superior Transport Kinetics Boosted by PPy@NCNT
In order to comprehensively elucidate the improved electrochem-
ical performance, explanations from various perspectives were
provided. The primary focus lies in the hierarchical regulation
of conductive network construction. Electronic conductivity as-
sessment of the composite cathode was conducted via direct
current (DC) polarization measurement (detailed information
is presented in Figure S11, Supporting Information). The elec-
tronic conductivity of A19P5 measured at 1.23 × 10−1 S cm−1 no-
tably surpasses that of A24, which recorded 1.35 × 10−2 S cm−1,
marking an improvement of nearly an order of magni-
tude (Figure 5a). In this context, emphasis is placed on
the electronic conductivity of A19N5, which is observed to
lie between that of A24 and A19P5 (as depicted in Figure
S11e,f, Supporting Information). Combined with the frag-
mented NCNTs after ball milling, as shown in Figure S1
(Supporting Information), the lower electronic tortuosity of
A19P5 is attributed to the appropriate mechanical prop-
erties and adequate conductivity of PPy@NCNT. The im-
proved electrochemical results highlight the importance of
incorporation of a continuous and conductive pathway in
thick electrodes to reduces the tortuosity of electronic trans-
port.

2.4. Mechanical Buﬀering Performance with Optimized
PPy@NCNT
To investigate the mechanical properties of PPy@NCNT, com-
prehensive examinations were conducted at both macroscopic
and microscopic levels. Firstly, we assessed the overall mechan-
ical properties. This involved cold pressing the powder onto a
carbon-coated aluminum foil under a pressure of 30 MPa, fol-
lowed by repetitive folding of the electrode. After undergoing
cold pressing, the morphology of the three diﬀerent conductive
agents is shown in Figure S13 (Supporting Information). Un-
der the applied high pressure, most of NCNT are fractured, due
to its higher Young’s modulus, making it less suitable for bat-
tery used under high pressure. In contrast, ﬂexible PPy@NCNT
exhibited a compressed but still continuous morphology. After
multiple folding cycles, both NCNT and AB sheets exhibited dis-
tinctive particle detachment (in Figure 6a,b), which was clearly
observed in FESEM images (use red cycle to label). In contrast,
PPy@NCNT maintained a smooth morphology even after mul-
tiple folding cycles (Figure 6c). In conclusion, PPy@NCNT can
maintain its form after folding and high pressure, which allows
PPy@NCNT to resist localized stress in the composite cathode.
An interfacial adhesion test was conducted using atomic
force microscopy (AFM) to assess the interfacial robustness
and resilience.[53,54] The initial topographical image is shown in
Figure 6d, revealing a relatively smooth surface of PPy@NCNT.
The speciﬁc testing process is shown as Figure S14 (Support-
ing Information), and force curve is depicted in Figure 6e (the
x-axis “separation” takes into account the tip’s movement dis-
tance based on the piezo tube’s motion): In the ﬁrst stage, the
piezo tube descends at a constant rate, gradually approaching
the sample surface. When near the surface of the sample, due
to the interaction forces between the sample and the tip, the nee-
dle tip experiences a jump-to-contact process, causing the force
curve to shift downward. As the tip continues to descend, that
is at point 2 of Figure S14 (Supporting Information), it deforms
the sample, and the probe deﬂects upward. At the same time the
force between the tip and sample increases, and a linear deforma-
tion indicates elastic deformation. According to the Derjaguin-
Müller-Toporov (DMT) model (speciﬁc details are presented in
Note S1, Supporting Information), this corresponds to a Young’s
modulus of 232 MPa. At point 4, upon reaching max force dur-
ing retraction, the needle tip begins to withdraw. The piezo tube
rises at a constant rate, and the force decreases. It can be seen
that the linear change exhibited during retraction largely overlaps
with the extension section, indicating a reversible elastic defor-
mation. Furthermore, during retraction, the maximum attractive
force exceeds that during extension, suggesting there is adhesion
between PPy and the tip.
Topography images (Figure 6f,g) and Young’s modulus
(Figure 6h,i) were obtained through AFM. In the modulus map
(Figure 6h), A24 shows a uniform modulus distribution, with
Young’s modulus values of 457 MPa, 476 MPa, and 464 MPa
for Regions 1–3, which also indicates the uniformity of the com-
posite cathode prepared by the two-step high-energy ball milling
process. With the addition of PPy@NCNT, A19P5 presents dif-
ferent modulus map (Figure 6i): A clearly visible region of low
modulus is found in the upper part, with a width of approxi-
mately 100 nm, matching the width of PPy@NCNT. The aver-
age Young’s modulus values for Regions 1–3 are 216, 324, and
452 MPa, respectively. The Young’s modulus in Region 1 is con-
sistent with the Young’s modulus derived through force curve
analysis, suggesting it corresponds to PPy@NCNT. The Young’s
modulus in Region 3 is close to that of A19P5, corresponding
to the composite cathode. In Region 2, which is at the interface,
the modulus is intermediate. Many previous results have shown
that local compression-induced strain can reduce ion transport
dynamics. The introduction of ﬂexible components will alleviate
the problem of chemical potential heterogeneity caused by local
strain in the solid-state system.[55]

approach provides higher resolution to distinguish diﬀerent pro-
cesses on the time scale. According to previous research, peaks
with time constants in the range of 10−7 to 10−6 s are associ-

from 10−5 to 10−4 s and 10−4 to 10−3 s are attributed to ionic
transport at the interfaces in anode and cathode, respectively.
Additionally, the peaks in the ranges of 10−3 to 10−2 s and 10−2
to 10−1 s correspond to charge transfer processes in the anode
and cathode, respectively, typically represented as Rct in Nyquist
plots.[61–64] These processes are related to the properties of the
electrode itself and the transport environment.[65] In the pris-
tine state, A19P5 exhibits a smaller Rct, which may be attributed
to a smoother electron pathway. After the ﬁrst activation cycle,
sulfur undergoes a complete amorphization process, enhancing
its intrinsic transport properties.[66,67] Consequently, the charge
transfer impedance of both samples decreases. However, A19P5
consistently maintains a lower level of Rct, thanks to its better
volume adaptability, which preserves the initial continuous con-
ductive pathway. After 20 cycles, mechanical damage gradually
accumulates in the A24 composite cathode, leading to a mis-
match between the composite cathode and the SE pellet, which
severely impedes ion transport. In contrast, for A19P5, the buﬀer-
ing eﬀect of PPy@NCNT prevents the appearance of signiﬁcant
cracks in the composite cathode throughout the entire cycling
process, ensuring smooth electron transport and stable capac-
ity. This is also supported by the results of the CV stability test

chanical properties are summarized. Supported by hollow NCNT,
PPy tightly wraps around the NCNT network, enhancing adhe-
sion through increased interfacial surface area and enhanced 𝜋–
𝜋interactions. At the same time, the surface-ﬂexible PPy has
a low Young’s modulus, allowing it to deform in response to
changes in the volume of the surrounding active material. Dur-
ing sulfur discharge to form Li2S, it undergoes compression, and
during charging, it rebounds, maintaining electrical contact, un-
like the rigid AB which loses electrical contact after expansion.
3. Conclusion
In conclusion, an eﬀective way of improving the electronic con-
ductivity while minimizing the interfacial side reactions with
sulﬁde electrolyte was found via adding a nitrogen-doped car-
bon nanotube (NCNT) network coated with polypyrrole (PPy) by
liquid-phase polymerization. Our ﬁndings highlight that the in-
corporation of a ﬂexible and conductive pathway at the electrode
level in ASSLSBs can eﬀectively alleviate initial electrochemical-
mechanical losses during cycling. Consequently, the A19P5 cath-
ode exhibits a notable initial improved CE and reversible capac-
ity with a sulfur loading range of 1.5 to 4.5 mg cm−2 at room
temperature. This approach is promising to be applied in other
conversion-type solid state battery systems. When combined with
proper conductive and mechanical stability-enhancing strategies,
it has big potential to achieve both high-capacity performance
and enduring cycling stability. Furthermore, future research in
this ﬁeld could focus on the more precise design of conductive
networks and ﬂexible/elastic layers to further enhance electrode
performance.
