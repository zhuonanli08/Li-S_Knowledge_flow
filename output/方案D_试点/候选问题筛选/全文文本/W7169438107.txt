ABSTRACT
All-solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) hold immense promise for next-generation safe and high-energy storage, yet
their power capability is fundamentally limited by sluggish solid-state sulfur redox kinetics. While catalysis is effective in
accelerating these reactions, we reveal for the first time that under overlapping potentials, such catalytic acceleration inevitably
triggers severe parasitic decomposition of sulfide solid-state electrolytes (SSEs), a critical yet overlooked bottleneck that disrupts
Li+ transport and limits high-rate performance. To decouple efficient sulfur conversion from interfacial degradation, we devise
a spatially selective catalyst architecture. Precisely sized cobalt clusters are confined within an ultra-microporous carbon host.
This design ensures intimate sulfur-catalyst contact for rapid conversion while physically isolating the catalytic surfaces from the
bulk SSE, thereby suppressing its catalytic decomposition. Consequently, the continuous Li+ transport network is preserved. The
resulting ASSLSB achieves an unprecedented room-temperature rate capability, stably cycling at an ultrahigh current density of
25.0 mA cm−2 (15 C) for over 15,000 cycles. This work provides a general design principle for reconciling catalysis with interfacial
stability in high-power solid-state batteries.
1
Introduction
All-solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) have garnered
interest as the next-generation energy storage paradigm for
electric vehicles and drones due to the high energy density
and intrinsic safety [1–3]. However, the inherently sluggish
solid-phase sulfur conversion reactions lead to severe interfacial
impedance, making it difficult for ASSLSBs to operate at high
sulfur loading and high current densities [4–7]. This kinetic
bottleneck prevents them from meeting practical applications
such as vertical take-off and landing in electric aviation, thereby
posing a major obstacle to their large-scale deployment [8, 9].

To address this challenge, considerable efforts have been devoted
to lowering the activation barrier of solid-state sulfur reactions
through catalytic strategies, which have indeed improved battery
performance [10, 11]. For example, various sulfides and halides,
serving as solid-state catalysts within the composite cathode,
furnish a viable solution to accelerate sulfur conversion kinetics
and thus substantially elevate sulfur utilization [12–16].
However, an often-overlooked issue arises from the fact that the
most promising sulfide-based solid-state electrolytes (SSEs) (with
ionic conductivity >10 mS cm−1) share a similar electrochemical
stability window with sulfur, around 1.08 to 2.24 V versus Li/Li+
[17–19]. Although its reduction kinetics are much slower than the
solid-state reduction of sulfur, the primary structural framework
of sulfide-based SSEs, namely the PS4
3−tetrahedral units, still
tends to undergo partial decomposition during discharge, leading
to the formation of a passivated layer with reduced species,
thereby intrinsically impeding continuous ionic transfer [20].
More critically, this overlap in electrochemical windows implies
that strategies aimed at accelerating sulfur conversion, such as
catalyst incorporation, inevitably enhance the electrochemical
activity of SSE decomposition as well, resulting in non-negligible
electrolyte consumption and further deterioration of ionic trans-
port pathways. As a result, even with catalytic assistance,
ASSLSBs still struggle to achieve high-rate charge/discharge per-
formance, as rapid cycling fundamentally requires uninterrupted
and stable Li+ transport across solid-solid interfaces. Therefore,
the design of catalysts and architectures for cathodes in sulfide-
based ASSLSBs must simultaneously enable efficient catalysis
of solid-state sulfur conversion while suppressing the parasitic
catalytic decomposition of the SSE, which is crucial for breaking
the high-rate cycling bottleneck of ASSLSBs.
In this work, we reveal that catalytically driven electrolyte decom-
position is one of the factors contributing to the degradation of
the ASSLBs (Figure 1). To address this contradiction, we design a
cobalt (Co) cluster catalyst model with precise size control, which
sustains catalytic activity while suppressing the decomposition
of SSEs. We found that the catalyst size governs the extent of
electrolyte degradation, that is, as the catalyst size decreases, the
large number of exposed active sites not only accelerates solid-
state sulfur conversion but simultaneously catalyzes parasitic
decomposition of the sulfide SSEs, thereby undermining the
overall stability of Li+ transport and leading to a pronounced
deterioration in high-current cycling performance. Specifically,
an ultra-microporous host (pore size < 0.5 nm) featuring Co
nanoclusters with precisely tunable sizes is synthesized via
direct carbonization of zeolitic imidazoline frameworks (ZIFs),
in which the cluster size is continuously regulated by accurately
controlling the Co/Zn molar ratio of the metal nodes in the
parent framework. This architecture substantially enlarges the
sulfur−catalyst contact area and further avoids the excessive
contact of the catalyst particles with the electrolytes outside of the
pores. Thus, the moderate-sized Co clusters (∼0.6 nm) effectively
reduce the energy barrier for sulfur conversion while minimizing
catalytic side reactions that induce Li6PS5Cl (LPSC), thus sta-
bilizing the Li+ transport pathways. As a result, the assembled
ASSLSBs deliver the state-of-the-art room-temperature perfor-
mance and outstanding cycling stability at high rates, realizing
a reversible specific capacity of 520 mAh g−1 after 15000 cycles at
an ultrahigh rate of 15 C (25.0 mA cm−2) at 30 ◦C for the first time,
significantly surpassing previously reported results. A reversible
areal capacity exceeding 5.4 mAh cm−2 is sustained under a high
sulfur loading of 4 mg cm−2 after 300 cycles. Even at a high
sulfur content (45 wt%) and loading (6 mg cm−2), the batteries
achieve an areal capacity exceeding 3.51 mAh cm−2 after 70 cycles
at 30 ◦C. This work highlights the importance of maintaining the
integrity of Li+ transport pathways during high-current-density
operation of ASSLSBs, providing a promising approach for its
practical applications.

In order to minimize excessive contact between LPSC and the
catalyst, ZIF-derived carbon materials that can anchor the cata-
lyst and possess a rich microporous structure are an ideal choice
[25]. Since micron-sized LPSC cannot enter the micropores, it
can only interact with the limited catalytic sites on the carbon
surface, thereby suppressing excessive catalytic decomposition of
LPSC. Meanwhile, the abundant channels provide a molecular-
level contact path for sulfur and the catalytic sites. A series of
Co-containing materials with different Co sizes were synthe-
sized by partially substituting Zn centers in the ZIF precursor
with Co and precisely adjusting the Co/Zn molar ratio before
high-temperature carbonization [26, 27]. The resulting catalysts
were denoted as Co-L (large), Co-M (middle), and Co-S (small)
in order of decreasing Co particle size. High-angle annular
dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-
STEM) measurements were used to characterize the size of
Co particles in different catalysts. As shown in Figures 2c and
S6a,b, Co-S shows atomic-level dispersion of Co on the carbon
substrate while Co-L displays pronounced Co agglomeration with
an average particle size of ≈1.4 nm. Co-M presents uniformly
distributed medium-sized Co clusters with an average particle
size of ≈0.54 nm. Inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry
(ICP-MS) quantified the Co loading at ≈2 wt% (Table S1).
The x-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns show broad carbon peak
without the characteristic peaks corresponding to metallic Co,
confirming that Co is not present as large-sized metal phase
(Figure S7a). Further increasing the Co loading to 4–5 wt% results
in pronounced metallic Co peaks in the XRD patterns (Co-NP),
indicating the formation of metallic cobalt at higher Co content
(Figure S7b).
Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images reveal that the three
samples exhibit a rhombic dodecahedral morphology similar
to that of the Co free counterpart (Figure S8a-h) [28]. Scan-
ning transmission electron microscopy energy-dispersive x-ray
spectroscopy (STEM-EDS) mapping shows that Co is uniformly
dispersed across the carbon substrate (Figure S9a–d). However,
higher cobalt content induces the migration and aggregation
of Co atoms, which disrupts the internal pore structure of the
host. As shown in the Figure S10a-f, Co nanoparticles can be
readily identified, accompanied by structural damage of the
carbon framework induced by particle migration. N2 adsorption-
desorption measurements reveal type I adsorption isotherms
for the three Co-containing samples and the Co free counter-
part, evidencing the existence of abundant micropores (Figure
S11). As shown in Figure S12a-c, CO2 adsorption-desorption
measurements further demonstrate that most samples exhibit
high microporous surface areas exceeding 900 m2 g−1 given in
Table S2 together with abundant ultra-micropores (<0.5 nm).
Sulfur can penetrate into the micropores as short-chain sulfur
molecules during the melting process and prevent their reversion
to cyclic S8 molecules at room temperature, which improves
the redox activity and sulfur utilization [29]. Subsequently, the
above materials were combined with sulfur by a thermal melting
process (denoted as Co-NP/S, Co-L/S, Co-M/S, Co-S/S, and Co
free/S). As shown in Figure S13a-c, the micropores have almost
disappeared, proving that sulfur almost completely fills the pore
channels [30]. Consequently, the large micropore volume can
uniformly confine sulfur and the catalyst within the pores,
creating intimate interfaces that accommodate the volumetric
changes of sulfur during cycling [31, 32].
The composite cathodes without sulfur were prepared by high-
energy ball milling to exclusively explore the influence of catalyst
size on the electrochemical decomposition of LPSC. According
to Figure S14, P 2p XPS analyses of the cycled cathodes showed
that the proportion of the reduced P(IV) characteristic peak
significantly decreased, in contrast to the cathode that directly
incorporated metallic cobalt particles (Figure S3). This result
indicates that the reconstruction of the catalytic interface by ZIF-
derived carbon suppresses the parasitic decomposition of LPSC.
The cyclic voltammetry (CV) profiles in Figure 2d display that,
cathodes containing the catalyst exhibit a larger integral area
compared with Co free cathode, suggesting that LPSC in contact
with the carbon matrix still inevitably undergoes continuous
parasitic decomposition catalyzed by part of Co-anchored on the
surface, and exhibits a pronounced size-dominated effect. Among
them, Co–S exhibits the largest integral area and the highest peak
current owing to its high density of active sites, while both Co-
M and Co-L display substantially reduced integrated areas. To
demonstrate that the parasitic decomposition is catalyzed by Co-
anchored on the carbon surface rather than by free Co species,
Co-S was subjected to acid treatment and subsequently character-
ized by XPS. As shown in Figure S15, the Co 2p spectra before and
after acid treatment show negligible changes. Moreover, as shown
in Figure 2e and S14, the area ratio of reduced P(IV) and PS4
3−
peaks in the P 2p XPS spectra increases with decreasing catalyst
size, indicating the extent of parasitic decomposition is correlated
with the catalyst size.
X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) and extended x-
ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) analyses were performed
to probe the coordination environment and chemical state of
the Co atoms. According to Figure S16, the near-edge absorption
energies of all samples are higher than that of the Co foil and the
energy increases from Co-L to Co-S, demonstrating that stronger
Co─N coordination elevates the oxidation state of Co. According
to the Co 2p XPS analyses (Figure S17), Co‑S exhibits only
characteristic peaks of Co─N bonds, whereas with increasing size

showing more pronounced aggregation. With the high-resolution
k–R bivariate analyses afforded by wavelet transform (WT), the
scattering contributions arising from cobalt atoms in different

According to Figure 2f, Co-S only exhibits the Co─N bonds,
demonstrating that the cobalt is atomically dispersed and densely
distributed [7]. The Co-L exhibits predominantly Co─Co bonding

2.2
Size-Dominated Electrochemical Behavior of
Catalytic Interfaces
As shown in Figures 3a and S21a,b, the anion spectra obtained
from time of flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF-
SIMS) tests detect the fragment signal Li2PS4
−attributed to
LPSC, showing that LPSC is uniformly distributed in the 2D
mapping. For the pristine cathodes, the Li2PS4
−signal intensities
are essentially identical. Notably, the signal maintains homo-
geneous and intense in both Co-L and Co-M cathodes while
drops markedly within Co-S cathode after cycling, indicating
severe destabilization of the PS4 units. In addition, ToF-SIMS
characterization was conducted on cathodes contained sulfur
(Co-S/S) collected at different discharge potentials during the first
cycle (Figure S22). As discharge proceeds, the intensity of the
Li2PS4
−signal gradually weakens. Additionally, we performed
XPS characterization on cathodes collected at different discharge
potentials during the first cycle (Figure S23). To quantify the
extent of electrolyte decomposition, we compared the relative
peak area ratios of PS4
3−and reduced P(IV). Upon discharging to
1.6 V, the electrolytes in the Co-S/S cathode undergo decomposi-
tion due to Co catalysis, as evidenced by the increased proportion
of the reduced P(IV) peak area. This result suggests that the
catalytic interface between LPSC and catalyst in the Co-L and Co-
M cathodes effectively preserves the structural integrity of LPSC,
whereas overly high-density catalytic sites in the Co-S cathode
leads to significant electrolyte degradation.
To more intuitively elucidate how the LPSC degradation impacts
the performance of all-solid-state sulfur cathodes, we first con-
ducted COMSOL simulations of the three-phase interface model
involving catalytic particles, sulfur, and LPSC on a 6 nm × 6 nm
square domain of a representative carbon surface, namely, the
actual microscopic environment in which solid-state sulfur con-
version and LPSC degradation occur as shown in Figure 3b and
Table S3. Besides, the size of the catalytic sites was determined
according to the statistically averaged particle size obtained from
electron microscopy. Within this domain, catalytic sites were
modeled by locally increasing the reaction rate (implemented
by enhancing the exchange current density) within a defined
circular region with an effective area of ∼7.5 nm2. According to
Figure 2b, the narrow electrochemical window of LPSC leads
to the formation of the solid electrolyte interface with low Li+
conductivity during cycling [39, 40]. Therefore, a Li+ transport
passivation zone was introduced around the catalytic region, in
which the Li+ conductivity was reduced proportionally to mimic
the loss of ionic transport caused by parasitic LPSC decom-
position. By capturing the spatial distribution of overpotential
during sulfur lithiation within this domain, we uncovered a clear
size-dominated behavior for solid-phase reaction kinetics, which
means that the overall conversion kinetics strongly correlate with
the characteristic size of the catalytic units. As illustrated in
Figure 3c, when the catalytic units (Co nanoparticle domains) are
too small (diameter of 0.25 nm), the excessively large interfacial
contact with LPSC intensifies electrolyte decomposition, produc-
ing extensive regions with hindered Li+ transport and resulting in
pronounced local overpotentials. Conversely, when the catalytic
units are too large (∼1.4 nm), the catalytic enhancement of
sulfur conversion is significantly weakened, and the overall
overpotential remains high. Only at an intermediate catalyst
size (∼0.6 nm, well matched to that of Co-M) is a balance
achieved between parasitic electrolyte decomposition and solid-
phase sulfur catalysis, leading to uniformly low overpotential
across the reaction interface. Figures 3d and S24 further correlate
catalyst size, the severity of parasitic catalysis, and the spatially
averaged reaction overpotential. These results highlight that
the geometric structure of the catalytic interface is essential
for maintaining catalytic activity while effectively suppressing
excessive decomposition of the solid electrolyte.
Moreover, in situ electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS)
coupled with distribution of relaxation times (DRT) analyses
were employed to investigate the impact of the size-dominated
catalytic reaction on cathode kinetic evolution at different DOD
[41]. Owing to the severe overlap in the Nyquist plots of ASSLSBs,
enabled clear separation of the impedance contributions asso-
ciated with interfacial ion transport across the cathode (D1),
sulfur redox reactions (D2), and Li+ diffusion within the cathode
(D3) [42, 43]. As shown in Figures 3e and S25, it can be
clearly observed that the introduction of catalysts effectively
reduces the overall impedance in ASSLSBs. Notably, Co-S/S
cathode exhibits lower peak intensities for D2 and D3 during
the early discharge stage (DOD 0%−30%) which coincides with
the parasitic reaction, whereas the Co-M/S and Co-L/S cathodes
show significantly higher impedance, indicating that kinetically
hindered ion diffusion and charge transfer during this stage result
in a diminished extent of LPSC decomposition (Figure S26).
As discharge proceeds, Co-M/S shows similarly low impedance
to Co-S/S during the sulfur conversion stage. In addition, we

2.3
Size-Dominated Catalytic Regulation of SRR
and Parasitic Reaction Kinetics
To investigate the influence of geometric structure on the catalytic
interface in solid-state sulfur cathodes, the reaction kinetics
of the ASSLSBs were characterized by CV at 0.1 mV s−1. As
shown in Figures 4a and S28, Co free/S exhibits a single pair
of redox peaks correlated with S/Li2S [44]. Following catalyst
incorporation, the reduction peak shifts positively from 1.00
to 1.20 V and shows much higher current density, suggesting
that Co effectively lowers the overpotential in sulfur reduction
reaction (SRR) and accelerates sulfur conversion. It is worth
noting that only a new reduction peak emerges at 1.55 V without
the appearance of a corresponding oxidation peak, which aligns
with the shoulder peak in Figure 2a, confirming that this
irreversible reaction is related to the parasitic decomposition of
LPSC. Furthermore, the Tafel slopes were analyzed to quantify
the kinetics of SRR and parasitic reactions. In Figure S29a-c, the
slopes for SRR (1.20 V) of Co-S/S, Co-M/S, and Co-L/S cathodes
are 457, 466, and 562 mV dec−1, respectively, much lower than
that of Co free cathode (875 mV dec−1), corroborating that the
introduction of the catalyst significantly improves SRR kinetics.
Besides, the slopes for electrolyte decomposition (1.55 V) of Co-
S/S, Co-M/S, and Co-L/S cathodes are 237, 264, and 266 mV
dec−1. Notably, the current intensities of both processes increase
monotonically with decreasing Co particle sizes, demonstrating
that the geometric structure of the catalytic interface concurrently
governs the extent of LPSC decomposition and the efficiency

and LPSC decomposition, the Co-M/S cathode exhibits the lowest

higher current densities. Thus, we conducted a comprehensive

underscores the critical role of the Co clusters with moderate size
in enhancing sulfur reaction kinetics and stabilizing Li+ transport
pathways.
The COMSOL simulation results confirm that the Co-L/S cathode
exhibits Li+ depletion near the catalyst particles due to the
decomposition of adjacent electrolyte (Figure S36). As for the
Co-S/S cathode, the Li+ exhibits extensive depletion due to the
collapse of transport channels, which results in the excessive local
overpotential and heterogeneous sulfur conversion consequently.
In contrast, the Co-M/S cathode exhibits the most uniform
Li+ distribution and the lowest overpotential. Consequently, the
assembled ASSLSBs exhibit distinct behaviors at 5 C (Figures 4f
and S37). Owing to the high catalytic activity, the Co-S/S and
Co-M/S cathodes initially deliver similar discharge capacities.
However, the excessive parasitic reaction in the Co-S/S cathode
collapses the ion-conducting network and triggers an abrupt
capacity plunge after 200 cycles, whereas the Co-M/S cathode
maintains robust cycling stability and retains a capacity of 994
mAh g−1 after 1500 cycles. To evaluate the Li+ conductivity
after cycling, the galvanostatic intermittent titration (GITT) was
subsequently carried out in ASSLSBs (Figure S38a-c). The ion

than those of the Co-S/S (2.8×10−11 cm2 s−1) and Co free/S
(3.9×10−11 cm2 s−1) cathodes due to the robust ion-transport path-
ways. Moreover, the overpotential of Co-M/S cathode (116 mV)
is lower than those of Co-S/S (178 mV) and Co free/S (167 mV)
cathodes throughout the entire charge and discharge process.
These results demonstrate that moderate-sized Co clusters can
balance the catalytic reactions between sulfur and the electrolyte,
thus effectively enhancing sulfur conversion and maintaining a
robust Li+ transport pathway in long-term cycling.

3
Conclusion
In summary, this study reveals that catalytically induced para-
sitic decomposition of SSEs under overlapping electrochemical
potential conditions inevitably disrupts Li+ transport pathways,
thereby limiting the high-rate performance of ASSLSBs. Based on
this understanding, we propose a catalyst design with a spatially
selective architecture, typically demonstrated by confining cobalt
clusters within an ultra-microporous carbon host, to facilitate
effective contact between sulfur and the catalytic sites while
minimizing the excessive exposure of catalysts to the electrolytes
outside the pores. Consequently, cobalt clusters with a moderate
size (∼0.6 nm) significantly reduce the energy barrier for SRR
(from 12.6 to 3.8 kJ mol−1) while maximally suppressing the
parasitic decomposition of LPSC (from 3.9 to 6.9 kJ mol−1), result-
ing in rapid and robust Li+ transport. Therefore, the assembled
ASSLSB delivers a remarkable initial capacity of 1202 mAh g−1
at 5 C and maintains 82% capacity retention after 1500 cycles.
More exceptionally, it can operate at 30◦C for over 15000 cycles
at a high current density of 25.0 mA cm−2, showing unrivaled
rate capacity and extraordinary cycling stability. When the sulfur
loading is increased to 4 mg cm−2, the assembled ASSLSB still
delivers a capacity exceeding 5.4 mAh cm−2 after 300 cycles.
Even at a high sulfur content (45 wt%) and loading (6 mg cm−2),
the batteries still deliver an areal capacity above 3.51 mAh cm−2
after 70 cycles at 30◦C. This work highlights the importance of
maintaining the integrity of Li+ transport pathways during high-
rate operation of ASSLSBs, providing a promising approach for its
practical applications. Our work highlights a feasible approach
to promote sulfur conversion and stabilize rapid Li+ transport
pathways through a rational catalyst-structure design, providing
new insights for the development of high-rate and long-life
ASSLSBs.

Experimental Section
4.1
Synthesis of Co/Zn ZIFs and Sulfur-Loaded
Derived Carbon
Co(NO3)2⋅6H2O (0.31 g) and Zn(NO3)2⋅6H2O (1.47 g, 1.58 g and
1.69 g, respectively) were dissolved in 60 mL of methanol; then 2-
methylimidazole (1.85 g) in 60 mL of methanol was subsequently
injected into above solution under vigorously stirring for 12 h at
room temperature. The resulting solution was washed three times
with methanol by vacuum filtration, and dried in a vacuum at
70◦C for 24 h. The powder of ZIFs was placed in a tube furnace
and heated to 1000◦C at a rate of 5◦C min−1, then maintained
for 3 h under flowing argon gas and naturally cooled to room
temperature. The resulting materials were designated as Co-L,
Co-M, and Co-S, respectively. The above materials and sulfur
were uniformly mixed in a ratio of 1:1. Then the mixture was
placed in a tube furnace at argon gas atmosphere and heated to
155◦C for 20 h and continued to heat up to 300◦C for 2 h under
flowing argon gas. After cooling down to room temperature,
the materials were designated as Co-L/S, Co-M/S, and Co-S/S,
respectively. For the Co free and Co free/S, only Zn(NO3)2⋅6H2O
was added during the initial synthesis, the other procedures were
identical to those described above.
4.2
Synthesis of Cathode Materials
The Co free/S solid-state cathode was prepared by high-energy
ball milling (Planetary high-energy ball mill, FRITSCH, Pul-
verisette7) Co free/S and Li6PS5Cl solid electrolyte (Canrd Tech-
nology Co. Ltd) with a mass ratio of 1:1. For other solid-state
cathodes, Co free/S was replaced by Co-L/S, Co-M/S, and Co-S/S.
The Co free solid-state cathode was prepared by high-energy ball
milling Co free after carbonization and Li6PS5Cl solid electrolyte
with a mass ratio of 1:1. For other solid-state cathodes, Co free
was replaced by Co-L, Co-M, and Co-S. The 5 wt% Co solid-
state cathode was prepared by high-energy ball milling with a
sulfur:Ketjen Black:Li6PS5Cl:Co mass ratio of 20:25:50:5, where
sulfur and Ketjen Black were pre-composited using the same
melt-diffusion method. For the 2 wt% Co and 0 wt% Co cathodes,
the decrease in Co content was compensated by a corresponding
increase in sulfur content.
4.3
Synthesis of Cathode Materials with High
Sulfur Content (45 Wt%)
ZIF-derived carbon and sulfur were first mixed at a mass ratio
of 1:4 and heated at 155◦C for 20 h. Subsequently, the resulting
mixture was prepared by high-energy ball milling with Li6PS5Cl
solid electrolyte and Ketjen Black at a mass ratio of 57:38:5.
4.4
Preparation of Solid-State Batteries
All-solid-state batteries were assembled in an argon-filled glove-
box. First, 90 mg of LPSC powder was loaded into a 10 mm
diameter PEEK sleeve and pressed between two stainless-steel
plungers at 3000 kg for 3 min to form an electrolyte pellet. Next,
the solid-state cathode was uniformly spread onto one side of
the electrolyte layer, followed by pressing at 5000 kg for 5 min.
Subsequently, a Li-In alloy (Canrd Technology Co. Ltd) anode foil
was placed on the opposite side of the electrolyte layer at 1000 kg
for 2 min. All batteries tests were conducted under a pressure of
1500 kg.

process and ion distribution in the three-phase interface model
involving catalytic particles, sulfur, and LPSC in solid-state Li–S
battery. The finite element simulation was carried out with the
COMSOL Multiphysics 6.0 platform. The local three-phase inter-
