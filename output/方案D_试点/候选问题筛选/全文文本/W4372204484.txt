A three-in-one C60-integrated PEO-based solid
polymer electrolyte enables superior all-solid-state
lithium–sulfur batteries†
Benben Wei,a Shuo Huang,a Yuhang Song,a Xuan Wang,a Min Liu,b Hongyun Jin
*a
and Guozhong Cao
c
Polyethylene oxide (PEO) is a potential solid polymer electrolyte for all-solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries,
yet its practical application has been hindered due to the low ionic conductivity, serious lithium dendrite
growth, and uncontrolled shuttle eﬀect. Fullerene (C60) is a versatile additive for solid polymer
electrolytes, since it lacks electronic conductivity, and the curved p surface of C60 tends to interact with
the n-orbitals of the PEO ether oxygen. Herein, a multifunctional additive, C60, is proposed to
compensate for the intrinsic imperfection of the PEO electrolyte. The unique 0D molecular structure of
C60 promotes the ionic conductivity from 0.57 × 10−4 to 1.27 × 10−4 S cm−1 at 60 °C by boosting the
chain segment movement of PEO and dissociating the ion association of LiTFSI. The high interface
energy of C60 towards Li can eﬀectively block the growth and penetration of Li dendrites, which is
responsible for the stable cycling durability for 1000 h. With the integration of C60, the rate-determining
liquid–solid conversion process is selectively facilitated to retard polysulﬁde shuttle, and furthermore, the
Li2S decomposition energy barrier is reduced in the lithium–sulfur electrochemistry. We highlight that
the PEO–C60 solid polymer electrolyte endows the all-solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries with improved
rate performance, long-span-life stability, and enhanced safety features.
Introduction
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries have been regarded as one of the
most promising energy storage systems owing to the high
gravimetric capacity (1672 mA h g−1) and intriguing theoretical
energy density (2600 W h kg−1) derived from multi-electron
redox electrochemistry, as well as the low cost and environ-
mental benignity of sulfur.1 Nonetheless, several technical
challenges still limit the commercialization of Li–S batteries.2
Typically, Li–S batteries with common liquid ether-based elec-
trolytes have the risk of leakage and combustion of ammable
organic electrolytes.3 The complicated stepwise chemical reac-
tion and multiphase transformation of lithium polysuldes
(LiPSs) intermediates with strong solvation (xS + 2e−4 Sx
2−, S8
4 LiPSs 4 Li2S2/Li2S) render a profound shuttle eﬀect, in
which LiPSs dissolve in the electrolyte and migrate from
cathode to anode along with fast capacity decay and poor
cyclability.4 Particularly, the inevitable lithium dendrite growth
and gas production in solution results in short-circuiting and
volume expansion of cells with consequent serious safety
issues.5 All in all, the replacement of liquid electrolytes with
solid electrolytes is necessary in the future since they provide
promising opportunities to overcome the above challenges.6
Polymer electrolytes based on PEO have many advantages,
such as low cost, reasonable mechanical/thermal stability, low
sensitivity to air and humidity, excellent lm-forming scalable
production, and facile processability compatible with current
manufacturing, which highlight their tremendous application
potential.7 However, the low energy density and poor stability
still obscure the practical application of PEO-based all-solid-
state lithium–sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs). The main reasons for
such limitations are as follows. (i) There are complex sulfur
redox reactions in a working cell, including uncontrolled LiPSs
dissolution and sluggish reaction kinetics.8 PEO has a similar
molecular structure to traditional polyether liquid electrolytes,
with a substantial solvent property and a relatively high Gut-
mann donor number of EO units.9 Therefore, LiPSs can dissolve
in the PEO matrix via complexation, driving capacity degrada-
tion with an adverse shuttle eﬀect. Besides, the slow reaction
kinetics derived from the intrinsic nature of sulfur is not being
tackled.10 (ii) Electrolytes based on PEO present a low ionic
conductivity of approximately 10−6 S cm−1 at room tempera-
ture. The semi-crystalline intrinsic nature of PEO renders

In this work, we introduce multifunctional C60 into the PEO
polymer matrix to improve the ionic conductivity of PEO, with
the ultimate goal of promoting innovation and application of
PEO-based ASSLSBs. Particularly, the liquid–solid conversion of
the rate-determining process in the discharge process is accel-
erated, and the Li2S decomposition energy barrier is reduced for
an improved redox reaction. The uncontrolled Li dendrite
growth is restrained, since the weak bonding of C60 (high
interface energy) to Li can eﬀectively promote Li diﬀusion along
the interface and prevent the dendrite penetration into the
electrolyte. As a result, ASSLSBs with C60-integrated PEO-based
solid polymer electrolytes exhibit high specic capacity, long
cycle life, and improved rate performance.
Results and discussion
Enhanced Li+ conductivity
As shown in Fig. 1a, the pure PEO-based solid polymer elec-
trolyte forms a exible and transparent freestanding lm, with
a thickness of ∼100 mm. The C60-containing electrolyte
membrane is brown and the color becomes deeper with the
increase of C60 content. The inuence of C60 concentration on
the ionic conductivity of polymer electrolytes is investigated by
electrochemical impedance spectroscopy. The Nyquist (Fig. S1†)
and Arrhenius plots (Fig. 1b) of PEO and PEO–C60 electrolytes
show that a 1% weight ratio of C60 increases the conductivity of
PEO from 0.57 × 10−4 to 1.27 × 10−4 S cm−1 at 60 °C (detailed
information is given in Table S1†). Notice that the addition of
high concentrations (∼3 wt%) of the C60 additive results in low
conductivity as compared to the neat PEO electrolyte, which can
be attributed to the low dissociation of C60 in the polymer
system and the poor intrinsic conductivity of C60. Excessive C60
in the PEO electrolyte can block the ion transfer route by per-
turbing the continuity of PEO nanodomains.31–34 The ionic
conductivity of the PEO-based electrolyte is closely related to its
crystallinity, which dominates the chain motion within rapidly
transferring Li ion channels. To comprehend the eﬀect of C60
on PEO crystallinity, the crystallinities of polymer electrolytes
were evaluated by X-ray diﬀraction (XRD). Pure PEO shows two
characteristic diﬀraction peaks at 19.36 and 23.72°, which are
ascribed to (120) and (112) planes (Fig. S2†). The shito a lower
diﬀraction angle aer introducing LiTFSI reveals the interac-
tion between Li+ and –COC– of PEO. With the addition of C60,
the peak intensity of PEO drops (Fig. 1c), suggesting a reduced
crystallinity. Diﬀerential scanning calorimetry measurement
(Fig. 1d) shows that the incorporation of C60 hinders the poly-
mer crystallization, resulting in a shiof melting temperature
from 60.9 to 58.7 °C. A smaller Tm corresponds to a more ex-
ible polymer chain in the PEO electrolytes. The C60 molecules
can disrupt the crystalline regions to promote the chain
segment movement and weaken the intermolecular interaction
of PEO chains, resulting in lowering of the polymer crystallinity.
The intense interaction between C60 and PEO resembles the
previously reported llers in PEO polymer electrolytes. Raman
analysis is carried out to further gure out the mechanism of
improved ionic conductivity. The characteristic peak of TFSI−
deconvolutes into two peaks assigned to free TFSI−and Li+–
TFSI−ion pairs in the polymer electrolyte (Fig. 1e). The
decreased ion pairs in the PEO–1% C60 suggests that C60 is
conducive to dissociating the ion association of LiTFSI. The
charge transfer between lithium salt anion and C60 will cause
a shiof C60 from 1469 cm−1 to a low wavenumber;35 no such
downshiobserved in Fig. 1f means that C60 acts as an inert
ller to improve the overall ionic conductivity by dissociating Li-
anion pairs rather than binding to TFSI−anions. This
phenomenon is conrmed by the C 1s high-resolution X-ray
photoelectron spectrum. In the C 1s spectra of the PEO elec-
trolyte, the peak at 284.8 eV is ascribed to aliphatic carbon from
sample impurities. The single C 1s peak centered at 286.5 eV
belongs to the ether group of PEO, and the peak at 292.7 eV is

Li dendrite suppression of Li symmetric cell performance
The stability of PEO-based electrolytes against lithium dendrite
penetration was evaluated in Li-metal symmetric cells via the
galvanostatic plating and stripping process. Fig. 2a demon-
strates that symmetric Li cells with PEO–1% C60 can function
well at 0.2 mA cm−2, while sudden failure occurs in PEO at 0.15
mA cm−2. The sudden potential drop means that the Li
dendrites impale the electrolyte and result in a short circuit.
The overpotential of PEO–1% C60 is lower than that of PEO at
a current density of 0.05 mA cm−2. When the current density
rises to 0.1 mA cm−2, the overpotential of PEO–1% C60 is higher
than that of PEO though the ionic conductivity of PEO–1% C60 is
superior to that of the PEO electrolyte. This phenomenon can be
attributed to the high interfacial energy of C60 towards Li metal.
The high interfacial energy of C60 impedes the formation and
nucleation of Li dendrite on the surface of the electrolyte. As
reported,37 high interface energy can facilitate the lateral
migration of Li+ along the interface. The high interface energy
of C60 could inhibit the vertical dendrite growth, and will
contribute to suppress the Li dendrite penetration into the
electrolyte. As a result, PEO–1% C60 exhibits strong dendrite
suppressing ability but appears less eﬃcient in improving the
overpotentials. The suppressed Li dendrite growth can be
further conrmed through the dense surface morphology of Li
metal with PEO–1% C60 (Fig. 2b) as compared to that of the
loose structure of Li surface morphology with PEO (Fig. 2c),
which are obtained from disassembled Li symmetric cells aer
30 cycles at a current density of 0.05 mA h cm−2 and areal
capacity of 0.1 mA h cm−2. PEO–2% C60 and PEO–3% C60 fail to
work at a current density of 0.1 mA cm−2, which is caused by the
low ionic conductivity and extremely high interface energy (Fig.
S6†). The critical current density of PEO–1% C60 is 1.3 mA cm−2,
superior to that of 0.6 mA cm−2 (Fig. S7†). The improved CCD
widens the application of PEO electrolytes in all-solid-state
lithium metal batteries. The symmetric cell employing PEO–
1% C60 demonstrates outstanding stability at 0.05 mA cm−2
with the remaining operative for over 1000 h and at low over-
potentials of 22 mV (Fig. 2d). A sudden potential drop in PEO is
observed aer 25 h with a high overpotential of 33 mV (Fig. 2e).
These facts suggest more negligible polarization induced by
PEO–1% C60 than PEO during the Li plating/stripping process.
When the current density rises to 0.2 mA cm−2, the Li/PEO–1%
C60/Li cell can still rmly operate for more than 200 h without
apparent voltage uctuation and short-circuit (Fig. 2f). The
outstanding outperformances reveal the signicant eﬀect of C60
on strengthening the interphase stability between the Li/
electrolyte interface.

Conclusions
Trifunctional C60 is demonstrated as a good additive for the
PEO polymer matrix to form a highly Li ion conductive, elec-
trochemically stable, and safety compatible solid electrolyte.
Owing to the outstanding Li dendrite suppression ability from
the high interface energy of C60 towards Li, the Li symmetric cell
delivers stable cycling for 1000 h. The ionic conductivity of
polymer electrolytes has been improved due to the decreased
crystallinity with the facial chain segment. Furthermore, the
composite electrolyte quickens the liquid–solid conversion in
the reduction process and reduces the solid–liquid conversion
potential barrier in the charging process by the C60 electro-
catalyst. Thus, ASSLSBs employing the PEO–C60 electrolyte
achieved outstanding cycle stability, improved safety features,
and enhanced rate performance, ascribed to the all-purpose C60
additive as an electrocatalyst, ion promoter, and dendrite
shield.
