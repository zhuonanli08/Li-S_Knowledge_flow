ABSTRACT: The decomposition behavior of electrolytes affects
the cycle stability and electrochemical redox activity in all-solid-state
lithium−sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs). However, there is a sparse
understanding of the electrochemistry of ASSLSBs involving the
oxidative decomposition of sulfide solid electrolytes (SEs) due to
the lack of fundamental studies. Herein, we unveil the redox
chemistry related to Li2S/S conversion reaction, electrolyte
decomposition, and redox reaction of the decomposition product,
based on differential capacity curves. The oxidation reaction of Li2S
proceeds simultaneously with a continuous oxidative decomposition
of sulfide SEs. Raman spectroscopy of the cell after cycling shows
that SEs in the cathode convert the thiophosphates with a S−S thiol
bond via the decomposition behavior of the electrolytes. The
implication of this reaction chemistry is expanded toward understanding the Li2S activation process in ASSLSBs. Additionally, we
demonstrate that Li2S/SE interface modification by different rotation speeds of ball milling in SE mixing allows us to control the
decomposition kinetics of electrolytes. The severe decomposition of electrolytes causes cycle fading instead of increasing the
electrochemical redox activity in the early period. The findings of this work highlight the need for interface engineering to avoid
severe degradation of electrolytes in the cathode and enhance the ability of SEs as a redox mediator.
■INTRODUCTION
Lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries have received much attention
as next-generation batteries because S and Li2S cathode
materials have high theoretical capacities of 1670 and 1167
mAh g−1, respectively.1 However, the use of organic liquid
electrolytes in Li−S batteries causes self-discharge through the
dissolution of lithium polysulfides, an intermediate product of
the cathode material, which were generated in the charge−
discharge process. The self-discharge reduces cycle stability
and, thus, is a hindrance to the commercialization of Li−S
batteries. All-solid-state Li−S batteries (ASSLSBs), which
replace the organic liquid electrolytes with inorganic solid
electrolytes (SEs), suppress the dissolution of lithium
polysulfides, thereby resolving the cycle fade caused by self-
discharge in Li−S batteries.2,3 Nevertheless, the poor
electronic and ionic conductivities of S and Li2S cathodes
still restrict the utilization of their high theoretical capacity. To
improve the electric transport properties in cathode
composites, several research groups have developed cathode
composites mixed with highly conductive electron additives
and SEs.3 For instance, electron additives, such as mesoporous
carbon,4 carbon nanotubes,5−7 and reduced graphene oxide8
were blended into cathode composites. Similarly, sulfide-based
SEs such as Li10GeP2S12,8−10 Li7P3S11,11 Li7P2S8I,12 and
Li6PS5Cl,13−17 are used in cathode composites. In recent
years, Ohno et al. demonstrated that sluggish ionic transport
properties in the cathode limit the battery performance of
ASSLSBs.18 Hence, the role of SEs blended in cathode
composites holds the key to improving the utilization of sulfur
cathodes.
The sulfide SEs in the cathode decompose into chemical
species with electrochemical redox activity at the highly
electrochemically conductive surfaces with a carbon additive in
the charging process.15,19−24 The decomposition of electrolytes
results in the loss of ionic conduction pathways, degrading the
overall cell performance.15 In contrast, the redox-active
chemical species produced through the oxidative decom-
position of sulfide SEs decreases the charge overpotential of
Li2S in liquid Li−S batteries.25 Thus, sulfide SEs can serve as a
redox mediator in ASSLSBs owing to their electrolyte
decomposition behavior, in addition to their role as conduction
pathways. However, there is a lack of fundamental studies on
the electrochemical redox-active decomposition product
produced from sulfide SEs in ASSLSBs. This is because the

to the oxidation potential of Li2S. In addition, the voltage

to the two-plateaued discharging voltage profile in liquid Li−S
batteries.26 These electrochemical behaviors have resulted in
misinterpretation of redox reactions among many researchers.
In fact, the reported voltage profiles of ASSLSBs involve
multistep plateaus in the discharge process, whereas such
reaction steps have been obscure so far.5,12−14,27−32 A recent
study reported that the Li2S cathode composites fabricated by
mixing Li3PS4 (LPS) in a mortar did not show any
electrochemical activity of LPS, excluding the capacity
contribution of SEs in ASSLSBs.33 Nevertheless, the voltage
profiles seem to involve reduction reactions with multiple steps
in the aforementioned reports; the reason for these reactions is
still unclear. In addition, such an ambiguous reaction
mechanism brings a limited understanding of the Li2S
activation process: a high activation barrier in the first charge
process and increased capacity during the early cycle.
In the present work, we identify the electrochemical
decomposition behavior of SEs in ASSLSBs using galvanostatic
charge−discharge tests and their differential capacity curves.
These fundamental experiments provide insight into the
ASSLSB electrochemistry involving the Li2S redox reaction,
oxidative decomposition of sulfide SEs, and redox reaction of
the decomposition product in ASSLSBs. This redox chemistry
contributes to the understanding of the Li2S activation process
in ASSLSBs. Additionally, we investigate the effect of different
Li2S/SE interfaces, which are designed by altering the rotation
speeds (250 and 510 rpm) in LPS SE mixing, on cell
performance. Engineering the Li2S/SE interface controls the
electrolyte decomposition kinetics governing the ability of SEs
as a redox mediator and cycle stability. This gives insight into
an optimized design of ASSLSBs that can suppress cycle fading
and enhance the electrochemical redox activity owing to the
decomposition of SEs.
■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Chemicals. Lithium sulfide (99.9%, Mitsuwa), LiCl (99.99%,
Wako Fujifilm), P2S5 (99%, Merck), and VGCF (vapor-grown carbon
fiber, Showa Denko) were procured and used without additional
purification.
Synthesis of Cathode Materials and SEs. For the synthesis of
the Li2S cathode material, 1 g of Li2S particles was ball-milled using
planetary ball milling (Fritsch Pulverisette 7 Premium line) and 45 ml
of zirconia pots containing 15 zirconia balls with a diameter of φ 10
mm at a rotation speed of 510 rpm for 10 h. Amorphous LPS SEs
were prepared by ball milling a mixture powder (1 g) of Li2S and P2S5
at a molar ratio of 3:1 under identical conditions, as described above.
Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 SEs were prepared for the electrolyte layer by ball
milling a mixture (2 g) of Li2S, P2S5, and LiCl with a stoichiometric
ratio at a rotation speed of 600 rpm for 20 h followed by heat
treatment at 450 °C for 2 h.
Preparation of Cathode Composites. For the synthesis of
cathode composites, 0.4 g of Li2S and VGCF powders with a weight
ratio of 5:1 was first ball-milled with 45 ml of zirconia pots containing
35 g of φ 4 mm zirconia balls at a rotation speed of 510 rpm for 10 h;
0.4 g of the Li2S-VGCF and amorphous LPS powders with a weight
ratio of 6:4 was then ball-milled with 45 ml of zirconia pots containing
35 g of φ 4 mm zirconia balls at a rotation speed of 250 or 510 rpm
for 2 h. The obtained Li2S-LPS-VGCF cathode composites milled at
250 and 510 rpm were denoted as Li2S-250 and Li2S-510,
respectively. For the synthesis of LPS-VGCF cathode composites,
0.4 g of amorphous LPS and VGCF powders with a weight ratio of
9:1 was ball-milled at a rotation speed of 250 and 510 rpm for 2 h.
Cell Assembly. For galvanostatic cycling tests, the Li−In|
Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5|Li2S-250 and -510 cells were assembled as follows.
First, 80 mg of Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 powder was filled with a polyether-ether-
ketone with two stainless-steel rods, followed by the application of a
uniaxial pressure of 256 MPa to form pellets with diameters of ∼10.0
mm at room temperature. The cathode composites were spread over
one side of the SE layer and pressed under 256 MPa at room
temperature. The Li2S cathode material loading in Li2S-250 and -510
is 3.2 mg cm−2. Indium and Li foils were placed on the other side of
the SE layer and pressed under 90 MPa at room temperature. The
assembled cell was sandwiched between two stainless-steel rods under
a pressure of approximately 50 MPa for electrochemical measurement.
The cells were rested for 1 h before performing the galvanostatic
cycling test for alloying. In the case of LPS-VGCF cathode
composites, the LPS material loading is 5.7 mg cm−2.
Electrochemical Measurements. The fabricated cells were
cycled under 0.1C (corresponding to the current density of
approximately 0.292 mA cm−2) in a voltage range of 0.6−3.0 V (vs
Li−In) at 30 °C using a charge−discharge device (Nagano, BST-
2004H). Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was
performed for the cell with Li2S-250 and -510 at the state of full
charge and discharge using an electrochemical measurement system
HZ-Pro (Hokuto Denko) in the frequency range of 1 MHz to 1 mHz
and an applied voltage of 10 mV.
Materials Characterization. Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD)
measurements were conducted with Cu Kα radiation and a scan rate
of 5° min−1 using a Rigaku SmartLab SE.34 Structural refinements
were conducted by the Rietveld method using the RIETAN-FP
computer program. The crystalline size, D, of Li2S in the prepared
cathode composite was calculated from the Halder−Wagner method

Decomposition Behavior of SEs. Figure 3a plots the
ionic conductivity versus inverse temperature of the pelletized
pristine LPS, LPS-250, and -510. These samples exhibit the
ionic conductivities of 4.8 × 10−4, 4.1 × 10−4, and 2.8 × 10−4 S
cm−1 at 30 °C, respectively. The change in the ionic
conductivity may be caused by the distortion of the PS4
3−
anion unit observed in the Raman spectra. Additionally, Ates et
al. reported that β-LPS SE pellets with higher porosity and a
larger particle size show higher ionic conductivity. This
observation demonstrates the major effect of grain boundary
resistance on the ionic conductivity of β-LPS.40 The pelletized
pristine LPS, LPS-250, and LPS-510 have pellet densities of
1.82, 1.93, and 2.22 g cm−3, which are calculated from the
volume measured by the dimensions of the pellets,
respectively. The LPS with the lower pellet density and larger
particle size shows higher ionic conductivity. The decrease in
ionic conductivity induced by ball milling may be originated
not only from the modified local structure but also the grain
boundary resistance. Figure 3b depicts galvanostatic charge−
discharge profiles of 90LPS-10VGCF (wt%) milled at rotation
speeds of 250 and 510 rpm (denoted as 90LPS-10VGCF-250
and -510, respectively) under a current density of 0.13 mA
cm−2 at 30 °C. Compared to the cell with 90LPS-10VGCF-
250, the cell with 90LPS-10VGCF-510 exhibits a higher
capacity for 50 cycles. The particle size of the component in
the electrode dictates electronic and/or ionic contact, affecting
the electrochemical reaction kinetics of active materials in all-
solid-state Li-ion batteries.35,41 The high capacity of the
90LPS-10VGCF-510 is attributed to a larger interfacial contact
area between the smaller SEs and VGCF but not the effective
ionic conductivity of SEs in the cathode. This larger contact
area can be explained by the higher pellet density of LPS-510
than that of LPS-250. Figure 3c shows the differential charge
capacity curves of the cell with 90LPS-10VGCF-510. The
curves involve an oxidation peak at 2.27 V in the first charge
process, and its intensity declines with each cycle. After the
initial oxidation reaction, a new oxidation peak appears at 1.76

Impact of the Li2S/LPS Interface on Battery Perform-
ance. The oxidative decomposition of LPS results in redox
activity, which can improve the battery performance limited by
the sluggish kinetics of Li2S cathode materials. The Li2S
reaction chemistry with contributions from the interfacial
reaction between LPS and Li2S (or VGCF) must be taken into
account. The cathode composites with different interface
designs were fabricated, as shown in Figure 4a. The Li2S-
VGCF composite shows broader Bragg peaks than milled Li2S,
which is attributed to the smaller crystalline size of Li2S in
Li2S-VGCF composites (Figure 4b). Ball-milling treatment for
VGCF mixing further pulverizes the Li2S particles. Using the
Halder−Wagner method, the average crystalline size of the
Li2S crystal was calculated to be 16.1 nm for Li2S-250 and 11.1
nm for Li2S-510 (Figure S2). The milled Li2S involves rough
secondary particles of over 50 μm in diameter (Figure 4c). As
can be seen in Figure 4d,e, the SEM-EDS images reveal that
each particle in Li2S-250 and -510 is aggregated and is in close
contact with the others. Phosphorus and sulfur atoms are
evenly distributed across the entire particle. The electro-
chemical redox activity of Li2S is affected by its micro-
structure.36,47−49 Based on the structural properties of LPS SEs
and Li2S cathode composites, we believe that Li2S-250 and
-510 were fabricated, in which Li2S shows similar redox activity
and LPS has different secondary particle sizes. Figure 5a,b
presents the galvanostatic charge−discharge profiles of the cells
with Li2S-250 and -510, respectively. Both cells show a high
activation barrier in the first charge process. The capacity of
these cells is ∼350 mAh g−1 in the first discharge and increases
with each cycle (Figure 5c). This increase of capacity in the
early period and the high activation barrier in the initial cycle
are called the activation process.7,27,30,36 The capacity of the
cell with Li2S-510 reaches a maximum of 564 mAh g−1 after 16
cycles and then decreases to 212 mAh g−1 with a low capacity
retention of 59% after 100 cycles. This decrease in capacity
should be caused by the degradation of electrolytes.11,15 In
contrast, the cell with Li2S-250 shows a maximum capacity of
496 mAh g−1, achieving relatively stable battery performance in
long-term cycles.

The Coulombic and conversion efficiencies were calculated
to uncover the evolution of electrolyte degradation during the
charge−discharge cycle (Figure 5d). The conversion efficiency
ϕC can be determined by dividing the discharge capacity Qd,n
by the charge capacity Qc,n−1, where n is the cycle number.15
The Coulombic efficiency ϕQ can be determined by dividing
the discharge capacity Qd,n by the charge capacity Qc,n in the
same cycle. The efficiency is dominated by three contributions:
the cathode material, decomposition of SEs, and redox-active
decomposition product. The ϕQ values of Li2S-250 and -510
show 82 and 94% in the first cycle, respectively. The ϕQ
stabilizes at 100% for Li2S-250 and 98.4% for Li2S-510 after a
few cycles. The low ϕQ can originate from the irreversibility
associated with the chemomechanical failure and/or decom-
position reaction of LPS. This chemomechanical failure should
be caused by the volume change of the cathode material
involving a redox reaction. The drastic drops in capacity and
efficiency are observed in the 67th cycle, which can be
attributed to the chemomechanical failure. Given that severe
oxidative decomposition occurs at LPS milled at 510 rpm, as
can be seen in Figure 3c, the low ϕQ of Li2S-510 observed in
long-term cycles should originate from the continuous
decomposition reaction of SEs. The conversion efficiency of
both Li2S-250 and -510 is ∼115% in the initial cycle and
converged to 100% after several cycles. The efficiency of over
100% implies an additional capacity contribution of the LPS
and an increase in the electrochemical redox activity through
the oxidation decomposition of LPS.
Reaction Chemistry of ASSLSBs. The differential
capacity curves of the cells with Li2S-250 and -510 are
shown in Figure 6a,b. The calculated differential curves show
an oxidation peak (○) at ∼2.3 V, a pronounced oxidation peak
(☆) that appears after the second cycle, and two reduction
peaks (□and ◊). The first oxidation reaction occurs at the
same potential as the oxidative decomposition of LPS, as
confirmed in Figure 3c. However, the initial charge capacity of
the cells with Li2S-250 and -510 is significantly different from
that of 90LPS-10VGCF-510. This shows that the oxidation
reaction of Li2S participates simultaneously with the oxidative
decomposition of LPS. A Li2S electrode blended with LPS in
liquid Li−S batteries is activated by a redox mediator
generated from the delithiation of LPS, decreasing the charging
potential in the first cycle.25 Hence, LPS should serve as the
redox mediator through the oxidative decomposition in
ASSLSBs. The oxidative decomposition of LPS is observed
even after the second cycle, which implies the dynamic
evolution of the interface between LPS and Li2S (or VGCF,
electron additive). To clarify the effect of the redox mediator of
sulfide SEs, we evaluated the cell performance of a cathode
composite blended with Li3YCl6 SEs, which show oxidative
stability in the operating voltage range of ASSLSBs.50 The cell
exhibits an extremely low capacity of less than 100 mAh g−1,
although the ionic conductivity of Li3YCl6 (σ30 = 2.9 × 10−4 S
cm−1) is similar to that of LPS (Figure S3a−c). The cathode
composite shows no oxidation decomposition of Li3YCl6 in the
charging process, as shown in Figure S3d of the differential
charge capacity curves. In a recent study, Li3HoBr6 having a
high ionic conductivity of ∼1 mS cm−1 and oxidative stability
was applied to ASSLSBs, resulting in a reversible capacity of
582 mAh g−1 at 0.1C.51 Here, the weight percentage of the
sulfur cathode in cathode composites is only 8% and the
operating temperature is 60 °C. These findings highlight that
the redox behavior of SEs that are oxidated at a slightly higher
oxidation reaction potential than Li2S enhances the redox
reaction kinetics of Li2S in ASSLSBs. Hakari et al. have
reported that the Li2S cathode blended with SEs or additives

Distinguishable reduction peaks are observed at ∼1.1 V (□)
and ∼1.3 V (◊) vs Li−In. A conversion reaction from a solid
to a solid in Li−S batteries commonly occurs through a single
step.56 In addition, the reduction reaction of sulfur in ASSLSBs
occurs at a potential of ∼1.1 V (vs Li−In).53 Therefore, the
reduction peaks at higher (◊) and lower voltages (□) are
attributed to the decomposition products of LPS and S
cathode materials, respectively. The reduction peak corre-
sponding to the conversion reaction of S (□) disappears
completely after 50 cycles. In other words, the Li2S cathode is
inactivated through long-term cycling. The loss of redox
activity indicates that the discharge capacity of Li2S-250 and
-510 after 50 cycles is entirely responsible for the reduction
reaction of the decomposition product. Nevertheless, the
capacities of Li2S-250 and -510 are significantly higher than the
capacity of the cell with 90LPS-10VGCF-510 after 50 cycles
(Figure 3b). This implies that the decomposition product from
LPS converts to compounds with higher redox activity through
the charge/discharge process. The reduction peak correspond-
ing to the cathode material reappears in dQ/dV curves of Li2S-
250 during 50−100 cycles (Figure S4). Thus, the Li2S cathode
material in Li2S-250 is reactivated, which leads to the
continuous capacity increase of Li2S-250 after 30 cycles. To
investigate the structural evolution of LPS in the cathode
during charge/discharge, Raman analysis was performed on
Li2S-250 and -510 without treatment and after the 20th cycle
(Figure 7). For the pristine Li2S-250, two peaks are detected at
428 and 280 cm−1, which correspond to the P−S and S−S
bonds in the PS4
3−anion unit, respectively. Raman spectra of
Li2S-250 after the 20th cycle show a weak peak related to the
S−S bond at 241 cm−1. For the pristine Li2S-510, a weak peak
corresponding to the P−S in the PS4
3−anion unit is observed

corresponding to P−S bonds in the PS4
3−is detected, which

3−occurs through the
charge/discharge process. Additionally, the peaks related to the
symmetry species of the S−S bonds appear at 156, 224, 250,

those of thiophosphates with the S−S thiol bond, P4S10+n
molecules; this compound is generated from a series of
reactions of tetraphosphorus decasulfide with disulfides,
showing a high reversible capacity of over 500 mAh g−1.57−61
Therefore, the amorphous LPS SEs in the cathode should react
with polysulfides produced by the delithiation of Li2S in the
charging process to form complex −P−Sn−P−polyanions that
contain long-chain cross-linked sulfur. The high capacity
obtained after the inactivation of Li2S can be described by
the formation of sulfur-rich thiophosphates with redox activity.
The redox peak corresponding to the decomposition products
in the cell with Li2S-510 shifts to the low potential side during
the long-term cycle. This can be attributed to the decrease in
chain length for cross-linked sulfur in the decomposition
product.57 This decomposition behavior should cause an
irreversible redox reaction, fading cycle stability. Our work
points out that the overall performance of ASSLSBs discussed
in previous studies may have been dominated by the
electrochemical activity of the decomposition products. The
decomposition behavior of SEs relates to the activation
process: the initial oxidation reaction with a higher activation
barrier, and an increase in capacity during dozens of cycles.
Lithium sulfide cathode is activated by the onset of
electrochemical redox-active decomposition products gener-
ated through the oxidative decomposition of SEs in the initial
charge. In the activation process, sulfide SEs in the cathode
react with sulfur through continuous oxidative decomposition
to form thiophosphates with a S−S thiol bond. This reaction
chemistry of ASSLSBs enhances the cell performance in the
early cycles. Figure 8 plots the overvoltage calculated from the
potential difference between the maximum oxidation and
reduction peaks in the differential capacity curves of the
galvanostatic cycling test, as shown in Figure 6a,b. The cells
with Li2S-250 and -510 show a high overvoltage in the first
cycle because of the activation process. The overvoltage
significantly reduces in the second cycle and again after
subsequent cycles. The second decrease means that the
dominant redox of the Li2S switches to the dominant redox
of the decomposition products. The cell with Li2S-510 exhibits
an apparent lower overvoltage after several cycles compared to
the cell with Li2S-250. This could be attributed to the larger
contact area between the Li2S cathode material and LPS SEs in
Li2S-510.

charge transfer resistance of the cathode material. The
resistance in the first cycle is approximately the same as the
charge transfer resistance of the fully charged sulfur cathode in
liquid Li−S batteries reported in the past.25 The resistance
increases through cycling, inducing the inactivation of Li2S.
The comprehensive electrochemical analysis and Raman
measurement reveal that the Li3PS4 in the cathode converts
the phosphorus compound with a S−S thiol bond through the
decomposition reaction, which dominates the overall cell
performance of ASSLSBs. This reaction chemistry improves
the electrochemical redox activity in the early period, whereas
the severe decomposition of the electrolyte negatively affects
cycle stability in long-term cycles. Thus, the variability in cell
performance that occurs simply by altering the rotation speed
in the mixing with SEs highlights the need for interface
engineering, not only avoiding severe degradation of electro-
lytes in the cathode but also enhancing the ability of SEs as a
redox mediator.
■CONCLUSIONS
Here, we investigated the decomposition reactions of LPS SEs
in Li2S cathode composites with different interface designs and
examined their effects on cell performance. The findings of our
study have contributed to the understanding of the reaction
chemistry in ASSLSBs involving the decomposition reaction of
sulfide-based SEs. Galvanostatic charge−discharge tests and
their differential capacity curves demonstrate that the Li2S
cathode material participates simultaneously in the oxidation
reaction as the decomposition reaction of LPS in the initial
charge process. The decomposition product produced through
the oxidative decomposition of LPS shows a reversible redox
reaction. Namely, Li2S is activated by the electrochemical
redox-active decomposition products of SEs. Such oxidation
decomposition of LPS occurs continuously even after the
initial charge process. Raman spectroscopy of the cell after the
20th cycle unveils that the Li3PS4 in the cathode converts into
the thiophosphate compound with the S−S bond through the
charge/discharge process. This reaction chemistry involving
continuous electrolyte decomposition enhances the cell
performance of ASSLSBs in the early cycles. In contrast, the
Li2S cathode causes inactivation through long-term cycles. The
capacities after the inactivation are entirely attributed to the
contribution of the decomposition products. This demon-
strates that the decomposition behavior of electrolytes
dominates the entire cell performance in ASSLSBs. The
increasing milling rotation speed in SE mixing for cathode
composite fabrication gains a larger effective contact area at the
Li2S/SE interface and thus facilitates the decomposition
reaction of SEs. This leads to an increase in the charge
transfer resistance attributed to the decomposition products.
The increasing decomposition kinetics dramatically enhances
the capacity in the early cycle but degrades cycle stability.
Therefore, this ASSLSB reaction chemistry indicates the need
for interface engineering and rational SE selection to control
the decomposition kinetics of SEs.
