The Role of Stack Pressure in Modulating Electrochemical Behavior 
of All‑Solid‑State Lithium–Sulfur Batteries
Daeun Lee1 · Youngseo Kim1 · Minjeong Shin1,2,3 
Received: 28 November 2024 / Revised: 7 January 2025 / Accepted: 11 January 2025 / Published online: 22 January 2025 
© The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Korean Institute of Chemical Engineers, Seoul, Korea 2025
Abstract
All-solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) represent a crucial frontier in energy storage research, promising higher 
energy densities and improved safety over traditional lithium-ion systems. Despite their advantages, ASSLSBs face signifi-
cant challenges, particularly in addressing interfacial instability and mechanical issues arising from the insulating nature and 
volume expansion of sulfur cathodes. Since the interfaces of all-solid-state batteries cannot accommodate the large volume 
changes, interfacial contact issues become increasingly pronounced in systems utilizing ­S8 conversion chemistry. Therefore, 
applying stack pressure during cell operation is considered a critical factor for optimizing the performance and cycle life of 
ASSLSB systems. In this study, we systematically investigated the impact of stack pressure on the electrochemical behavior 
of ASSLSBs under four different stack pressures. Electrochemical cycling results showed a marked difference in capacity 
retention—74% retention after 100 cycles at high pressure, while only 6% capacity retention was observed at low pressure. 
This pressure-dependent cycling performance was analyzed from two perspectives: the ­Li+/e⁻ transport properties and cath-
ode interfacial resistance. Detailed electrochemical characterizations revealed that low stack pressure leads to contact loss 
and deactivation of active material, which disrupts the effective ion transport pathways and increases interfacial resistance, 
significantly impairing the overall performance of the cell. This work highlights the critical role of stack pressure in enhanc-
ing the electrochemical performance of ASSLSBs, providing key insights for optimizing interfacial stability and transport 
properties in the field of all-solid-state batteries.
Keywords  All-solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries · Stack pressure · Galvanostatic intermittent titration technique · 
Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy · Interfacial resistance · Diffusion coefficient
Introduction
Lithium–sulfur batteries (LSBs) are regarded as a promis-
ing candidate for next-generation batteries due to the high 
theoretical capacity of ­S8 (1672 mAh ­g−1), high theoreti-
cal energy density (2600 Wh ­kg−1), and the low cost and 
abundant availability of sulfur [1–3]. However, LSBs face 
stability issues due to the flammability of the liquid electro-
lyte and the use of the highly reactive lithium metal anode 
[4]. Additionally, during charge/discharge processes, lithium 
polysulfide intermediates, which are soluble in most liquid 
electrolytes, dissolve into the electrolyte, causing issues such 
as the polysulfide shuttle effect, loss of active material, and 
rapid capacity fading [5, 6]. To address the challenges posed 
by polysulfide dissolution and the shuttle effect, extensive 
studies have explored strategies, such as electrolyte design 
[7–9], cathode structural modifications [10–12], and pro-
tective coatings for the lithium anode[13, 14]. Recently, 
all-solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs), which 
utilize solid electrolytes (SEs) in place of liquid electro-
lytes, have attracted considerable attention. In ASSLSBs, 
the reaction mechanism of ­S8 changes due to the absence of 
a medium in which polysulfides can dissolve. Instead of fol-
lowing a solution-mediated process involving the formation

of soluble polysulfide intermediates, the electrochemical 
reaction proceeds via the formation of insoluble products, 
effectively suppressing the shuttle phenomenon [15, 16].
Despite such advantages, ASSLSBs face several critical 
issues. First, in contrast to conventional liquid electrolyte 
systems, the electrochemically active surface area in all-
solid-state batteries (ASSBs) is restricted due to the limited 
solid–solid point contact at the SE/electrode interface [17]. 
The limited ­Li+/e⁻ transport poses a significant challenge at 
the cathode interface, where the heterogeneous nature of the 
composite cathode—comprising active material, conductive 
carbon, and solid electrolyte—further hinders ion transport 
and reaction kinetics [18]. Specifically, cathode active mate-
rials that are not in optimal contact with other constituent 
phases become electrochemically isolated. Due to the insu-
lating nature of ­S8 and ­Li2S, issues such as electrochemical 
contact loss and deactivation become more pronounced in 
ASSLSBs.
Furthermore, the significant volume change during the 
conversion between ­S8 and ­Li2S induces mechanical stress 
and strain within the internal matrix. The brittle nature of the 
SE, characterized by its low fracture toughness and limited 
ability to absorb mechanical stress without cracking, limits 
its ability to accommodate stress, making it susceptible to 
cracking and leading to the degradation of the ASSBs [19, 
20]. The repeated expansion and contraction during cycling 
lead to the dynamic evolution of the cathode microstructure 
and interface, which affects the chemo-mechanical stabil-
ity of the cell. Excessive volume changes result in contact 
loss of the active material within the composite cathode, 
disrupting efficient charge-transfer pathways [19, 21]. Con-
sequently, the uneven interfacial contact in ASSLSBs accel-
erates sluggish reaction kinetics and the deactivation of cath-
ode active materials. Therefore, implementing strategies to 
regulate the interfacial contact in ASSBs is essential.
In ASSBs, applying stack pressure during battery opera-
tion to enhance interfacial properties has been widely 
adopted [22]. The significance of stack pressure in ASSBs 
has gained increasing attention, with several recent studies 
thoroughly investigating the impact of stack pressure. In a 
system using Li–In∣Li6PS5Cl (LPSCl)∣LiNi0.3Mn0.3Co0.3O2 
(NMC333), X-ray tomography experiments demonstrated 
that increasing the applied stack pressure reduced voids 
within the cathode composite and improved the contact 
area of the active material [21]. In addition, a study using 
­Li4Ti5O12 (LTO)∣Li10GeP2S12 (LGPS)∣LiNi0.5Mn0.3Co0.2O2 
(NMC532) systematically investigated the effect of stack 
pressure (1–17 MPa), and the results showed that increasing 
stack pressure enhanced the electrochemically active contact 
area and improved cell performance by mitigating contact 
loss [22]. Meanwhile, since ­S8 has lower ionic/electronic 
conductivity and a higher volume expansion rate compared 
to layered NMC cathodes, the charge transport properties 
within the cathode become even more critical in ASSLSB 
systems. Therefore, stack pressure is expected to play a cru-
cial role in regulating the cathode/SE interface in ASSLSBs, 
significantly influencing battery performance and cycle life.
In this work, we systematically evaluate the effect of stack 
pressure on the electrochemical properties of ASSLSBs. We 
utilized prelithiated ­Li2S as the active material instead of ­S8, 
as its pre-expanded structure offers buffer space to accom-
modate volume changes occurring during battery operation. 
A cathode composite consisting of ­Li2S, conductive carbon, 
and LPSCl in a weight ratio of 1:1:2 wt% was used to fabri-
cate Li–In∣LPSCl∣Li2S composite cells, which were cycled 
under stack pressures of 1.8 MPa, 3.5 MPa, 7.1 MPa, and 
10.6 MPa. Systematic analysis of varying stack pressures 
showed that higher pressures improved capacity, retention, 
reduced polarization, and enhanced rate capability. The 
mechanistic origins of these improvements in ASSLSBs 
were analyzed using galvanostatic intermittent titration 
technique (GITT) and electrochemical impedance spectros-
copy (EIS) measurements. This study provides fundamen-
tal insights into the effect of stack pressure on the dynamic 
interactions within the ASSLSB cathode interfaces.
Experimental Section
Materials Lithium sulfide (Thermo Fisher, 99.9% metal 
basis) and argyrodite Li₆PS₅Cl (CISOLD, 3 µm particle size) 
were used as received. Ketjenblack (Nouryon, EC-600JD) 
was prepared by in an agate mortar for 10 min, followed by 
heating in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for 8 h to remove mois-
ture. Lithium metal foil (0.15 mm thick) was purchased from 
HONJO metal and indium foil (0.127 mm thick, 99.99% 
metals basis) was obtained from Thermo Fisher.
Cathode composite preparation The ­Li2S composite cath-
ode consisted of ­Li2S, Ketjenblack (KB), and LPSCl. ­Li2S 
and LPSCl powders were ball-milled separately in a zirconia 
milling jars (25 mL) with zirconia balls (3 mm), sealed in 
an Ar-filled glovebox. For each powder, ball-milling was 
conducted at 500 rpm for 10 h using a Tencan planetary 
ball-mill XQM-0.4A. Dried KB was ground for 10 min 
in an Ar-filled glove box and then mixed with the previ-
ously milled ­Li2S and LPSCl powders in a weight ratio of 
­Li2S:KB:LPSCl (1:1:2 wt%). The mixed powder was ball-
milled at 500 rpm for 30 h to produce a ­Li2S/KB/LPSCl 
composite. The morphology and composition of the ­Li2S/
KB/LPSCl composite were analyzed using a JEOL JSM-
7500F scanning electron microscope.
Preparation of LPSCl-Li2S bilayer pellet and all-solid-
state cell assembly The LPSCl-Li2S bilayer pellet was 
prepared by sequentially placing 0.075 g of LPSCl and 
3–4 mg of ­Li2S/KB/LPSCl composite into a 6 mm-diam-
eter die, resulting in a ­Li2S loading of 0.11–0.14 mg ­cm−2.

The powders were compressed using a hydraulic

in an Ar-filled glovebox.
Electrochemical measurements The assembled cell stacks 
were placed in a custom-built stack pressure setup equipped 
with a force gauge (Fig. S1), and uniaxial pressures of 1.8, 
3.5, 7.1, and 10.6 MPa were applied throughout the elec-
trochemical measurement. The cells were allowed to rest at 
open-circuit potential (OCP) for 12 h prior to measurement. 
Galvanostatic cycling was performed using a WonATech 
battery cycler within a voltage cut-off range of 0.58–2.68 V 
vs. Li–In. (1.2–3.3 V vs. Li/Li+) at a current loading of 0.1 
C. Rate capability test was conducted by varying the C-rate 
from 0.05 C to 0.1 C, 0.2 C, 0.5 C, 1.0 C, and then back to 
0.05 C, with 5 cycles performed at each current load. Gal-
vanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) measure-
ments were performed by applying a 0.08 C current pulse for 
7.5 min, followed by a 150-min relaxation period. This cycle 
was repeated until the cut-off voltage was reached. All galva-
nostatic measurements were conducted using a WonATech 
battery cycler. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy 
(EIS) measurements were performed using a BioLogic 
SP-300 impedance analyzer. Potentiostatic EIS (PEIS) was 
conducted over a frequency range of 7 MHz to 1 kHz with 
a voltage amplitude of 10 mV. Distribution of relaxation 
times (DRT) analysis and EIS fitting were carried out using 
RelaxIS 3 software (Rhd Instruments).
Results and Discussion
In ASSLSB systems, cathode composites are typically pre-
pared by mixing the active material, carbon, and solid elec-
trolytes to make the active material ­(S8 or ­Li2S) electrochem-
ically active. However, achieving a uniform distribution of 
these components within the composite is challenging, even 
after extensive ball-milling [21]. Instead, as shown in the 
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images in Fig. S2, the 
components are unevenly dispersed, and agglomerates are 
observed. This suggests the presence of electrochemically 
isolated ­Li2S regions that are not in optimal contact with 
­Li+/e⁻ conducting matrix.
One effective way to enhance the electrochemical activity 
of ­Li2S is to apply pressure to establish intimate interfacial 
contacts [23, 24]. We note that the densification pressure 
applied to the bilayer pellet (LPSCl/Li2S composite) during 
the cell manufacturing process may also influence the cath-
ode microstructure [25, 26]. However, this study focuses on 
the external stack pressure applied during battery operation, 
with a consistent densification pressure of 370 MPa applied 
to all cells. As shown in Fig. S1 schematic, a force gauge 
was integrated into a custom-built all-solid-state setup to 
maintain constant uniaxial pressure during electrochemical 
cycling. In addition, a Li–In alloy anode was used instead 
of Li metal to minimize the cell degradation caused by the 
instability of the Li/SE interface [24]. It was observed that 
using a Li metal anode resulted in uncontrolled cell short-
circuiting (Fig. S3), whereas the Li–In alloy anode enabled 
stable charge–discharge cycling for over 100 cycles and 
improved the reproducibility of the cycling experiments 
[27].
First, the galvanostatic cycling performance of 
Li–In∣LPSCl∣Li2S composite cell was evaluated under 
stack pressures of 1.8, 3.5, 7.1, and 10.6 MPa (Figs. 1, 2). 
Figure 1a presents the discharge capacities of each cell and 
Fig. 1b shows corresponding Coulombic efficiency (CE) 
for each cycle. Initial cycle discharge capacities were 134 
mAh g⁻1, 213 mAh g⁻1, 254 mAh g⁻1, and 363 mAh g⁻1 at 
1.8 MPa, 3.5 MPa, 7.1 MPa, and 10.6 MPa, respectively. 
Higher operating pressure resulted in larger capacities, 
indicating a positive correlation between stack pressure 
and active material utilization. All cells under varying

In addition to higher initial cycle capacities, greater 
stack pressures improved capacity retention and cycling 
stability. After 100 cycles, discharge capacities were 7.4 
mAh g⁻1, 130 mAh g⁻1, 198 mAh g⁻1, and 268 mAh g⁻1, 
corresponding to the capacity retention of 6%, 61%, 78%, 
and 74% at 1.8 MPa, 3.5 MPa, 7.1 MPa, and 10.6 MPa, 
respectively. Notably, the cell under a low stack pressure 
of 1.8 MPa exhibited rapid capacity fading between 20 
and 40 cycles, with the capacity at cycle 40 dropping to 
21 mAh g⁻1 (~ 16% capacity retention). This suggests 
that actual electrochemical reactions are not occurring 
effectively. We speculate that physical contact loss of the 
­Li2S active material within the cathode composite aggra-
vates with cycling, and a pressure of 1.8 MPa is insuf-
ficient to prevent this contact loss [29, 30]. The cathode 
active material undergoes ~ 80% volume contraction and 
expansion during charge/discharge, significantly altering 
local contact and arrangement within the composite [31, 
32]. Repeated volume changes likely reduce the elec-
trochemically active ­Li2S regions, leading to severe cell 
degradation.
Figure 1b shows the CE during the charge/discharge pro-
cess. At a high stack pressure of 10.6 MPa, the cell main-
tains ~ 100% CE throughout cycling. In contrast, the cell 
under 1.8 MPa exhibits significant fluctuations in CE from 
the beginning. An interesting observation at 1.8 MPa pres-
sure is that during 20–40 cycle periods, where rapid capacity 
fading occurs as discussed in Fig. 1a, the CE also decreases. 
A CE lower than 100% indicates that the discharge capac-
ity in the ­nth cycle is smaller than the corresponding charge 
capacity. The reduction in discharge products available for 
subsequent charge reactions leads to a smaller charge cycle 
capacity in the (n + 1)th cycle, contributing to capacity fad-
ing. Therefore, insufficient stack pressure fails to effectively 
suppress contact loss caused by the volume expansion of the 
cathode, adversely affecting cell performance.

For 3.5 MPa, 7.1 MPa, and 10.6 MPa, the discharge over-
potential generally increases (i.e., decrease in cell potential) 
over 100 cycles, but the variations remain relatively small. 
In contrast, the rise in overpotential during charging is more 
pronounced. The increase in overpotential during cycling is 
smallest at 10.6 MPa, while the cell at 1.8 MPa shows a sig-
nificantly larger rise in overpotential. The large overpoten-
tial may stem from the intrinsic high resistance of the cell, 
limited ­Li+ diffusion, and the large kinetic barrier for the 
conversion reaction. Among the various origins of overpo-
tential, (1) ohmic overpotential—associated with the intrin-
sic ­Li+/e⁻ conductivity and cell contact resistance—and (2) 
concentration overpotential—linked to ­Li+ diffusion within 
the electrolyte, electrodes, and their interfaces[35]—are 
strongly influenced by stack pressure. Higher stack pressure 
likely reduces the ohmic resistance of the cell and enhances 
Li⁺ diffusion within the composite electrode by increasing 
the physical contact area between active material and SE, 
which will be further discussed in more detail [22].
Figure 3 shows the C-rate-dependent cycling perfor-
mance of the Li–In|LPSCl|Li2S cell under stack pressures of 
1.8 MPa and 10.6 MPa. The C-rate was varied from 0.05 C 
to 0.1 C, 0.2 C, 0.5 C, 1 C, and then back to 0.05 C, and the 
capacity retention and Coulombic efficiency were analyzed 
each step. Discharge capacities were normalized to the 1st 
cycle discharge capacity of each pressure to enable compari-
son of C-rate dependent capacity variations. As shown in 
Fig. 3a, an increase in C-rate leads to progressive capacity 
decay under both pressure conditions. At high current load-
ing, the diffusion of ­Li+ through the solid electrolyte and 
into the cathode composite, as well as the sulfur reaction 
kinetics, is not fast enough to replenish the depleted ­Li+.
[36, 37] The more rapid capacity fade observed at 1.8 MPa 
compared to 10.6 MPa suggests that ­Li+ diffusion and reac-
tion kinetics are further impeded under lower stack pres-
sure. The transport of ­Li+ and e⁻ is affected by the internal 
pathways within the electrode [29, 38]. In all-solid-state 
batteries, unlike liquid electrolyte systems, poor solid–solid 
contact and the presence of voids or insulating materials 
within the electrode hinder ion transport across interfaces 
[38, 39]. In this regard, the significant volume change and 
insulating nature of sulfur cathodes likely lead to the forma-
tion of unfavorable pathways for ion transport at low stack 
pressure, resulting in a poor rate capability. When the C-rate 
returns back to 0.05 C, the capacity retention is 89.1% and 
101.5% at 1.8 MPa and 10.8 MPa, indicating better capacity 
recovery at higher pressure.
Next, we discuss the Coulombic efficiency variations at 
each current loading. When the C-rate in increased, the ini-
tial CE of each current step is smaller than 100%. Larger 
deviations from 100% are observed at higher C-rates, likely 
due to the changes in the amount of active material that can 
reversibly participate in electrochemical charge/discharge 
[40]. The cell at 10.8 MPa exhibits less CE variation com-
pared to the cell at 1.8 MPa and recovers to nearly 100% CE. 
In contrast, at lower pressure, the recovery rate declines as 
the C-rate increases, with the final cycle at 1.0 C showing a 
CE of only 74.4%. This result suggests that higher pressure 
facilitates the formation of a more favorable ­Li+/e⁻ transport 
pathway, mitigating contact loss capacity decay caused by 
significant volume changes.

Next, ­Li+ diffusion coefficients (D) were extracted from 
the GITT measurements (Fig. 4b). Larger diffusion coeffi-
cients indicate faster reaction kinetics and more uniform ion 
transport within the composite, resulting in enhanced active 
material utilization and improved rate performance [43]. 
Although it is challenging to use GITT to accurately extract 
diffusion coefficients for composites or materials undergoing 
phase transitions, it remains a valuable method for investi-
gating different trends between comparative groups under 
the same experimental conditions. As shown in Fig. 4b, vari-
ations in diffusion coefficients during cycling are observed 
in both cells, with a gradual decrease during the charge 
cycle, a sharp increase at the onset of the discharge cycle, 
and a subsequent decrease during the discharge cycle. The 
variations of diffusion coefficients during cycling are closely 
associated with the conversion reaction between ­Li2S and ­S8, 
which involves significant volume changes and the dynamic 
evolution of the cathode microstructure [44]. Similar trends 
are observed in both cells under different stack pressures, 
but the cell subjected to higher stack pressure exhibits con-
sistently larger diffusion coefficients throughout the charge/
discharge cycle. Higher stack pressure likely enhances inter-
facial contact, enabling more efficient ion transport within 
the ­Li2S composite and improving reaction kinetics. The 
larger diffusion coefficients observed at high pressure corre-
late well with the battery cycling results, which demonstrate 
high capacity, reduced cell polarization, and enhanced rate 
capability.

of the Li–In|LPSCl|Li2S composite cells were performed 
to investigate the origin of improved cycling performance 
under high stack pressures. Figure 5a shows the Nyquist 
plot obtained after the 12 h OCP hold, before applying the 
galvanostatic current load. The Nyquist plot shows several 
semicircles followed by a low-frequency tail, and the overall 
cell resistance decreases with increasing stack pressure. The 
EIS spectra of the as-prepared cells were deconvoluted using 
the distributional relaxation time (DRT) analysis, with the 
corresponding DRT curves shown in Fig. 5d. The DRT tech-
nique identifies suitable equivalent circuit models by extract-
ing characteristic time constants and resistances associated 
with various electrochemical processes in battery systems 
[45]. The DRT curves of the as-prepared cells reveal three 
distinct relaxation time constants (τ), each corresponding 
to a specific electrochemical process. The peak at τ = ­10–7 s 
represents the grain boundary resistance (RGB) of LPSCl 
[46]. The low-frequency feature at τ = 3 × ­10–4 s likely cor-

a). To further investigate this possibility, the EIS evolution

Conclusion
This study underscores the critical role of stack pressure 
in modulating the electrochemical behavior of ASSLSBs 
by altering solid–solid interfacial contact and influencing 
interactions within the cathode microstructures. Galvano-
static charge/discharge cycling experiments showed that 
higher stack pressures resulted in a larger capacity, improved 
capacity retention, and enhanced rate capability. In addition, 
the effect of stack pressure on ­Li+ ion transport and interfa-
cial properties was investigated using GITT and EIS meas-
urements. At low stack pressure, chemo-mechanical stress 
induced by electrode volume changes is not effectively buff-
ered, reducing the effective contact area at the interface. This 
results in increased interfacial resistance and poor transport 
properties, which progressively cause contact loss and iso-
lation of the active material during cycling. Consequently, 
these effects lead to large cell polarization and severe capac-
ity fading under low stack pressure. At high stack pressure, 
stable interfacial contact is established and maintained 
throughout cycling, effectively mitigating volume changes 
and preventing active material contact loss. This results in 
superior capacity retention, improved rate capability, and 
prolonged cycle life. The insights gained from this work 
lay a foundation for optimizing stack pressure as a practical 
strategy to address critical challenges in ASSLSBs, while 
also providing valuable guidance for interfacial engineering 
in all-solid-state batteries, paving the way for their broader 
adoption in next-generation energy storage systems.
