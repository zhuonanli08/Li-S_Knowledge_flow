ABSTRACT
Sulfide-based all-solid-state batteries (ASSBs) are promising next-generation energy storage devices, yet their practical application
is hindered by pressure dependence—arising from electrolyte brittleness and interfacial contact loss under dynamic volume
changes during cycling, which demands high stacking/operating pressures. Herein, we design sulfide-based composite solid
electrolytes (CSEs) with organic components and systematically investigate their functional mechanisms. Mechanical-physical
tests reveal that the organic components enhance initial solid-solid contact via adhesive effects and forming transitional
interface, while buffering volume-pressure variations via elastic strain accommodation. Electrochemical characterizations further
verify these stabilizing effects. Using lithium–sulfur batteries as a model system, the CSE-based ASSBs achieved stable long-term
cycling (over 1000 cycles without capacity decay) under low pressure (12.5 MPa), and retained 78% capacity after 300 cycles at 5 MPa
(compatible with practical manufacturing conditions). The CSE’s effectiveness was also validated for other cathode systems. This
work not only decouples the mechanical-electrochemical roles of organic components in CSEs but also provides mechanistic
insights for electrolyte layer design, guiding the development of pressure-independent ASSBs toward scalable production.
1
Introduction
All-solid-state batteries (ASSBs) have witnessed remarkable
advancements in recent years, emerging as a promising candidate
for next-generation energy storage devices. Inorganic electrolytes,
typified by sulfides, have garnered extensive attention from both
academia and industry [1–3]. Sulfide solid electrolytes (SSE)
exhibit high ionic conductivity comparable to liquid electrolytes,
with superior mechanical strength and thermal stability, position-
ing them as the performance benchmark for electrolyte layers
in future battery applications [4, 5]. However, their inherent
brittleness poses challenges in maintaining interfacial contact
with cathodes and achieving favorable processability, which limit
improvements in battery cycling stability and energy density.
Moreover, the need for high operating pressures (dozens or
hundreds of MPa) has constrained their practical application
[6–8].
The reliance of sulfide-based ASSBs on high operating pres-
sures stems from two primary factors [8]. First, densifica-
tion of battery components and maintaining contact at rigid
electrode-electrolyte interfaces necessitate external macroscopic
mechanical force [9, 10]. Second, non-uniform volume expansion
and contraction of battery components (e.g., anode or cath-
ode particles) during charge–discharge cycles require sustained
external pressure during battery operation to preserve interfacial

contact and stabilize electrochemical reactions [11, 12]. To
mitigate this pressure dependence and enhance the sustained
interfacial contact during battery cycling, optimizations target-
ing various battery components have been explored. From the
cathode perspective, strategies including surface modification of
cathode active materials (CAM) [13, 14], leveraging the mechan-
ical balance and synergistic effects of multi-component cathodes
[15], developing new cathode systems with low strain rates [16],
and structural design and component matching of composite
cathodes [17, 18] have been employed. From the anode per-
spective, approaches such as optimizing lithium deposition and
stripping via alloying [19], constructing interlayers to promote
uniform lithium diffusion [20], and designing composite anodes
[21] have been utilized.
Additionally, surface modification to construct functional elec-
trolytes for dynamically adaptive interfaces [22] and electrolyte–
electrode co-pressing structure design [23] can also mitigate
interface-induced side effects. From the perspective of modi-
fying the electrolyte layer itself, sulfide-based composite solid
electrolytes (CSEs) have been actively investigated, with the
incorporation of soft, adhesive, or processable organic compo-
nents to address the aforementioned challenges [24, 25]. Notably,
numerous studies on composite electrolytes have demonstrated
the beneficial effects of organic components in CSEs on energy
density [26], mechanical properties [27], and electrochemical
performance [28]. However, the precise mechanism by which
these binders influence the actual cycling behavior of batteries
remains unclear.
In this work, we systematically decouple the physical, mechani-
cal, and electrochemical roles played by the organic component in
CSEs and their application in ASSBs. Specifically, from a physical
standpoint, the adhesive property of the organic component
strengthens and enhances initial interfacial contact between the
electrolyte and electrode particles by facilitating the formation of
a mortise-tenon-joint structure. Mechanically, its elasticity, anal-
ogous to that observed in elastomeric or polymeric electrolytes,
provides strain accommodation and stress buffering capabilities
to stabilize internal volume and pressure fluctuations during
cycles. Within this analytical framework, we quantitatively
demonstrate that CSEs enhance both static interfacial contact and
dynamic contact under volume and pressure variations, using
lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries with severe volume expansion
as a representative system. The resulting CSE-based Li-S cells
exhibited stable long cycling under a low operating pressure of 5
MPa: through an innovative stepwise pressure reduction protocol,
they retained 78% of their capacity after 300 cycles. This marks the
first demonstration of stable operation in sulfide electrolyte-based
all-solid-state Li-S batteries under pressure conditions compatible
with practical manufacturing requirements. Our findings offer
design principles for mitigating pressure dependence in future
ASSBs through tailored electrolyte layer engineering.
2
Results and Discussion
2.1
Characteristics of CSE
Figure 1 illustrates the mechanism by which deformable binders
in electrolytes mitigate the operating pressure of batteries. When
non-uniform expansion stress from the electrode layer propa-
gates to the electrolyte layer, soft, and highly elastic organic
composite components, which are uniformly distributed within
the CSE, relieve horizontal stress concentration through their
inherent reversible elastic deformation. This process simultane-
ously endows the CSE with an improved capability for reversible
volume change. This enhanced volume variation capacity of
the electrolyte layer serves as the basis for mitigating pressure
fluctuations and contact loss on the cathode side during battery
operation.
Beyond the mechanical buffering provided by the intrinsic elastic
deformation and adhesive properties of the organic components,
which mitigate stress in the CSE and the entire battery, the
integrated composite structure of the CSE and electrodes further
enhances the electrode-electrolyte interfacial contact. During co-
formation with the cathode, the loose, high-specific-surface-area
electrolyte membrane (before cold pressing) forms an interlock-
ing mortise-tenon structure. This phenomenon is attributed to
the differential deformation of organic and inorganic components
(possessing distinct moduli) in the CSE during co-pressing with
the cathode layer, enabling cathode materials to penetrate the
electrolyte layer to varying depths. After densification under
high stacking pressure, this uniform horizontal penetration is
maintained through organic adhesion, forming a transition layer
with enhanced interfacial contact. Consequently, this struc-
ture mitigates contact failure induced by volume variations of
electrode active materials during charge–discharge cycles.
2.2
Elastic Effect of CSE
A representative self-supporting flexible CSE membrane com-
posed of Li5.5PS4.5ClBr0.5 (LPSCB) as electrolyte and styrene-
ethylene-butylene-styrene (SEBS) as organic additive is fabricated
by slurry blade casting (see the Method section and Note S1,
Figures S1–S6)). Similar to many previous studies [24, 26, 29,
30], the binder endows the CSE membrane with bendability and
flexibility, as well as the ability to be easily fabricated into thin
films with adjustable thickness (Figure 2a and Figure S2). A
uniformly distributed binder plays a crucial role in enhancing
the mechanical properties of the composite material [31, 32].
Typical morphology of the CSE membrane is reconstructed by
X-Ray computed tomography (XCT) (Figure 2b,c), indicating the
dispersion state of the two components in the membrane. Raman
spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction (XRD), and X-ray photoelectron
spectroscopy (XPS) analyses (Figures S3 and S4) demonstrate
that no detectable changes in the crystal structure or chemical
composition of LPSCB occur after introducing SEBS. Therefore,
the performance variations between CSE and SSE are primar-
ily attributed to the differences in physical and mechanical
properties induced by SEBS.
Figure 2d and Figure S5 demonstrate how the elastic polymer
binder enhances electrode-electrolyte contact. Through quan-
titative nanomechanical mapping (QNM) analysis (Figure 2d),
we observe a substantial reduction in the electrolyte’s elastic
modulus, with the median value dropping from 20.5 to 3.08 GPa
(spatial distribution in Figure S7). This mechanical softening pro-
vides three key advantages: it resists lithium dendrite penetration
[33, 34], provides greater strain tolerance, and improves overall

the unloading stage, the proportion of elastic deformation in CSE

CSE possesses a stronger ability to buffer electrode strain during

The elastic effect of CSE induced by SEBS is also reflected
by its impact on the ionic conductivity. Compared to SSE,
the conductivity of CSE decreases significantly (Figure 2e). A
higher SEBS content leads to a more pronounced decrease in
conductivity (Figure S3). This decrease is partially attributed to
the insulating nature of the binder. However, the conductivity
calculated solely based on the percolation effect of composites
should be higher than the measured value (Note S2), indicating

decreases with increasing binder content, as depicted in Figure
S6. This reduction in packing density is attributed to the elastic
recovery capability of the SEBS binder, which induces a certain
strain relaxation effect in the CSE after pressing [31].
2.3
Enhanced Interfacial Connection of CSE
To substantiate the aforementioned mortise-tenon-joint mech-
anism, a sulfur cathode system was selected to pair with the
CSE. Owing to the redox reactions involving sulfur species trans-
formation, sulfur cathodes undergo significant volume changes
(typically exceeding 80% [36]), thereby generating substantial
mechanical stress and strain. During repeated charge–discharge
cycles, excessive volume expansion and contraction induce
microstructural alterations within active material particles [37].
These changes lead to irreversible, severe contact loss, which,
in turn, impairs the operational stability of Li-S ASSBs. Thus,
the utilization of sulfur cathodes can better amplify the physical-
mechanical benefits of CSE.
A sulfur-based cathode was designed based on the dispropor-
tionation reaction (Note S3, Figures S8 and S9). When this
composite cathode was co-cold-pressed with CSE, a transitional
interlocking structure between the cathode and electrolyte was
formed (Figure 3a, Figures S10, and S11). In contrast, the interface
obtained by cold pressing the conventional SSE and cathode was
flat (Figure 3b).
To investigate the effect of the interfacial morphology on elec-
trochemical processes, Tafel curves were measured for cathode
symmetric cells containing different electrolytes (Figure 3c,d).
The exchange current density was then determined to character-
ize charge transfer kinetics at the electrode-electrolyte interface.
Comparative analysis revealed that at a testing pressure of
12.5 MPa, the exchange current density of CSE-cathode sym-
metric cells increased by more than 20% compared to that of
SSE-cathode symmetric cells, confirming that the CSE membrane
enhances the kinetic activity of the electrode reactions.
The exchange current density is dependent on both interfacial
properties and true interfacial contact area. Given that the
interfaces formed by the CSE and SSE with the electrode share
the same charge transfer mechanism and tightness of interfacial
contact, the higher exchange current density observed for the
CSE indicates a larger true interfacial area. This finding pro-
vides further verification for the proposed mortise-tenon-joint
mechanism. This unique structure enables the actual contact
area of CSE, when co-pressed with the cathode, to exceed the
cross-sectional area typical of a rigid, circular interface.
To eliminate the influence of the test pressure variable, Figure S12
presents the exchange current density for SSE-cathode symmetric
cells tested at a higher pressure of 125 MPa. Although the
exchange current density increased under this elevated pressure
compared to lower operating pressures, it remained lower than
that achieved in the CSE-cathode system. This result not only
confirms that testing pressure influences cathode mass transport
by altering the intimacy of interfacial contact but also further
demonstrates that CSE can reduce the battery’s operational
reliance on high external pressures.
The mortise-tenon-joint interfacial structure, characterized by
higher interfacial roughness and contact area, can effectively
reduce interfacial impedance and facilitate mass transport. This
stands in contrast to the conventional smooth SSE-cathode inter-
face that requires high operating pressure (∼100 MPa) to maintain
the contact necessary for ion and electron transport. A reduction
in operating pressure often causes interfacial delamination in
SSEs, leading to high interfacial impedance, capacity loss, and
eventual battery failure [8, 12, 38]. The superior performance of
the flexible composite electrolyte (CSE) is clearly demonstrated
in Figure S13, which compares the interfacial impedance of sulfur
cathode symmetric cells employing CSE and SSE electrolytes
under varying pressures. Although both cell systems exhibit a
substantial rise in overall impedance with reduced pressure,
cells with the flexible CSE consistently maintain a signifi-
cantly lower absolute impedance. This performance advantage
is directly attributed to the CSE’s superior interfacial contact,
which ensures stable cell operation even under low-pressure
conditions.

deconvolute the respective contributions (Figure 3d,e, and Figure

The practical battery assembly process follows a sequence of
cold-pressing, standby, and operation. During the standby period,
processes like strain relaxation [39–41] can degrade the intimacy
of interfacial contact, a phenomenon particularly detrimental
to interface stability under lower stack pressures. This effect is
corroborated by the impedance and DRT analysis in Figures
S13 and S14, which compare the two aforementioned battery

the resting period induces a significant increase in interfacial
impedance. However, in contrast to the interface formed with
rigid electrolytes, the CSE-cathode structure can effectively coun-
teract such strain recovery through mechanisms of physical
interlocking structures and adhesive effects, thereby promoting
superior interfacial stability.
Notably, this interfacial improvement is not unlimited. As shown
in Figures S13 and S14, which present the EIS Nyquist plots
and DRT analysis after the external pressure is completely
removed, i.e., under 0 MPa, although CSE cells exhibit lower
absolute impedance than their SSE counterparts, the electrode-
electrolyte interfacial impedance still approaches 10 kΩ. This
residual impedance makes it challenging to maintain a normal
charge–discharge process.

To further validate the enhanced interfacial contact and the
ability of CSE to mitigate the volume expansion of sulfur cath-

of 12.5 MPa. As shown in Figure 4b, a cell with a low areal sulfur

exhibited significant operational instability (Figure S17a). Figure
S17b presents the corresponding voltage profiles and in situ
pressure variation, revealing pressure fluctuations of 6%–12% per
cycle, which were more pronounced in the initial stages. The
rigid SSE structure lacks the capacity for effective strain accom-
modation and internal stress dissipation, leading to a progressive
decline in the cell’s actual operating pressure. The resulting
irreversible loss of interfacial contact subsequently causes a sharp
drop in the amount of electrochemically active cathode material,
resulting in capacity fading to near-zero within a few cycles—a
phenomenon further evidenced by the progressive shortening of
charge–discharge plateaus.

attributed to several interconnected mechanisms. Sulfur cath-
odes often exhibit a capacity rise phase during the initial
cycling stage. This “activation” process arises from an internal
component. The controlled electrochemical decomposition of
the LPSCB electrolyte releases capacity, and its decomposition

The cathode activation process is evident in the evolving shape of

with the increase in cell capacity.
The interfacial contact of full cells was characterized using SEM.
Under SEM observation conditions (without external pressure),
the poor contact between the sulfide SSE and the composite
cathode immediately after cell assembly manifests as continuous
gaps (Figure S19a), exhibiting a state of partial adhesion and par-

no voids (Figure S19b), largely due to the organic binder and
electrode-electrolyte transitional structure. This robust contact
is maintained even in the absence of external pressure, making
it resistant to delamination. Such intimate contact effectively
mitigates interfacial delamination between the electrode and
electrolyte layers caused by sulfur cathode volume expansion
during charge–discharge cycles, thereby addressing capacity loss
issues.
Cross-sectional SEM images of the CSE and SSE in sulfur cathode
batteries after cycling are presented in Figure 4c,d. Notably,
the CSE battery maintains excellent interfacial contact with the
cathode. In comparison, the SSE battery cycled at 125 MPa
shows significant electrolyte-cathode separation upon removal of
operating pressure.
To investigate the interface evolution during cell cycling and
assess its impact on cycling performance, we conduct DRT
analysis of cell impedances throughout cycling, as shown in
Figure 4e–g and Figures S20–S25. Figures S20 and S21, respec-

the discharged state over the first five cycles under an operating
pressure of 125 MPa. In Figure S20a, the shift of the cathode
interface impedance peak to higher frequencies and its reduced
magnitude confirm interface optimization and cathode material
activation, corroborating the initial capacity increase in full
cells assembled with CSE. Meanwhile, Figure S20b reveals a
continuous decrease in bulk impedance over the first five cycles,
indicating improved electrode-electrolyte wettability, interface
reconstruction, and likely activation of the cathode material

activation stage and stable stage under 12.5 MPa, indicating that
after activation stabilizes, the interface impedance in each cycle
primarily originates from regular active material transformation.
Specifically, charged-state products exhibit low electrical conduc-
tivity and severe volume shrinkage, leading to increased interface
impedance.
Figure 4e and Figure 4f present the charged-state DRT profiles
of CSE and SSE cells during the first 5 cycles at 125 MPa.
Notably, the CSE cell exhibits an extremely low and essentially
unchanged absolute impedance in the charged state during
cycling, attributed to the electrolyte membrane dynamically
compensating for voids induced by sulfur shrinkage. In con-
trast, the SSE cell lacking deformability fails to provide such
compensation, resulting in an impedance increase with cycling.
Figure S23 indicates that SSE-based batteries also face similar
contact loss during the charging process. Figure 4g compares the
variations of impedance contributions during discharge under
varied pressures for CSE- and SSE-based cells. The left axis
of Figure 4g represents the proportional relationship between
the impedance induced by electrolyte-cathode interfacial contact
(region IV) and the impedance arising from the solid-phase diffu-
sion process in the DRT analysis (Figures S24 and S25). Notably,
under lower operating pressure (12.5 MPa), the electrolyte expe-
riences more severe interface and diffusion impedance. The
larger impedance ratio of interfacial contact to charge transfer
(solid-phase diffusion) in SSE cells suggests that, compared
to CSE cells, interface contact issues are more critical than
mass transfer/diffusion limitations. Figure S25 further reveals
a shift of mediate-frequency peaks (corresponding to initial
interfaces/contact between constituent components and charge
transfer in the composite cathode) to low frequencies alongside
decreased resistance, which is attributable to the transition from
high-resistance interfacial reactions confined to the surface of
insulating sulfur particles to low-resistance (albeit kinetically
slower) bulk diffusion and re-reaction of polysulfides within
the entire conductive network [47, 48]. The resistance decrease
results from improved interfacial contact, material redistribution,
and enhanced product conductivity.
2.5
Pressure Adjustment Strategy Toward
Low-Pressure Operation
As noted earlier, the stress-strain buffering capability of CSE
is becoming insufficient under low pressures. In Li-S batteries
with poor cathode mass transfer capability, when the operating
pressure was further reduced to 5 MPa, even with the functional
advantages of CSE, it cannot fully offset the contact deterioration
caused by abrupt pressure reduction. As shown in Figure S26,
CSE cells not only delivered a much lower initial capacity (548.6
mAh g−1), but also suffered rapid capacity decay, with a capacity
retention of 33.3% by the third cycle. The previously observed
initial capacity increase was also absent, indicating that the
cathode activation process was suppressed.

3
Conclusion
In this study, we systematically demonstrate the advantages of
elastic organic binder components in sulfide-based composite
electrolytes and their full cells, with a particular focus on
enhancing mechanical function, physical contact, and electro-
chemical performance. These benefits are analyzed from three
key perspectives: (1) the provision of an elastic strain space to
accommodate volume changes, (2) the formation of interlock-
ing transition structures that improve interfacial stability, and
(3) the adhesive effect that ensures robust electrode-electrolyte
contact.
Using high-volume-change S cathodes as a representative exam-
ple, we show that composite electrolytes with these charac-
teristics effectively mitigate contact degradation and capacity
loss caused by volume-pressure variations during operation.
Through a stepwise pressure compensation strategy, the Li-S
battery maintains stable performance even at a low operating
pressure of 5 MPa, a condition that meets practical production
requirements.
Furthermore, this work provides a generalizable framework
for designing composite electrolytes with tailored mechanical
properties to match varied cathodes (e.g., NCM, FeS2) and
low-pressure assembly requirements. The proposed pressure-
tuning strategy (high-pressure activation followed by low-
pressure operation) offers a practical pathway for scaling
up ASSBs, as it reduces the reliance on complex pressure-
maintaining equipment in manufacturing and operation. Over-
all, this study advances the fundamental understanding of
electrolyte–electrode mechanical interactions and paves the
way for the development of next-generation high-performance
ASSBs.
4
Methods
4.1
Preparation of Sulfide Electrolyte and
Sulfide-Composite Electrolyte Membrane
The Li5.5PS4.5ClBr0.5 (LPSCB) solid electrolyte was synthesized
using a solid-phase reaction method. The starting materials—
Li2S (Meryer, 99.9%), P2S5 (Sigma–Aldrich, 99%), LiCl (Aladdin,
99%), and LiBr (Aladdin, 99.99%)—were weighed according to the
target stoichiometric ratio. The mixture was then ball-milled in a
planetary ball mill (FRITSCH PULVERISETTE 7 premium line)
at 400 rpm for 8 h. Subsequently, the milled powder was pressed
into pellets and annealed at 480◦C for 16 h. After naturally cooling
to room temperature, the resulting product underwent secondary
ball milling followed by sieving to obtain a fine and homogeneous
powder.
The composite electrolyte membrane was fabricated by the slurry-
casting technique. Initially, 250 mg of Styrene Ethylene Butylene
Styrene (SEBS) was dissolved in 10 mL of toluene solvent, which
had been pre-dried with molecular sieves to remove moisture.
The electrolyte slurry was then prepared by mixing LPSCB
powder with the polymer solution and additional toluene solvent
at 2500 rpm for 5 min. The resulting slurry was cast onto a PET
plate using a doctor blade and left to dry at room temperature for
1 h and overnight at 60◦C.
All of the above preparation steps were carried out in an Ar-filled
atmosphere (O2 ≤0.1 ppm, H2O ≤0.1 ppm) to prevent moisture
and oxygen contamination.
4.2
Preparation of Sulfur Composite Cathode
The fine-sulfur/multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNT) com-
posite cathode was prepared by an extraction-like process based
on the method reported by Chen et al. [66]. In a typical procedure,
submicron to micrometer-sized sulfur particles were first synthe-
sized via the disproportionation reaction of sodium thiosulfate in
hydrochloric acid. These sulfur particles were then mixed with
MWCNTs and the LPSCB electrolyte by ball milling at 400 rpm
for 1 h.
4.3
Materials Characterization
The sample morphology was investigated by scanning electron
microscopy (Zeiss Merlin field-emission). Three-dimensional
internal structure was probed using micro-computed tomography
(Zeiss/Xradia Versa 620), with the resulting data sets recon-
structed and analyzed using Dragonfly software. Nanomechan-
ical properties, specifically Young’s modulus, were measured
via Atomic Force Microscopy (BRUKER Dimension FastScan)
operated in the Quantitative Nanomechanical Mapping (QNM)
mode. The specific surface area was determined by the Brunauer-
Emmett-Teller analysis using a Quadrasorb SI-MP system. The
mechanical properties of SSEs were measured using a nano-
indentation system (MML Nano indenter) under a constant load
of 0.5 mN. Before testing, CSE and SSE were first pressed at
250 MPa.
4.4
Electrochemical Measurements
The ionic conductivity (σ) of the electrolyte was evaluated
by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) using an
impedance analyzer (ZAHNER-elektrik IM6). The electrolyte
pellets (or membranes) were sandwiched between two stainless
steel rods acting as current collectors. The EIS measurement was
conducted at room temperature over a frequency range of 1 Hz–
3 MHz with an AC amplitude of 10 mV. σ was calculated using the
equation σ = L/(RS), where L is the thickness, S is the area of the
pellet, and R is the bulk resistance obtained from the impedance
spectrum.

4.5
Cell Fabrication and Measurements
Symmetric cells with electrolyte and positive electrode material
were assembled. A CSE or SSE layer was first placed in a 10 mm
PEEK die and pressed at 250 MPa. Then, the cathode composite
powder (5 mg) was pressed on both sides of the electrolyte pellet
or membrane at 625 MPa. And the electrochemical performances
were tested at 0–500 MPa using a battery test system (LAND,
C2001A).
For the fabrication of All-Solid-State Lithium–Sulfur Batteries,
a CSE or SSE layer was first placed in a 10 mm PEEK die and
pressed at 250 MPa. Then, the cathode composite powder (about
2–10 mg) was pressed on the top of the electrolyte pellet or
membrane at 625 MPa. Next, a piece of Li-In alloy foil (Li0.5In)
was placed on the other side of the SSE layer and pressed under
100 MPa. As for the fabrication of full cells, the negative to
positive electrode capacity (N/P) ratio was set at ∼1.3. All cells
were assembled in a glove box (H2O and O2 ≤0.01 ppm) and
tested under a stack pressure of 5–125 MPa. The charge−discharge
behavior of the cells was tested using a battery test system (LAND,
C2001A) under a cutoff voltage of 0.0−3.0 V (vs. Li−In) at RT.
In each comparative full cell test group involving CSE and SSE,
we maintained the same areal loading of the cathode active
materials.
The FeS2-based composite cathodes were prepared by mixing
FeS2 powder (Macklin, 99%), LPSCB, and acetylene black in a
weight ratio of 20:40:4 using a mortar and pestle for 0.5 h. The
LiNbO3-coated LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 (NCM811) (Canrd) and the
obtained LPSCB and VGCF were mixed by mortar and pestle for
0.5 h with a weight ratio of 70:30:2. The FeS2 and NCM811 full
cells were prepared following the same steps as the Sulfur-based
ASSBs.
4.6
Stress Measurements
Stress measurements were conducted using a force sensor (Bei-
jing Scistar Technology Co. Ltd) with a selectable range of
20–100 MPa. The force sensor was connected to a data collector on
a computer, enabling real-time data visualization. Before starting
the stress tests, the force sensor was calibrated to ensure accuracy.
After applying the initial external pressure, all cells were held
at open circuit for over 12 h before cycling to allow for stress
relaxation and stabilization.
