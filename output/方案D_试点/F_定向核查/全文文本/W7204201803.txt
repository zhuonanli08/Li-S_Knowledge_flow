ABSTRACT
The practical implementation of Li2S cathodes in all-solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries is severely hindered by sluggish
ionic/electronic transport and a high activation barrier, particularly under high-mass-loading conditions. Herein, lithium
thiostannate (Li4SnS4) is introduced as a molecular mediator to activate Li2S redox chemistry. In situ growth of Li4SnS4 on Li2S
constructs a continuous Li+-transport network, increasing the ionic conductivity from 2.25 × 10−5 to 3.99 × 10−5 S cm−1. More
importantly, the hybridization between unsaturated Sn p orbitals and interfacial sulfur atoms induces the formation of Sn─S bonds,
leading to local electron-density redistribution around sulfur. This interfacial electronic reconstruction weakens Li+ confinement
within the sulfur lattice, softens the Li─S bond, and lowers the oxidation barrier, thereby reducing the initial charge potential from
2.94 to 2.41 V. Consequently, the Li2S@Li4SnS4 cathode delivers stable cycling over 100 cycles under demanding conditions of 50
wt.% Li2S and areal loading of 5 mg cm−2. Moreover, a prototype pouch cell retains sufficient capacity to power a light-emitting
diode panel after 100 cycles. This work demonstrates that interfacial electronic-structure engineering offers an effective strategy
for activating Li2S cathodes toward high-energy all-solid-state Li-S batteries.

Introduction
All-solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) have emerged
as a transformative technology for next-generation electrified
transportation and grid-scale energy storage, offering high energy
density and intrinsic safety over conventional lithium-ion batter-
ies [1–3]. Within this landscape, the Li2S cathode stands out as
a pivotal enabler. As the fully lithiated counterpart of elemental
sulfur, Li2S not only delivers a comparable theoretical capacity of
1166 mAh g−1 but also unlocks the potential for “anode-free” cell
configurations, a critical pathway to maximizing practical energy
density [4–6]. Moreover, its pre-expanded state avoids the severe
volume expansion of sulfur cathodes, allowing for more robust
electrode architectures [7–9]. Despite these compelling attributes,
Li2S cathodes face critical challenges from high charge potential
(>4 V vs. Li/Li+) required for Li─S bond dissociation during the
initial charging, coupled with their inherently insulating nature
in both electronic (∼10−9 S cm−1) and ionic (∼10−13 S cm−1)
conductivity [10–12]. These kinetic bottlenecks cause low active
material utilization and severe cell polarization, particularly

Extensive efforts have been devoted to overcoming these kinetic
limitations, including nanostructuring of Li2S or solid-state
electrolytes (SSE) [13–15], incorporation of catalysts [16–18] or
redox mediators [19–21], and elemental doping [22–24]. While

face inherent constraints. For instance, nanosizing Li2S shortens

Li─S bond energy that governs the activation barrier, and often
compromises cathode uniformity due to particle agglomera-
tion [25–28]. Similarly, the limited solid-solid contact between
catalysts and active materials in ASSLSBs hinders the forma-
tion of molecular-level interfaces required for effective catalysis
[29–31]. The resulting interfacial mismatches not only impede
charge transfer but may also introduce new kinetic bottlenecks,
limiting the overall catalytic efficiency for sulfur conversion
reactions [32–34]. From an electronic structure perspective, acti-
vating Li2S requires weakening the hybridization between Li 2s
and S 3p orbitals to dissociate the Li─S bond [35–38]. However,
achieving such modulation at the solid-solid interface remains
challenging, as conventional heterogeneous interfaces lack the
chemical interaction necessary for effective orbital overlap and
electronic coupling.
Herein, we propose a conceptually distinct approach based on
in situ growth of a Li+-conductive mediator on Li2S to create
a chemically bonded, electronically coupled interface. Guided
by density functional theory calculations, we identified that
the competitive coordination between tetrahedral cations and
lithium for interaction with lattice sulfur establishes a negative
correlation between M─S and Li─S bond strengths, with Li4SnS4
exhibiting the most pronounced effect. The unsaturated p-orbitals
of Sn interact with the S 3p orbitals in Li2S to form interfacial
Sn─S bonds, which drive a local electron density reconstruc-
tion at lattice sulfur sites, thereby weakening the Li─S bond
and lowering the activation energy barrier for Li2S oxidation.
Consequently, the designed Li2S cathode exhibits a substantially
enhanced ionic conductivity (from 2.25 to 3.99 × 10−5), reduced
initial oxidation potential (from 2.94 to 2.41 V), and achieves a
high Li2S utilization of 65.2% under high-loading conditions (50
wt.% Li2S, 5 mg cm−2), with stable cycling over 100 cycles. The
practical feasibility is further demonstrated by a prototype pouch
cell, which reliably powers a blue light-emitting diode (LED) even
after 100 cycles at 0.1 C, underscoring its promise for real-world
applications. This work establishes a new paradigm for activating
Li2S through interfacial electronic structure engineering toward
high-energy-density ASSLSBs.

Results and Discussion
2.1
Electronic Structure Modulation of Li2S by
Molecular Mediators
Unveiling the interplay between lattice sulfur and lithium atoms
lies at the heart of activating Li2S cathodes, as it dictates the bond
strength and the energy barrier for Li+ extraction. In Li2S crystals,
the significant electronegativity difference between the lattice
sulfur and lithium drives the nearly complete electron transfer
from the Li 2s orbital to the S 3p orbital, forming a highly ionic
Li─S bond (Figure 1a). The orbital hybridization between the
well-matched Li 2s and S 3p orbitals causes significant electron
localization around S2–, creating a deep electrostatic potential
well that exerts strong Coulombic confinement on Li+, thereby
rendering the Li─S bond difficult to dissociate (Figure 1b).
This orbital hybridization is inherently sensitive to the local
coordination environment. Therefore, modulating the electronic
structure of lattice sulfur can alter its interaction with the Li+,
thereby weakening the orbital coupling of the Li─S bond.
Based on this understanding, we selected five representa-
tive main-group sulfide electrolytes (Li3PS4, Li4GeS4, Li4SnS4,
Li3SbS4, and Li4SiS4) as molecular mediators to modulate the
electronic structure of lattice sulfur (Figure S1). These mediators
offer good chemical compatibility with Li2S, decent ionic conduc-
tivity, and, importantly, can be obtained through in situ reactions
with Li2S, facilitating the construction of well-defined interface
models for theoretical investigation. To this end, systematic
density functional theory (DFT) calculations were conducted to
investigate the local coordination environment of lattice sulfur.
Interestingly, lattice sulfur interacts with the tetrahedral cations
(P5+, Ge4+, Sn4+, Sb5+, and Si4+) of the molecular mediators, with
orbital overlap between the Li s orbitals and the S p orbitals
(Figure S2). This indicates coordination competition between
the tetrahedral cations and lithium for overlap with the S p
orbital. Varying the tetrahedral cations results in different M─S
(where M═P, Ge, Sn, Sb, or Si) bond couplings and corresponding
variations in electron transfer at the lattice sulfur sites, which
in turn influences the Li─S bond strength. Consequently, the
competitive coexistence of M─S and Li─S bonds results in a
negative correlation between the strengths of the M─S and Li─S
bonds (Figure S3). Among the five candidates, the Li2S@Li4SnS4
sample exhibited the shortest M─S bond length, accompanied by
weak interaction between lattice sulfur and Li+, reflected by the
longest Li─S bond length of 2.94 Å.
This coordination competition was further corroborated by
projected crystal orbital Hamiltonian population (pCOHP) anal-
ysis and the integrated crystal orbital Hamiltonian population
(iCOHP). The iCOHP values for the M─S bonds followed a
volcano-type trend from P to Sb, while those for the Li─S
bonds exhibited an inverted volcano trend (Figure 1c and
Figure S4). Notably, Li2S@Li4SnS4 reached the extreme of this
trend, displaying the strongest M─S bonds alongside the weakest
Li─S bonds. Specifically, the antibonding states of the Sn─S bond
shift above the Fermi level with reduced occupation, indicating
stronger Sn-S bonding compared to the other M─S bonds. In
contrast, the antibonding states of the Li─S bond in Li2S@Li4SnS4
shift below the Fermi level and become occupied, yielding a
weakened Li─S bond. This extreme behavior originates from the
unique electronic configuration of Sn4+, specifically its empty
5p orbitals that are energetically well-matched with the fully
occupied 3p orbitals of lattice sulfur. This alignment facilitates
strong p-p orbital interactions between Sn and lattice sulfur,
maximizing orbital overlap and promoting substantial electron
transfer from S 3p to Sn 5p states. Consequently, electron density
is effectively withdrawn from the lattice sulfur sites, weakening
the electron cloud localization around S2– and reducing the
Coulombic trap on Li+. This redistribution of electron density

2.2
Synthesis and Characterization of
Li2S@Li4SnS4
Guided by these computational insights, we selected Li4SnS4
as the molecular mediator to activate the Li2S cathode, owing
to its most pronounced competitive coordination effect with
lattice sulfur. To establish a favorable electrochemical interface,
we employed SnS2 as a precursor for in situ reaction with Li2S
(Figure 2a). The negative formation energy (–27.1 kJ mol−1)
indicates that the reaction between Li2S and SnS2 is thermo-
dynamically favorable. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns in
Figure 2b and Figure S8 show that the Li2S@Li4SnS4 sam-
ple retains the characteristic diffraction peaks of Li2S (JCPDS
No. 23-0369), while no signals corresponding to SnS2 (JCPDS
No. 98-000-0113) are detected. This observation confirms the
complete consumption of the SnS2 precursor without altering
the crystal structure of Li2S. Furthermore, Rietveld refinement
(Figure 2c) verifies the presence of Li4SnS4 (JCPDS No. 97-029-
0832), confirming the successful chemical reaction between Li2S
and SnS2.

ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements. As shown
in Figure S9, the peaks at 160.7 eV in the S 2p XPS spectra
and at 485.2 eV in the Sn 3d XPS spectra are assigned to the
Sn─S bond of SnS2 [39–41] For Li2S@Li4SnS4, the S 2p peaks

2.3
Kinetic Analysis of Cathodes with Li4SnS4
Mediator
To investigate the effect of the Li4SnS4 mediator on the kinetics
of sulfur conversion, the composite cathode was prepared by
mixing the ionic conductor (Li5.5PS4.5Cl0.8Br0.7, 7.8 mS cm−1,
Figure S10), electronic conductor (vapor-grown carbon fiber,
VGCF), and active materials (Li2S or Li2S@Li4SnS4), followed
by electrochemical measurements. Both the ionic and electronic
conductivity were measured by direct current (DC) polarization
(Figure S11). As shown in Figure 3a, the Li+ conductivity of
the Li2S@Li4SnS4 cathode was 3.99 × 10−5 S cm−1, nearly twice
that of the Li2S cathode (2.25 × 10−5 S cm−1). In addition, the
electronic conductivity of the Li2S@Li4SnS4 cathode (3.6 × 10−1
S cm−1) is higher than that of the Li2S cathode (1.5 × 10−1
S cm−1, Figure S12). These results indicates that the Li4SnS4
mediator not only effectively enhances Li+ conduction but also
concurrently improves the electron transport capability of the
material, leading to a significant improvement in the overall
conductivity of cathode.
The kinetics of the sulfur redox reaction at the cathode were
evaluated using cyclic voltammetry (CV) at different scan rates.
Compared to the Li2S cathode (Figure 3b and Figure S13a), the
Li2S@Li4SnS4 cathode (Figure 3c and Figure S13b) exhibits con-
sistently higher peak current and larger peak area across all scan
rates. More quantitatively, we analyzed the linear relationship
between the peak current and the square root of the scan rate
[44, 45]. As shown in Figure 3d and Figure S14, the cathodic and
anodic reaction slopes for the Li2S@Li4SnS4 cathode were 47.6
and 54.7, respectively, markedly higher than those of the Li2S
cathode (26.9 and 18.86), reflecting significantly enhanced elec-
trode reaction kinetics. Tafel curves derived from the reduction
and oxidation peaks of the CV curves further corroborate this
enhancement (Figure 3e). The Li2S@Li4SnS4 cathode exhibits
lower Tafel slopes than the Li2S cathode in both the oxidation
process (374 vs. 459 mV dec−1) and the reduction process (496 vs.
645 mV dec−1). Correspondingly, the relative activation energy
of the oxidation process, estimated from the Tafel slope [46, 47],
was reduced by 10.46 kJ mol−1 for the Li2S@Li4SnS4 cathode
(Figure 3f). These kinetic analyses collectively confirm that the
Li4SnS4 mediator significantly accelerates the redox kinetics
of the Li2S cathode, consistent with the interfacial electronic
modulation that softens the Li─S bond and enhances charge
transport.
The influence of the Li4SnS4 mediator on the kinetic behavior
of the Li2S cathode was further investigated by operando elec-
trochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) coupled with distribu-
tion of relaxation times (DRT) analysis during the initial charging
process. In the DRT analysis, the high-frequency response is
attributed to Li+ transport through the solid electrolyte and solid-
solid contact resistance, whereas the intermediate-frequency
response reflects interfacial Li+ transport. The dominant peak
at approximately 0.1 s is assigned to the charge-transfer process
associated with Li2S oxidation. (Figure S15 and Table S1). The
results reveal a negative correlation between the charge-transfer
impedance and the state of charge (SoC). At the early stage of
charging, the impedance reaches its maximum, corresponding
to the activation of Li2S. As charging proceeds and Li2S is
continuously consumed, the charge transfer impedance grad-
ually decreases. Crucially, with the assistance of the Li4SnS4
mediator, the Li2S@Li4SnS4 cathode exhibits a consistently low
charge transfer impedance throughout the charging process
(Figure 3g–i). These findings demonstrate that the Li4SnS4 medi-
ator plays a positive role in activating the Li2S cathode, effectively
enhancing charge transfer kinetics and accelerating the redox
kinetics of the Li2S cathode.

electrode/electrolyte interface and structural mismatch between
the cathode and the SSE in the solid-solid configuration of
ASSLSBs hinder orbital alignment and spatial overlap, thereby
suppressing the coupling between electronic states and limiting
interfacial charge transfer kinetics. However, with the assistance
of the Li4SnS4 mediator, this solid-solid interfacial barrier is
effectively mitigated. On one hand, Li4SnS4 engages in strong
interactions with the lattice sulfur of Li2S through its unique
Sn─S covalent bonds, inducing electron cloud reconstruction at
the interface and enabling more favorable orbital hybridization
and charge redistribution. On the other hand, its mixed ionic-
electronic conductivity establishes continuous charge transport
pathways, effectively bridging the interfacial contact losses
caused by rigid structural mismatch. As a result, the solid-
solid catalytic process, originally constrained by insufficient
orbital overlap and high interfacial impedance, is significantly
activated, providing a favorable electronic structure environment
and kinetic pathway for efficient decomposition and conversion
of Li2S.

3
Conclusions
In summary, we developed a molecular mediator strategy to
overcome the intrinsic kinetic limitations of Li2S cathodes in
ASSLSBs. Through the in situ growth of Li4SnS4 on Li2S, an
atomically bonded interface is constructed, in which Sn─S
bonding induces local electron-density redistribution around
sulfur sites, weakens Li-S interactions, and lowers the activation
barrier for Li2S oxidation. This interfacial electronic modulation
enhances cathode ionic conductivity and markedly reduces the
initial charge potential from 2.94 to 2.41 V. Consequently, the
Li2S@Li4SnS4 cathode achieves a high Li2S utilization of 77%
and stable cycling over 500 cycles with a low capacity-fading
rate of 0.02% per cycle. Even under stringent conditions with
a high Li2S content of 50 wt.% and an areal loading of 5 mg
cm−2, the cathode maintains stable operation for over 100 cycles.
Its practical feasibility is further demonstrated by a prototype
pouch cell that continues to power an LED after 100 cycles. This
work highlights interfacial electronic-structure engineering as an
effective and generalizable strategy for overcoming the kinetic
barriers of conversion-type cathodes toward high-energy-density
all-solid-state batteries.
