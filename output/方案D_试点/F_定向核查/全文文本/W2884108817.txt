As the theoretical limit of intercalation material-based lithium-ion batteries 
is approached, alternative chemistries based on conversion reactions are 
presently considered. The conversion of sulfur is particularly appealing as it is 
associated with a theoretical gravimetric energy density up to 2510 Wh kg−1. In 
this paper, three different carbon-iron disulfide-sulfur (C-FeS2-S) composites are 
proposed as alternative positive electrode materials for all-solid-state lithium-
sulfur batteries. These are synthesized through a facile, low-cost, single-step 
ball-milling procedure. It is found that the crystalline structure (evaluated by 
X-ray diffraction) and the morphology of the composites (evaluated by  
scanning electron microscopy) are greatly influenced by the FeS2:S ratio.  
Li/LiI-Li3PS4/C-FeS2-S solid-state cells are tested under galvanostatic conditions, 
while differential capacity plots are used to discuss the peculiar electrochemical 
features of these novel materials. These cells deliver capacities as high as 
1200 mAh g(FeS2+S)−1 at the intermediate loading of 1 mg cm−2 (1.2 mAh cm−2), 
and up to 3.55 mAh cm−2 for active material loadings as high as 5 mg cm−2 at 
20 °C. Such an excellent performance, rarely reported for (sulfur/metal sulfide)-
based, all solid-state cells, makes these composites highly promising for real 
application where high positive electrode loadings are required.

1. Introduction
Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) are the current technology of 
choice for powering portable electronics, power tools and, even, 
electric vehicles.[1] Thanks to the joint research efforts from 
both academia and industry, LIBs are on a good path to fully 
utilize the theoretical gravimetric energy densities of current 
generation insertion cathodes (i.e., about 650–750 Wh kg−1).[2] 
This is considered by car makers, however, as the minimum 
critical target for positive electrode materials[2] in order to 
ensure the widespread distribution of zero-emission[3] full

benefit from increased electronic conductivity, lower polysulfide 
dissolution (due to trapping effects), moderate volume 
expansion/contraction, and a more stable electrode/electrolyte 
interface, while the additional compounds may also participate 
in the redox reaction as active materials.[7,17,18] Electrolyte addi-
tives,[19] polysulfide-saturated,[20] or solvent-in-salt[21] solutions 
have also shown promising results in reducing anode corro-
sion and improving the reaction kinetics, while reducing the 
polysulfide dissolution. Polymeric[22] and gel electrolytes,[23,24] 
also in hybrid systems using ceramic interlayers,[25,26] have 
been proposed to cope with the abovementioned issues. In this 
regard, the realization of solid-state devices,[27,28] more specifi-
cally all-solid-state batteries employing sulfide-based electrolytes, 
is characterized by several advantages.[29–31] Apart from the 
typical benefits of sulfide-based electrolytes, which are higher 
safety and formation of a stable interphase at the lithium metal 
electrode,[29] all-solid-state cells do not incur in the polysulfide 
dissolution and, consequently, shuttling, thus ensuring high 
performance over hundreds of cycles.[31–33] In fact, the (dis-)/
charge mechanism of all-solid-state lithium sulfur batteries is 
radically different from that observed in the conventional, liquid 
electrolyte-based ones. The voltage profiles are generally charac-
terized by a single pseudo-plateau, corresponding to the direct 
conversion of sulfur to form lithium disulfide.[34,35] Still, given 
the generally low electron conductivity and the large volume 
expansion associated with the conversion reaction, composite 
materials are required to achieve good performance and long 
cycle life. Carbon matrixes[36] and, more recently, transition 
metal sulfides[18] (which are known to have a positive effect on 
liquid electrolyte cells[17]), have been reported to promote the 
conversion reaction occurring in all-solid-state cells.[37] Further-
more, these can also favor the reversible oxidation/reduction 
of the solid electrolyte (as in the case of sulfide glasses), which 
is added into the composite cathode to ensure adequate ionic 
conductivity, especially in the presence of the finely dispersed 
carbon, added to ensure a good electronic conductivity.[31,38]
Transition metal sulfides are electrochemically active in a 
similar voltage range of sulfur.[17,18] The conventional redox 
reactions between transition metal sulfides and lithium (MS2 or 
MS, with M = Fe, Mo, Co, Ti, etc.) may involve insertion and/or 
conversion reactions depending on the transition metal and the 
sulfide crystalline structure.[18] Their use in the battery field is by 
no mean a novelty.[39,40] However, thanks to the recent advances 
in material science and nanoengineering,[18] as well as in the 
understanding of the issues associated with sulfur/polysulfide 
conversion, these materials are nowadays being revisited as 
viable alternative cathodes. It is now known that, when used in 
conventional liquid electrolyte cells, transition metal sulfides can 
show anchoring/trapping effects on lithium polysulfides,[17] thus 
effectively supporting the sulfur conversion to lithium disulfide. 
FeS2, in particular, has been proposed as positive electrode mate-
rial in lithium cells[41–47] and as capacity-contributing additive 
for ether-based Li-S cells.[48,49] Regarding this latter application, 
however, FeS2 is known to increase the capacity fading of the 
composite positive electrode,[48] possibly due to an increased dis-
solution of iron, which contributes to shuttling and FeS deposi-
tion at the negative electrode.[48] An all-solid-state configuration 
implies the absence of soluble polysulfides and the possibility to 
have a direct sulfur conversion.[34,35] This is expected to have a 
marked beneficial effect on the electrochemical behavior of the 
material. However, the effect of metal sulfides in general, and 
of iron sulfide in particular, is not fully understood nor widely 
studied in all-solid-state lithium batteries, and thus prompts fur-
ther attention.
In this paper, we focus on the synergy between sulfur and iron 
disulfide by studying three different carbon-iron disulfide-sulfur 
(C-FeS2-S)-based composites characterized by increasing sulfur 
contents, namely, C:FeS2 40:60 (wt%, referred as sample CF), 
C:FeS2:sulfur 40:50:10 (wt%, referred as sample CFS(8:2)), and 
C:FeS2:sulfur 40:30:30 (wt%, referred as sample CFS(5:5)). The 
composites are synthesized by a facile and relatively inexpensive, 
single-step ball-milling procedure. It is found that the crystalline 
structure (evaluated by X-ray diffraction (XRD)) and morphology 
(evaluated by scanning electron microscopy (SEM)) of the com-
ponents are greatly influenced by their ratio, in particular by 
the FeS2:S ratio. As the electrolyte for the all-solid-state cells, a 
LiI-added sulfide-based electrolyte is used, namely, LiI-Li3PS4.[31] 
This electrolyte benefits from improved ionic conductivity 
(about 10−3 S cm−1) at room temperature. Moreover, it is often 
associated with the formation of a stable interface with lithium 
metal, which is a critical issue for many inorganic lithium-ion 
conductors.[50] The use of a lithium metal negative electrode for 
all-solid-state batteries is rarely reported in the literature, whereas 
other negative electrodes, such as Li-In, are mostly employed to 
avoid the formation of unwanted interphases and suppress den-
drite formation.[51–53] The use of an alloy as the counter electrode, 
however, implies a lower energy density and a different electro-
chemical behavior of the electrochemical cell.[51,53] By employing 
lithium metal, the differential capacity plots derived from gal-
vanostatic voltage profiles can be used to discuss the peculiar 
electrochemical features of the cathode materials. At 20 °C, the 
Li-CFS cells deliver a capacity as high as 1200 mAh g(FeS2+S)−1 
at the intermediate loading of 1 mg cm−2 (i.e., 1.20 mAh cm−2). 
Up to 3.55 mAh cm−2 are achieved using active material load-
ings as high as 5 mg cm−2, which is one of the targets for Li/S 
battery commercialization.[7] To the best of our knowledge, this 
is the first report of an all-solid-state Li/(metal sulfide-S) cell 
employing a carbon-transition metal sulfide-sulfur composite 
electrode capable of cycling at such high loading using lithium 
metal as the anode. As such, this chemistry represents a prom-
ising alternative for the realization of the next-generation high 
energy density conversion-based batteries.
2. Results and Discussion
The crystalline structure of the composites was characterized 
by X-ray diffraction (see Figure 1). The diffraction patterns of 
the composites after the first ball-milling procedure (Figure 1a) 
reveal that the sulfur defective phase Fe(1−x)S (pyrrhotite,

crystalline FeS2 (pyrite, ICDD PDF 01-071-0053, cubic, 
Pa-3(205) space group) are present when no elemental sulfur 
is added (CF sample). The formation of the defective phase 
appears to be partially hindered when 10% of sulfur is added, 
as evidenced by the diffraction pattern of the CFS(8:2) sample. 
When the sulfur content was increased, however, the formation 
of pyrrhotite was suppressed. In fact, only crystalline FeS2 is

The morphology of selected materials has been studied by 
scanning electron microscopy and is reported in Figure 2. For 
FeS2 (Figure  2a) large aggregates of submicrometric particles 
are observed. Activated carbon (Figure 2b) is also characterized 
by even larger aggregates of particles with a rather broad size 
distribution. When the two materials are ball-milled together 
(CF sample, Figure  2c) the activated carbon seems to mostly 
retain its original morphology, while the iron sulfide aggre-
gates are broken and the resulting submicrometric particles 
are homogenously dispersed on the surface of the activated 
carbon. No apparent material segregation is observed, as fur-
ther evidenced by Figure  S3 (Supporting Information) where 
the energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) analysis for the CF sample 
is reported. When sulfur is included in the ball-milled mixture, 
an evident particle size reduction can be observed in the SEM 
image of the sample with the highest sulfur content (CFS(5:5), 
Figure  2d). It is known that sulfur can effectively trigger 
mechanically induced self-propagating reactions (MSR)[54,55] 
and, given the high temperatures reached during a mechano-
chemical synthesis, could melt or sublimate/evaporate.[54] In 
this way, it can act as wetting agent and hinder the material 
reaggregation thus reducing the particle size.[56] The second 
ball-milling step, which includes the solid electrolyte, yields 
materials with a more homogenous morphology (Figure 2e,f). 
In particular, the sample with the highest sulfur content 
(CFS(5:5)-LPSI, Figure 2f) is characterized by a rather regular, 
spherical morphology of the aggregates. The morphology of 
sample CFS(8:2) and CFS(8:2)-LPSI is comparable to that 
of samples CFS(5:5) and CFS(5:5)-LPSI (images reported in 
Figure S4, Supporting Information).

3. Conclusion
Herein, we reported a novel cathode composite based on sulfur 
and iron disulfide for high-performance lithium-sulfur batteries 
at high loading. The composite cathode material is synthesized 
by a simple, one-step, relatively cheap mechanochemical pro-
cedure. Its crystalline structure and morphology are favorably 
affected by the presence of sulfur, which is the key for stabilizing 
the pyrite phase during the synthesis procedure by suppressing 
the formation of the defective pyrrhotite phase. The material 
reveals a complex, yet novel, (dis-)/charge behavior. The cells, 
which benefit from the use of a lithium metal anode, can deliver 
a capacity as high as 1200 mAh g−1 over 20 cycles at the loading 
of 1 mg cm−2. The results suggest that more stable capacities 
are achieved when the pyrrhotite formation is suppressed, sug-
gesting that having iron in the fully oxidized state might cause 
the reversibility of the electrochemical process. This assumption, 
however, still requires further validation. Using active material 
loadings as high as 5 mg cm−2 at 25 °C, that is one of the tar-
gets for Li/S batteries commercialization, the cells delivered up 
to 3.55 mAh cm−2. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first 
report of an all-solid-state Li/(metal sulfide-S) cell employing a 
carbon-sulfur-transition metal sulfide material which can cycle 
at such high loading using lithium metal as the anode and, as 
such, is proposed as a promising alternative for the realization 
of next-generation high energy density batteries.

For cell assembly, an in-house, two-electrode (Ø = 13 mm) cell setup was 
employed.[68,69] In detail, a cylindrical plastic case with an internal diameter 
of 13 mm was used as die-set for pellet preparation. About 300 mg of LPSI 
were introduced inside the die-set and pressed by hydraulic press (YLJ-24, 
MTI corp.) at 2 MPa to form a prepellet. A lithium metal disk (thickness = 
30 µm, Ø1.2 cm. Honjo Metal, Osaka) was applied as anode on one side 
of the electrolyte prepellet. Afterward, a selected amount of the composite 
cathode material was spread over the other side of the electrolyte prepellet. 
The preformed cell was finally pressed at 10 MPa for 30 s, at 20 °C, using the 
hydraulic press. The cylindrical plastic case was then placed inside a metal 
case equipped with a screw applying force on the upper current collector 
and, therefore, ensuring the mechanical stability of the pelletized cell, as 
well as helping keep an intimate contact between the materials. This screw 
was fastened by applying a torque of 3 N m by means of a precision torque 
wrench. The active material loading, considered as the total amount of S+FeS2 
for the specific capacity calculation, ranged between 1.0 and 5.0 mg cm−2. 
To carry out the electrochemical tests, the cells were additionally sealed 
in aluminum bags filled with Ar to avoid air contamination. Galvanostatic 
cycling tests were performed in the 1.3–3.1 V voltage range, using a either 
a Maccor 4000 Battery Test System or a VMP3 Potentiostat/Galvanostat/
EIS (Bio-Logic). The impedance measurements were performed within 
the frequency range from 1 MHz to100 mHz applying a 10 mV sinusoidal 
amplitude using a VMP3 Potentiostat/Galvanostat/EIS (Bio-Logic). For all 
electrochemical tests, cells were placed in thermostated climatic chambers 
set at 20  °C (1–2 mg cm−2) or 25  °C (4–5 mg cm−2), with a maximum 
deviation of ±1 °C. The cells were let to rest at open-circuit voltage (OCV) 
condition for 24 h prior to each test. The morphology of all materials was 
performed by using a field emission SEM (Zeiss LEO1550VP Gemini) 
equipped with an EDX spectrometer (Oxford Instruments X-MaxN, 50 mm2, 
15 kV). The structural characterization was performed by XRD (Bruker D8 
Advance diffractometer equipped with a CuKα source, λ = 0.154 nm). An 
air-tight, Ar-filled sample holder was used to transfer the samples from the 
glove box to the SEM chamber. For the XRD measurements, Kapton tape 
and air-tight sample holders were employed to avoid sample degradation. 
TGA was performed under N2 stream in a temperature range of 30–600 °C, 
with a constant heating rate of 10  °C min−1 by using a Discovery TGA 
(TA Instruments). To prevent contamination the samples were sealed in 
aluminum air-tight pans inside the glove box.
