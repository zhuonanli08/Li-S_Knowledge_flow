Assessing the practical feasibility of solid-
state lithium–sulfur batteries
Check for updates
Ganyu Wang1, Xiaoyu Luo1, Wenlong Sun2 & Jingsheng Cai
3
Compared to current lithium-ion batteries, solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries (SSLSBs) promise
signiﬁcantly enhanced energy density and improved safety, rendering them attractive for applications
such as electric vehicles, unmanned aerial vehicles, and wearable devices. This review transcends the
conventional material-centric perspective, analyzing the application feasibility of SSLSBs while
particularly emphasizing the challenges and corresponding solution strategies in their potential
applications. Key obstacles include achieving high power and rapid charging, enhancing cycle life,
and maximizing practical energy density at the device level. Technological breakthroughs encompass
novel materials, structural design, and interface engineering, with particular emphasis on the
importance of considering inactive components in pouch cell when evaluating energy density. This
review also discusses the patent landscape and industrialization progress of SSLSBs, highlighting
advanced technologies like artiﬁcial intelligence as a crucial driving force for accelerating the
development of SSLSBs and industrialization, and provides strategic research recommendations to
facilitate the practical applications of this promising energy storage technology.
Amidst the global transition towards cleaner and low-carbon energy
structures, high-energy-density energy storage technologies have emerged
as pivotal drivers for the advancement of electric vehicles, autonomous
devices and intelligent devices1–3. Lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries are
increasingly recognized as one of the most promising post-lithium battery
systems due to their remarkable advantages, particularly in terms of energy
density4–10, cost-effectiveness11–14, and environmental friendliness15–17. The
theoretical speciﬁc energy density of Li−S batteries is approximately

Moreover, as an abundant and inexpensive material, sulfur enhances the
cost-effectiveness of Li−S batteries, rendering them a viable candidate for
large-scale applications. However, the commercialization of conventional
liquid Li−S batteries has long been hindered by critical challenges such as
the shuttle effect of lithium polysulﬁdes (LiPSs) and the growth of lithium
dendrites19–21. The dissolution of LiPSs in liquid electrolytes leads to irre-
versible loss of active materials, aggravated self-discharge, and reduced
Coulombic efﬁciency22,23, while the risk of short circuits caused by lithium
dendrite penetration through the separator poses a signiﬁcant threat to
battery safety24–27. To address these issues, researchers have introduced
solid-state electrolytes (SSEs) into the Li−S system two decades ago28–30,
forming solid-state lithium−sulfur batteries (SSLSBs). By replacing liquid
electrolytes with SSEs, SSLSBs theoretically achieve the following break-
throughs: (1) suppression of LiPSs diffusion and the shuttle, thereby redu-
cing capacity loss and the instable cycling performance31,32; (2) mechanical
blocking of lithium dendrites33–35 and reduction of thermal runaway risk36,
enhancing safety. These characteristics make SSLSBs uniquely valuable in
applications such as aerospace, deep-sea exploration, long-range electric
vehicles, autonomous robotics, and wearable devices (Fig. 1).
SSLSBs are primarily classiﬁed based on the types of SSEs. According
to material properties, they can be categorized into polymer electrolytes,
inorganic electrolytes, and composite electrolytes. Among these, polymer
SSEs, exempliﬁed by the typical polyethylene oxide (PEO)37,38 are char-
acterized by low production costs39, ease of fabrication40,41, and favorable
interfacial compatibility42,43 due to the inherent ﬂexibility of polymers.
However, its Li+ conductivity at room temperature remains relatively low
(typically <10−6 S cm−1), necessitating operation at elevated temperatures
(typically >60 °C) to achieve sufﬁcient ionic conductivity44,45. Inorganic
SSEs46,47 are dominated by oxide-based and sulﬁde-based SSEs, exhibiting
superior ionic conductivity and mechanical strength compared to poly-
mers. Furthermore, they demonstrate enhanced effectiveness in directly
suppressing the dissolution of LiPSs to alleviate the shuttle48. Repre-
sentative oxide SSEs49,50 include garnet-type Li7La3Zr2O12 (LLZO),

Despite signiﬁcant advancements in laboratory-scale research on
SSLSBs, the transition from fundamental research to practical application
remains fraught with substantial challenges. From an industrialization
perspective, the practical implementation of SSLSBs can be classiﬁed into
three primary domains: the standardization and compatibility of perfor-
mance across diverse materials, the performance discrepancy between
material and device properties, and the economic barriers encountered in
applications. Firstly, at the material level, the issue of solid–solid interfacial
contact impedance60,61 between the SSE and the electrode is particularly
pronounced. In contrast to liquid electrolytes that can effectively wet the
electrode surface, insufﬁciently effective ion transport channels caused by
voids at the rigid interfaceof SSLSBs signiﬁcantlyimpair lithiumdeposition,

effectively translating the high speciﬁc capacity at the material level to the

stems from insufﬁcient attention in research publications toward the opti-
mization of other inactive components63,64. Additionally, the sensitivity of
sulﬁde SSEs to moisture and oxygen necessitates that full cell assembly must
be conducted under inert atmospheres, signiﬁcantly increasing equipment
investment and production costs65–67. Although the raw material cost of
sulfur cathodes is low, the additional costs associated with high-
performance SSEs and lithium metal anode processing techniques may
offset the material advantages. It is noteworthy that the technological
maturity of SSLSBs must be discussed within the broader competitive
landscapeofenergystoragetechnologiessuchasoxide-basedLiCoO2/LLZO
all-solid-state batteries. Furthermore, recent advancements in liquid Li−S
batteries68, such as electrolyte modiﬁcations (e.g., high-concentration
electrolytes69,70, ﬂuorinated solvents71) and separator functionalization72,73,
have extended their cycle life beyond 800 cycles, imposing higher demands
on the competitive differentiation of SSLSBs. Consequently, the practical
application pathway for SSLSBs necessitates a clear delineation of their
technological positioning: whether they are intended as a replacement for
liquid Li−Sbatteriesorasasolutiontargetinghigh-endapplicationsbeyond
the reach of existing technologies.

In this review, we systematically analyzes the critical challenges and
potential solutions encountered in the practical application of SSLSBs,
including future industrialization prospects, with a particular focus on
elucidating the applicability value of SSLSBs. In contrast to previous review
articles that primarily focus on materials and components, this review is
structured around the following questions and outlooks: (1) What are the
challengesthatSSLSBscurrentlyneedstofaceinpotentialfutureapplication
scenarios? (2) What are the necessary technological advancements required
forthepracticalimplementationofSSLSBs?(3)Whatistheindustriallayout
of SSLSBs and what engineering technologies can accelerate the indus-
trialization of SSLSBs? By integrating multiple perspectives from academic
researchers and enterprise engineers, this review aims to provide valuable
insights into the development directions of SSLSBs for both academic and
industrial communities.
Potential practical applications of SSLSBs
Currently, there is an increasing demand for battery applications across
various ﬁelds, particularly in electric vehicles, unmanned aerial and
underwatervehicles, intelligent wearable devices, and autonomous robotics,
which require wireless endurance capabilities74,75. Their electrical energy
supply system places signiﬁcant emphasis on high energy density and safety
considerations. In the ﬁeld of wireless autonomous devices, high-energy
densitybatteriesareof paramountimportance76,77.Sincethetotalweightofa
wireless autonomous device must account for the battery’s own weight, the
approach of increasing battery packs to extend range encounters inherent
limitations78, particularly for high-performance wireless autonomous
devices requiring lightweight design. In ﬁelds involving human interaction
and contact, battery safety has garnered signiﬁcant attention79–82. SSLSBs,
characterized by their high energy density and enhanced safety features,
precisely meet the stringent requirements for energy supply devices in these
applications. Notably, SSLSBs systems eliminate the need for envir-
onmentally hazardous metallic elements while offering cost-effectiveness
and improved electrode stability.
Electric vehicles
The future development of electric vehicles is anticipated to achieve a
driving range of 1500 km, which necessitates battery energy density83
exceeding 500 Wh kg−1. This enhancement is critical for extending electric
vehiclerangewithoutincreasingbatterypacksizeorweight.Regardingcycle
life,Electricvehiclesare expectedto maintaineffectiveoperationfor10to15
years84. Based on a charging frequency of twice per week, the battery must
achieve a minimum cycle life of 1500 cycles. Automotive applications
necessitate consideration of extreme environmental conditions85. Liquid
electrolytes encounter signiﬁcant challenges at 0 °C, including increased
viscosity and crystallization, which result in substantial capacity loss and
potential battery damage during charging86. At elevated temperatures
exceeding 60 °C, liquid electrolyte decomposition may occur87. The SSE
employed in SSLSB demonstrates superior performance, maintaining sta-
bility across an operational temperature range of −30 °C to 100 °C88. Safety
concerns in electric vehicles primarily involve thermal runaway risks89.
SSLSBs demonstrate particular advantages in safety, effectively preventing
combustion risks90, thereby signiﬁcantly enhancing passenger safety. From
an environmental perspective, the widespread adoption of electric vehicles
in the future will impose signiﬁcant pressure on battery manufacturing and
recycling processes. The SSLSB electrodes eliminate the need for envir-
onmentally hazardous heavy metal elements such as cobalt, nickel, and
manganese.Theutilizationofsulfurasanalternativecathodeactivematerial
can reduce environmental impact by up to 90%17,91.
Unmanned aerial vehicles
Unmanned aerial vehicles, particularly small-scale electric drones, impose
stringent weight constraints, necessitating energy supply systems capable of
delivering sufﬁcient electrical energy and power output to ensure efﬁcient
ﬂight operations92. This demands batteries with both high energy density
and substantial current discharge capabilities. Currently, drones have been
extensively employed to replace human operators in executing tasks within
complex environments, particularly for inspecting hazardous electrical
facilitiessuchaspowertransmissionlinesandsubstations93–96.However,the
ﬂight duration of drones is typically limited to approximately 30 minutes97,
which signiﬁcantly constrains their operational range and mission execu-
tion time. High-energy-density batteries can signiﬁcantly enhance both
ﬂight time and range, positioning drones as a potential application scenario
for SSLSBs. Notably, China’s government-supported low-altitude economy
policy98 has facilitated the emergenceof unmanned aerial vehicles. Recently,
NIO Inc. has developed the innovative electric vertical take-off and landing
vehicle ET Flyzer, designed for urban short-distance commuting. The
incorporation of manned electric aircraft necessitates the consideration of
battery safety, substantially increasing the potential applicability of SSLSBs.
Unmanned underwater vehicles
The applications of unmanned underwater vehicles span diverse ﬁelds,
encompassing marine resource exploration, ecological monitoring, and
underwater inspection99. These vehicles have become indispensable in
executing complex subaquatic operations, including seabed mapping and
pipeline inspection, which impose speciﬁc requirements on battery
endurance100. However, since underwater cruising does not impose strin-
gent requirements on speed, the demand for high battery power output is
not imperative101. This characteristic aligns well with the battery system of
SSLSBs, which exhibits high energy density but requires further improve-
ment in power density. Furthermore, given the speciﬁc operational
requirements in deep-sea environments, unmanned underwater vehicles
necessitate the integration of battery systems capable of withstanding
extreme hydrostatic pressures, with operational depth speciﬁcations span-
ning from 50 to 1000 meters or beyond102. For instance, the Blueﬁn 1.5 kWh

Autonomous robotics
In autonomous robotics, the necessity for high ﬂexibility and the capability to
perform intelligent tasks enables robots to effectively replace humans in
hazardous and unsanitary operations77,104,105. Spot, a quadrupedal robot
developed by Boston Dynamics106, employs a belly-mounted lithium-ion
battery system, which constitutes approximately 13% of the robot’s total
weight. This battery provides 564 Wh of energy, supporting data collection
and inspection tasks. Despite this, Spot’s operational duration ranges only
between 90 and 180 minutes. Considering the time required for the robot to
return for autonomous recharging107, the endurance remains insufﬁcient for
tasks that necessitate traversing complex environments. However, con-
strained by the internal space of robots, achieving a balance between
endurance and design remains challenging108. To achieve enhanced endur-
ance, distributed battery109,110 and structural battery111–113 designs have been
implementedinroboticsystems.Thesespecializedbatteriesnecessitateahigh
degreeofintegrationwithroboticdesign114,therebyincreasingthecomplexity
of robotic system development while potentially compromising battery
performance115. Consequently, SSLSBs with high energy density have
emerged as a promising candidate for energy supply, offering the potential to
reduce design complexity and enhance the endurance of robotics.
Wearable devices
Wearable devices have become an indispensable component of modern
technology. However, their reliance on conventional battery systems pre-
sents signiﬁcant challenges, including limited energy density, frequent
charging requirements, and environmental concerns arising from battery
waste116,117. Although traditional battery technology is well-established, it
fails to adequately meet the evolving demands for long-lasting wearable
devices, which ideally should operate without daily charging118,119. For

Challenges and application-oriented technological
breakthroughs
The transition of SSLSBs from laboratory to practical applications neces-
sitates a comprehensive performance evaluation across multiple dimen-
sions. The inherent advantage of SSLSBs in energy density requires further
validation through pouch cell testing. Due to the increasing application of
battery integration with the chassis in electric vehicles, the limited design
space necessitates consideration of volumetric energy density. While
meeting gravimetric energy density requirements, it is crucial to ensure that
battery volume matches the compartment space. Similarly, the conﬁned
spaces in unmanned aerial vehicles and wearable devices require evaluating
whether volumetric energy density meets application demands alongside
gravimetric energy density considerations. For applications requiring high
power output, such as unmanned aerial vehicles, and rapid charging cap-
abilities, as in electric vehicles, the power density and fast-charging per-
formance of SSLSBs still require improvement. This necessitates addressing
the poor conductivity of sulfur species and ion transport limitations within
the battery. Regarding cycle life, SSLSBs face challenges associated with
volumetric expansion and non-uniform lithium metal deposition, which
compromisebatteryintegrity.Inthesubsequentdiscussion,wewillexamine
the technological breakthroughs required for SSLSB applications based on
their existing limitations, with the aim of identifying viable strategies to
overcome these challenges.

InadditiontotheinherentionicconductivityofSSEs,thedesignofthin
electrolytes can directly shorten the ionic transport distance between the
positive and negative electrodes of SSLSBs, thereby enhancing the power
density. During the preparation of traditional SSEs, reducing the thickness
of SSEs makes it increasingly difﬁcult to maintain their mechanical stability.
In particular, for inorganic electrolytes136, sulﬁde SSEsmay sufferfromweak
inter-particle bonding and direct fragmentation during direct pressing,
while oxide SSEs are more prone to cracking as independent thin ﬁlms due
to their inherent brittleness. Introducing ﬂexible polymers as supports is an
effective solution, which can provide mechanical strength and support
structures for inorganic SSEs. For example, the composite SSE ﬁlm137 pre-
pared by incorporating carboxylated nitrile butadiene rubber into LPSCl
enables continuous preparation at a thickness of <50 μm. In-situ ﬁlling of
polymerelectrolytes and lithium salts into the 78Li2S–22P2S5 thin inorganic
electrolyte not only achieves a self-supporting structure but also reduces
porosity, increasing the original thin SSE conductivity by 2.7 times.
Polymer-basedSSEscan also endow SSLSBs withthe ability to adapt to
deformation, facilitating their application in ﬂexible wearable devices120. A
ﬂexible SSLSB assembled with a crosslinked gel electrolyte based on PEO
and PAN employs a PAN ﬁber network design to simultaneously address
issues of ionic conduction, mechanical strength, and LiPSs shuttling138. This
ﬂexible SSLSB maintains a capacity retention rate of over 96% after 1000
bending cycles. Flexible matching of electrodes is also of great importance.
All-solid-state polymer electrolytes can also endow SSLSBs with ﬂexibility,
offering higher safety and stronger capability to prevent LiPSs shuttling
compared to gel-state polymer SSEs. However, some all-solid-state polymer
electrolytes exhibit low Li+ conductivity at room temperature, typically
requiring operation at high temperatures (>60 °C) to achieve ionic con-
ductivity sufﬁcient for power output139,140. This limitation consequently
restricts the application scenarios of polymer-based SSLSBs under ambient
conditions. Interestingly, there is an integrated approach that can solve the
temperature issue by integrating a photothermal conversion system into the
battery structure141. This system effectively generates heat through carrier
non-radiative recombination within silicon nanoshells, with the generated
heatsubsequentlytransferredrapidlytothebatterysystemviaacoppercore.
Through solar irradiation, the converted thermal energy enables enhanced
reaction kinetics in SSLSBs. Due to the poor thermal conductivity of poly-
mers, ionic conductivity tends to be non-uniform during temperature
variations, which readily induces inhomogeneous lithium deposition.
Incorporating two-dimensional boron nitride (BN) nanosheets into PEO-
based SSEs can synergistically enhance the ionic conductivity, mechanical
strength, and thermal transport of PEO-based SSEs, thereby improving
cycling stability under temperature ﬂuctuations139. Given that the ionic
conductivity of polymer-based SSEs is dependent on segmental motion142,
maintaining an amorphous state is particularly crucial for ensuring ionic
conductivity at and below ambient temperatures143. The incorporation of
plasticizer components144 enables the polymer electrolyte to maintain a
completely amorphous state across a wide temperature range extending to
subzero conditions, thereby facilitating rapid Li+ conduction and stable
interfacial chemistry in batteries at low temperatures.
In addition to the solid-solid transition reactions at the cathode and
SSEs, the interface also signiﬁcantly inﬂuences fast charging performance.
Indium triﬂuoride (InF3) was employed as a bifunctional initiator to facil-
itate the in-situ polymerization of 1,3-dioxolane (PDOL) electrolyte145. The
interaction between InF3 and lithium metal promotes the formation of a
hybridsolid electrolyteinterphase(SEI)enrichedwithlithium ﬂuoride(LiF)
and lithium-indium alloy. This structural modiﬁcation effectively enhances
Li+ kinetics and mitigates the impedance effects at the anode interface.
Cycling performance
To achieve long cycle life in SSLSBs, it is essential to consider factors such as
active material loss and degradation of battery components146. The primary
manifestations of battery component damage include (Fig. 4a): (1) Volume
changes in the electrode during charge-discharge cycles of solid-state Li−S
cathodes,whichcanreadilycompromisethecathodestructureanddamagethe
conductiveionnetworkthroughrepeatedexpansionandcontraction,resulting
in excessive polarization and capacity loss. (2) Degradation of SSEs, typically
occurs during charging processes. This phenomenon may lead to electrolyte
penetration and subsequent short-circuiting. Although inorganic SSEs exhibit
higher modulus, they remain susceptible to dendrite penetration issues.
In the ﬁeld of liquid Li−S batteries, self-healing strategies have been
extensively employed to mitigate the polysulﬁde dissolution and shuttle
effect in the cathodes147–151. In SSLSBs, self-healing approaches are recog-
nized as an effective strategy to mitigate the degradation of SSEs. SSE self-
healing predominantly relies on the intrinsic self-healing properties of
materials, which are primarily attributed to reversible chemical bonds and
hydrogen bonds within the material system152, typically based on polymer
matrices. A novel polymer-based quasi-solid-state electrolyte has been
successfully synthesized153, demonstrating remarkable interfacial stability
during prolonged cycling. This enhanced performance is attributed to the
incorporation of reversible S–S bonds and hydrogen bonds within the
polymer matrix (Fig. 4b). The signiﬁcantly lower activation energy of S–S
bonds compared to hydrogen bonds facilitates the rapid self-healing pro-
cess, which exhibits a high self-healing efﬁciency of approximately 95.3%
within an exceptionally short recovery time of 5 min (Fig. 4c). In cathodes
based on sulﬁde SSEs, carbon materials can enhance electron transport but
accelerate the oxidative decomposition of sulﬁde SSEs. Introducing non-
conductive polar host mesoporous matrices (such as SiO2) can suppress
electrolyte decomposition via non-conductivity154. Meanwhile, the strong
interaction between the high-polarity surface and S/Li2S avoids sulfur
detachment, maintaining stable conductive pathways. In this work, the
electron conductivity issue is addressed by constructing three-dimensional
conductive networks with different carbon materials. Additionally, intro-
ducing hybrid ion/electron conductive transition metal sulﬁde VS2 into the
cathode to form a core-shell structured cathode architecture with sulfur
coated on VS2 nanosheets can mitigate degradation of the SSE155. Layered
VS2 acts not only as an electron conductor but also provides Li+ transport
channels. This structure reduces the surface contact between electron-
conductive components and the SSE in the cathode, thereby suppressing
SSE decomposition during charging and enabling stable cycling perfor-
mance with high sulfur utilization.

Artiﬁcial intelligence
In the research and development of AI models, the selection of datasets and
models constitutes a crucial core, determining the application scope and
accuracy of the models. Although several open materials science databases
have been available for researchers to reference183–187, inconsistent standards
among these databases have led many researchers to still prefer mining and
collectingdatafrompublishedliterature188,189.Thislimitationindatasetscale
has posed a challenge for achieving high-quality predictions with small
datasets, necessitating researchers to focus on feature engineering, including
data cleaning and feature screening. For instance, a training set comprising
only 70 experimentally synthesized NASICON materials was used to train a
logistic regression-based classiﬁcation model. By screening 8 key features
from 47 initial features through permutation and combination evaluation,
the feature dimension was reduced by 83%, signiﬁcantly improving model
performance190. The model achieved an accuracy exceeding 84% in distin-
guishing the superior and inferior performance of superionic conductors.
Researchers have also explored methods such as transfer learning191,192 to
address small dataset issues. Beyond direct utilization of external data
sources, high-quality datasets can be generated through calculations. A
high-precision machine learning interatomic potential (ML-IAP) was
developed to study the cathode–electrolyte interface of SSLSBs193. The
research ﬁrst employed a passive learning phase, using ab initio molecular
dynamics (AIMD) simulations to generate~3000 structures underdifferent
temperatures and volume strains, followed by static self-consistent calcu-
lations via density functional theory (DFT) to obtain accurate energy, force,
and stress data for moment tensor potential (MTP) ﬁtting. In the sub-
sequent active learning phase, seven interface models were constructed to
iteratively optimize the MTP. Large-scale molecular dynamics (MD)
simulations further validated the MTP’s capability to predict interface
reaction products and lithium diffusion pathways, providing critical refer-
ences for the design of cathode–electrolyte interfaces in next-generation
SSLSBs(Fig.6a).Giventhatmaterialsand technologiesinthe SSLSB ﬁeld do
not develop in isolation, the dataset selection scope for SSLSBs can be
expanded to the broader domain of solid-state batteries. The advancements
in solid-state batteries and lithium metal batteries can similarly promote the
development of SSLSBs, providing high-quality datasets for AI applications
in SSLSB research.
In terms of model selection, classical supervised machine learning
algorithms have been applied to predict the ionic conductivity of SSEs190,
including linear regressor, support vector machine, decision tree, artiﬁcial
neural network, random forest regressor, gradient boosting regressor,
extreme gradient boosting, and voting regressor. When trained and vali-
dated on a dataset of 120 SSEs, the voting regressor (an ensemble of gradient
boosting regressor, linear regressor, and random forest regressor) demon-
strated the best performance, effectively avoiding overﬁtting issues. Mean-
while, studies have shown that simple models outperform complex ones on
small datasets194. Compared with supervised learning, unsupervised learn-
ing models can mine latent correlations in data without labeled samples,
mitigating the impact of low-quality data and scarcity of labeled datasets.

3D printing
3D printing technologies have been widely investigated in the ﬁeld of
lithium batteries182, particularly in Li−S batteries9,10,201 and solid-state
batteries202–204, with straightforward extensibility to the domain of SSLSBs.
This technology demonstrates remarkable advantages in the fabrication of
ﬂexible batteries. For instance, inkjet printing enables the use of solvated
Li3PS4nanoparticlesasinorganicion−conductivebinders205(Fig.6c).These
nanoparticles undergo pressure−free sintering during solvent removal at
250 °C, integrating Li2S@CNT composite cathodes into monolithic struc-
tures while endowing ﬂexibility. This approach allows stable cycling for 300
cycles without stacking pressure, delivering a capacity of 840 mAh g−1Li2S
3D printing also enables precise control over electrolyte layer thickness206,
addressing the challenge of thin electrolyte fabrication. Researchers have
achieved in-situ fabrication of solid−state electrolyte layers with a thickness
of only 2.8 µm on electrodes via inkjet printing207. Moreover, beneﬁting
from the ﬁne regulation of printed electrode structures208, 3D printing offers
a novel strategy for achieving high sulfur loading in SSLSBs.
Compared with liquid Li−S batteries, the manufacturing of SSLSBs
eliminatestheneedforelectrolyteinjection,enablingcontinuousfabrication
of integrated cathodes and electrolytes via 3D printing. This process ensures
excellent interfacial bonding between electrodes and electrolytes. In one
study, two thermodynamically immiscible gels were used to prepare the
cathode and gel electrolyte209. Speciﬁcally, an ethyl methyl sulfone (EMS)-
based gel electrolyte and sulfur cathode paste were stencil-printed and cured
on arbitrary continuous surfaces. Subsequently, a tetraethylene glycol
dimethyl ether (TEGDME)-based gel was coated onto the cathode using an

fabrication, the cathode curing time is less than 1 minute, rendering the
entire process continuous while achieving seamless integration of the
electrolyte and cathode layers (Fig. 6d). This technology not only enables
facile printing of non-planar SSLSBs on curved surfaces but also supports
fabrication of multilayer SSLSB. Unlike ﬂexible batteries that require
bending, batteries printed directly on curved surfaces adapt to the curvature
naturally, resulting in minimal internal stress.
Discussion
In summary, this review has comprehensively analyzed the critical chal-
lenges and prospective solutions associated with the practical application of
SSLSBs. We have moved beyond a solely materials-centric perspective,
typical of many reviews, to address crucial questions regarding the
application-oriented viability of SSLSBs. We also investigate the substantial
potential of SSLSBs in revolutionizing energy storage systems, particularly
for applications demanding high energy density and enhanced safety, such
as electric vehicles, unmanned aerial and unmanned underwater vehicles,
autonomous robotics, and wearable electronics. However, the transition
from laboratory prototypes to widespread commercial applications is
multifaceted, requiring concerted efforts across materials science, device
engineering, and manufacturing processes. Our analysis highlights that
while SSLSBs possess inherent advantages in terms of theoretical energy
density and safety, signiﬁcant hurdles remain in translating these beneﬁts to
practical, commercially viable devices. Key challenges include: (1) achieving
high power density and fast-charging capabilities, primarily limited by the
ionic conductivity of solid-state electrolytes and the sluggish kinetics of
sulfur redox reactions; (2) improving cycling performance by mitigating
volume expansion-induced electrode degradation and preventing dendrite
penetration of the solid electrolyte; and (3) realizing high practical energy
densities by optimizing inactive components and considering device-level
parameters, as demonstrated through our pouch cell modeling. We have
reviewed various technological breakthroughs to address these limitations,
including material innovations (such as aliovalent substitution in solid-state
electrolytes, mixed ion-electron conductors, redox mediators, and self-
healing SSEs) and structural design strategies (such as photothermal con-
version systems, sulfur conﬁnement, conductive frameworks, and nanos-
tructured cathodes) as well as interfacial engineering approaches. Our
modeling investigates the impacts of parameters (e.g., sulfur loading, sulfur
ratio, SSE thickness, and N/P ratio) on the energy density of device-level
SSLSBs, indicating the necessity of high sulfur loading, thin electrolytes, and
multi-layer electrode stacking in the design of high device-level energy
density.Meanwhile,itisrevealedthatsulfurcontentatahighrationolonger
optimizes cost-effectiveness.
The integration of advanced technologies with SSLSBs has provided
transformative opportunities for the development and optimization of
SSLSBs. 3D printing technology offers exciting possibilities for customized
SSLSB manufacturing, enabling one-step fabrication to improve interfacial
bonding between electrodes and electrolytes while effectively controlling
component dimensions. This approach is expected to reduce production
costs of thin electrolytes for SSLSBs and enhance design ﬂexibility. Future
research should focus on optimizing 3D printing parameters and materials
to ensure compatibility with various SSLSB material systems, and to achieve
mechanical integrity and electrochemical performance exceeding tradi-
tional manufacturing methods. The surge of AI technology has also accel-
erated SSLSB research. AI- and ML-driven material design possesses
multiple core advantages: ML algorithms trained on massive material
property datasets can predict and design novel solid-state electrolyte com-
positions and cathode materials, signiﬁcantly reducing reliance on time-
consuming traditional trial-and-error experiments. Additionally, they help
uncover complex relationships and patterns in large-scale data, avoiding
critical information that may be missed by manual analysis. With AI
technology, researchers can notably accelerate the discovery and develop-
ment of high-performance SSLSBs, ultimately promoting their large-scale
commercialization. The integration of fundamental materials science,
