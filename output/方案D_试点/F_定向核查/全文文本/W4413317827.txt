ABSTRACT
Sulfur
positive
electrodes
in
liquid-type
lithium–sulfur
(Li–S)
batteries exhibit two discharge plateaus at approximately 2.4 and
2.1 V (vs. Li). However, conventional sulfur composite electrodes in
all-solid-state Li–S batteries typically do not display these two clear
discharge plateaus. Furthermore, the reaction mechanisms in such
frameworks remain poorly understood. In this study, the cause of
these diﬀerences and reaction pathways were clariﬁed by examining
the reaction progression and distribution through the electrode
thickness using hard X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, Raman
spectroscopy, and incremental capacity analysis (ΔQ/ΔV). The results
indicate that the reaction proceeds from neutral bridged sulfur to
Li2S via lithium polysulﬁdes with Li-coordinated anionic sulfur, similar
to liquid type ones. Furthermore, clear diﬀerences in the reaction
progression
across the
electrode
thickness
are
observed.
In
low-reactivity electrodes, the degree of reaction progress varies more signiﬁcantly across the thickness compared with that in highly
reactive electrodes. This suggests that in low-reactivity sulfur composite electrodes, the two-plateau reactions proceed simultaneously
throughout the discharge region. In contrast, high-reactivity electrodes show clear discharge plateaus, with regions in which only the
second plateau reaction occurs. These insights will accelerate further understanding of the reaction for all-solid-state Li–S batteries.

1. Introduction
All-solid-state lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries represent one of the
most promising candidates for next-generation batteries character-
ized by high energy density and enhanced safety. This improved
performance is attributable to the use of sulfur as a positive
electrode active material, which has a high theoretical capacity of
1672 mAh g¹1, as well as inorganic solid electrolytes (SEs).1,2
Compared with conventional Li–S batteries that use organic liquid
electrolytes, all-solid-state Li–S batteries demonstrate considerably
improved cycling performance as the dissolution of lithium
polysulﬁdes into electrolyte is suppressed.3 Furthermore, higher
energy densities can be expected by reducing the amount of
electrolyte in the positive electrode to lower the SE/S ratio.4
However, the reaction area and carrier transfer paths in solid-state
batteries are more limited than those in conventional liquid-type
batteries owing to reduced contact between particles. Thus, their
charge–discharge reactivity is lower than that of conventional liquid-
type Li–S batteries. Notably, liquid-type Li–S batteries clearly
exhibit two-stage discharge plateaus: the ﬁrst plateau at approx-
imately 2.4 V (vs. Li), corresponding to reactions such as S8 ¼
Li2S8, Li2S8 ¼ Li2S6, Li2S6, and Li2S8 ¼ Li2S4; and the second
plateau at approximately 2.1 V (vs. Li), pertaining to reactions like
Li2S4 ¼ Li2S2 and Li2S2 ¼ Li2S.5,6 In contrast, most solid-state Li–
S batteries do not exhibit these two-stage discharge plateaus and
show lower rate performance owing to their higher resistances.7,8
Furthermore, the reaction mechanisms of sulfur composite elec-
trodes in all-solid-state batteries remain poorly understood caused by
diﬃculties of identiﬁed reaction intermediates.9–11 In the Raman
spectroscopy and X-ray absorption spectroscopy analyses, the peaks
of the reaction intermediates overlapped with those of sulfur and
sulﬁde solid electrolytes, making it diﬃcult to unambiguously
conﬁrm their presence. Moreover, sulfur undergoes signiﬁcant
volume changes during charging and discharging, necessitating its
continuous conﬁnement to prevent electrode breakdown.12 This
renders
in situ
observations
of
the
charge–discharge process
challenging. Therefore, clariﬁcation of the reaction pathways and
mechanisms in sulfur positive composite electrodes in all-solid-state
batteries remains a crucial research challenge.
Prior
studies
have
demonstrated
improvements
in
sulfur
reactivity in all-solid-state Li–S batteries by using high P/S ratio
SEs and high-speciﬁc-surface-area carbon in the positive composite
electrodes, which resulted in slight multi-step discharge plateaus.13–15
In this study, we clarify part of the discharge reaction mechanism
and the eﬀect of improved sulfur reactivity on the electrode reaction
by analyzing the reaction distribution across the electrode cross-
section using Raman spectroscopy, synchrotron hard X-ray photo-

2. Experimental
Reagent-grade sulfur (Sigma Aldrich, 99.5 %), Li2S (Mitsuwa
Chemical, 99.9 %), P2S5 (Sigma Aldrich, 99 %), Li2SO4 (Sigma
Aldrich, 99.99 %), Li2CO3 (Rare Metallic Corp., 99.99 %), LiBr
(Sigma Aldrich, 99.999 %), activated carbon (AC, MSC-30, Kansai
Coke and Chemicals Co., Ltd.), and ketjenblack (KB, EC-600JD,
Lion Co.) were used as the starting materials. The sulfur reagent was
puriﬁed by sublimation. Li2SO4 and Li2CO3 were dried under
vacuum at 300 °C for 12 h. All process were carried out in an Ar
atmosphere or in a sealed vessel ﬁlled with Ar. The SEs and positive
composite electrodes were prepared by mechanical milling using a
planetary ball mill apparatus (Premium Line P-7, Fritsch Co.).
Li1.5PS3.3 (60Li2S–40P2S5) and Li4.0PS4.5 (80Li2S–20P2S5), as
the SEs for positive composite electrodes, were prepared by
mechanical milling at 500 rpm for 10 h with ZrO2 balls (ball
diameter 4 mm; total mass 160 g) in a ZrO2 pot, using reagents with
Li2S : P2S5 molar ratios of 60 : 40 and 80 : 20 (ionic conductivities
of 0.027 and 0.42 mS cm¹1 at 25 °C), respectively, following a
previously reported procedure.13,14 A reference SE (0.45Li2SO4–
0.30Li2CO3–0.25LiBr,
LSCB)
with
an
ionic
conductivity
of
3.6 µS cm¹1 at 25 °C was prepared by mechanical milling at
370 rpm for 60 h with ZrO2 balls (ball diameter 5 mm; total mass
120 g) in a ZrO2 pot, using reagents with molar ratios of
Li2SO4 : Li2CO3 : LiBr = 45 : 30 : 25, following a prior study.16
Positive composite electrodes were prepared by mechanical milling
at 370 rpm for 2 h with ZrO2 balls (ball diameter 5 mm; total mass
40 g) in a ZrO2 pot, using reagents with weight ratios of S : SE : AC
or KB = 50 : 40 : 10, following a previous report.13,14 The compo-
site electrodes fabricated with Li1.5PS3.3 + AC and Li4.0PS4.5 + KB
were
labeled
H-r
electrode
and
L-r
electrode,
respectively,
representing high- and low-reactivity sulfur electrode. A reference
positive composite electrode (Ref electrode) was prepared using the
same process with a weight ratio of S : LSCB : AC = 25 : 65 : 10.
The half-cell conﬁguration was established for the cross-sectional
analysis of the electrode: Al as the current collector/a sulfur positive
composite electrode/Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 (LPSC) as the second SE layer/
Li5.3PS4.3Br0.7Cl (LPSBC) and Li3PS4 glass (B-SE, weight ratio =
9 : 1) as the ﬁrst SE layer/Li–In alloy.17,18 Here, LPSC was prepared
by mechanical milling at 500 rpm for 10 h, followed by annealing at
450 °C for 5 h under Ar atmosphere, consistent with a prior report.19
The B-SE powder (80 mg) was placed in a polycarbonate tube (inner
diameter = 10 mm), which could be separated after releasing the
restrain, and pressurized at 200 MPa using stainless steel rods to
form the ﬁrst SE layer. Subsequently, the LPSC powder (50 mg),
sulfur positive electrode powder (7.5 mg), and Al powder (50 mg)
were placed on the ﬁrst SE layer and pressed under 200 MPa by
stainless steel rods. Li and In foils were stacked on the surface of the
pellet, making contact with the ﬁrst SE layer, and pressed under
80 MPa by stainless steel rods to obtain the Li–In alloy (Li/In =
0.64). In Lix–In (x < 1), the Li–In alloy maintained a constant
potential of 0.62 V vs. Li.20 The half-cell was assembled in an Ar-
ﬁlled glove box and placed in a sealed vessel ﬁlled with Ar.
Electrode performance was stabilized by charging and discharging
the cells over ﬁve cycles at 0.25 mA cm¹2 and 25 °C using a charge–
discharge measurement device (ACD-01, Asuka Electronics Co.
Ltd.). After discharge to speciﬁc states at 0.25 mA cm¹2 and 25 °C,
the assembly consisting of Al/positive composite electrode/second
SE layer was extracted from the cell in the Ar-ﬁlled glove box. The
half-cell of Ref electrode was assembled using the same cell
structure, but the positive composite electrode weight was 8.0 mg
and charge–discharge testing was performed at 60 °C.
¦Q/¦V analysis was performed using modiﬁed discharge
proﬁles of the half-cells. The discharge data at equal capacity
intervals were processed into proﬁles at 1 mV intervals. Consecutive
identical voltage values were deleted, and the data were linearly
interpolated between two points to ensure that the intervals between
the data points were 1 mV. The discharge data were smoothed using
a 20-point moving average, and ¦Q/¦V was calculated using the
central diﬀerence method.
Samples for sulfur chemical state analysis across the electrode
cross-section were obtained by extracting them from half-cells
conditioned to speciﬁc discharge states, following stabilization for
ﬁve cycles under the same conditions as in the charge–discharge
tests. AP-HAXPES measurements were performed using a diﬀer-
entially pumped analyzer (Scienta Omicron HiPP-2) installed at
BL46XU of SPring-8. The incident X-ray beam, with a photon
energy of 7.94 keV, was monochromatized using Si(111) double
crystals and Si(311) double-crystal channel-cut monochromators.
The X-ray was an incident on samples at an angle of 85° relative to
the surface normal, and the emitted photoelectrons were detected at
a take-oﬀangle of 85° relative to the surface plane. The Ar pressure
inside the analysis chamber was maintained at either 5000 or
1000 Pa. The X-ray beam width along the electrode thickness
direction was approximately 5 µm, and its intensity was decreased
using Al (50 µm) and Si (200 µm) to reduce sample damage.
Samples were prepared in an Ar-ﬁlled glove box and transferred to
the analysis chamber of the spectrometer using an airtight transfer
vessel. Al–electrode and SE–electrode interfaces were determined
by monitoring the Al 1s and Cl 1s spectra, respectively. After the
corresponding element was no longer detected, the measurement
points were for the Al and SE sides were positioned by moving 5 µm
toward the center of the electrode. Thus, these points were 5–15 µm
from each interface. The midpoint between these points, based on
the device position coordinates, was also established as a measure-
ment point. The binding energies of all spectra were calibrated to the
Fermi edge energy of Au (7940.04 eV), measured from an Au plate
as the external standard. After subtracting the Shirley background,
the S 1s HAXPES spectra were analyzed by curve ﬁtting to a
Lorentzian asymmetric line shape using CasaXPS software.
The Raman spectra of the electrode cross-sections were obtained
using a spectrometer (NRS-7100, JASCO Co.) with an excitation
wavelength of 532 nm. To prevent air exposure, the measurement
samples for Raman spectroscopy were prepared in an Ar-ﬁlled glove
box and sealed in a vessel using a thin glass plate and polyimide
tape. Measurements were conducted at the center of the electrode.

3. Results and Discussion
This study was focused on the cross-sectional analysis of
electrodes; thus, half-cells were assembled in a manner that
facilitated extraction of the electrodes after the charge–discharge
test. As shown in Fig. S1, electrodes in various discharge states
were extracted by releasing the constraint of a separable polycar-
bonate tube. In this conﬁguration, molding of the ﬁrst SE layer was
subjected to the maximum pressure of fabrication process. Thus, the
three-layered sample, consisting of Al/positive electrode/second
SE, could be readily extracted from the cell by ensuring a separation
between the ﬁrst and second SE layers. The Al and second SE layer
not only functioned as a support for the electrode but also helped
determine the HAXPES analysis position. This is because Al in the
current corrector and Cl in the second SE, were not components of
the electrodes, facilitating interlayer observations. This conﬁgu-
ration signiﬁcantly improved the success rate of electrode extraction
after charge–discharge tests. Furthermore, synchrotron HAXPES
enabled the analysis of deep regions (of the order of several tens of
nanometers), which are inaccessible to conventional photoelectron
spectroscopy relying on soft X-ray beams. This capability helped
mitigate the inﬂuence of equilibration within the electrode post-
extraction. Additionally, ambient-pressure (AP)-HAXPES measure-
ment enables to decrease sublimation of sulfur for stable analysis.
The positive electrodes used in this study were high-reactivity
(H-r) and low-reactivity (L-r) electrodes, prepared with Li1.5PS3.3
and Li4.0PS4.5 as SEs, respectively.13,14 These conﬁgurations were
selected to evaluate the diﬀerences in discharge proﬁles. Addition-
ally, a Ref electrode, composed of 45Li2SO4–0.30Li2CO3–0.25LiBr
(LSCB) as the SE, was used to evaluate sulfur reactions during
discharge.16 The binding energies of elemental sulfur and the SO4
component in LSCB were approximately 2472 and 2479 eV,
respectively, as measured by S 1s HAXPES of S–C and LSCB–C,
respectively (Fig. S2a). Thus, the S 1s spectrum of LSCB did not
overlap with those of elemental sulfur and its discharge components
with lower valence. Therefore, the Ref electrode was suitable for
evaluating the complete discharge reaction and discharge state of
sulfur at the analysis point, based on changes observed in the S 1s
HAXPES spectra.
The Ref electrode exhibited good reversibility at 60 °C. The
discharge
capacity

assessing the complete discharge reaction in the electrode, especially
along the thickness direction. The ¦Q/¦V proﬁle of the Ref
electrode during discharge in the 5th cycle showed a clear peak at
approximately 1.44 V (vs. Li–In) and shoulder peak at approx-
imately 1.52 V (vs. Li–In) (Fig. 1b). The modiﬁed discharge proﬁles
derived at 1 mV intervals were in good agreement with the original
curves, validating their suitability for ¦Q/¦V assessment. The
shoulder peak at approximately 1.52 V and clear peak at 1.44 V are
considered to correspond to the ﬁrst plateau reaction and a mixed
reaction involving both the ﬁrst and second plateau reactions
typically observed in liquid-type batteries, respectively. To conﬁrm
the reaction, S 1s HAXPES spectra of the cross-section of the Ref
electrode were measured through the thickness direction at diﬀerent
discharge states (0, 100, 500, 1100, and 1500 mAh g¹1 (S)) at
0.25 mA cm¹2 and 60 °C (Fig. 2). In the complete S 1s spectra
(Fig. 2a), three main components were observed at approximately
2472, 2470, and 2468 eV, attributable to neutral bridged sulfur, Li-
coordinated anionic sulfur in lithium polysulﬁdes, and S2¹ in Li2S,
respectively. Here, the spectral components were designated as S(0),
S(D¹), and S(2¹) for neutral bridged sulfur, Li-coordinated anionic
sulfur, and S2¹ in Li2S, respectively.
Figure 3 shows the Raman spectra of the cross-section of the Ref
electrode at diﬀerent discharge states. At 0 mAh g¹1 (S), peaks
corresponding to elemental sulfur (S8) were observed at approx-
imately 475, 220, and 150 cm¹1. These peaks dramatically decreas-
ed at a discharge state of 100 mAh g¹1 (S), and new peaks appeared
at around 445 cm¹1. It was diﬃcult to assign these peaks to
individual lithium polysulﬁdes, such as Li2Sx (x = 2–8), owing
to nearby peaks at approximately 440 cm¹1.21–24 However, the
formation of long-chain lithium polysulﬁdes was estimated by
the signiﬁcant decrease in sulfur peaks after discharging only
100 mAh g¹1 (S) because short-chain lithium polysulﬁdes gener-
ation require much amount of reduction proceeding. As discharge
progressed, the peak near 450 cm¹1 decreased, while that near
355 cm¹1, corresponding to Li2S, increased. The Li2S peak in the
Ref electrode exhibited a lower wavelength compared with that of
pure Li2S (375 cm¹1), attributable to the high polarity of LSCB.
Furthermore, the peak near 445 cm¹1 shifted to a slightly lower
wavelength of 440 cm¹1 at 1500 mAh g¹1 (S), consistent with the
formation of short-chain lithium sulﬁdes such as Li2S2. This
assignment is considered reasonable because the peak shift occurred
in the later stage of discharge, indicating that the peak near 440 cm¹1
is diﬀerent from that associated with elemental sulfur (Fig. S2b).
These results suggest the existence of several lithium polysulﬁde
species during discharge.

S 1s spectra, whereas Li2S4, and Li2S2 would show only the S(D¹)
peak. Below, we discuss the progress of the reaction in the

of the H-r and L-r electrodes. The H-r and L-r electrodes showed
speciﬁc capacities of 1760 and 1320 mAh g¹1 (S) in the 5th cycle at
0.25 mA cm¹2 and 25 °C, respectively. These modiﬁed discharge
proﬁles of H-r and L-r electrodes derived at 1 mV intervals were in
good agreement with the original curves, validating their suitability
for ¦Q/¦V assessment (Figs. 4b and 4d). The H-r electrode
exhibited a higher speciﬁc capacity and discharge potential,
attributable to improved sulfur reactivity owing to the use of a high
P/S ratio SE and high-surface-area carbon, consistent with previous
reports.13,14 Notably, the capacity of the H-r electrode included the
high contribution from the SE at 0.7 V (vs. Li–In), resulting in a
higher capacity than the theoretical capacity of sulfur. In addition,
the discharge curve of the H-r electrode showed a slight multi-step
discharge plateau, considerably diﬀerent from the behavior of the
L-r electrode. These diﬀerences were more clearly reﬂected in the
¦Q/¦V plots at the 5th discharge curve (Fig. 4b). The ¦Q/¦V of
the L-r electrode exhibited a single broad peak at approximately
1.32 V (vs. Li–In), similar to the typical proﬁle of a sulfur composite
electrode. In contrast, the ¦Q/¦V curve of the H-r electrode showed
two clear peaks at approximately 1.41 and 1.29 V (vs. Li–In), as well
as a broad shoulder around 1.52 V (vs. Li–In). The peak near 1.29 V
(vs. Li–In) was sharp, suggesting a single plateau reaction. Therefore,
the discharge reactions in the H-r electrode likely proceeds mainly
through the ﬁrst plateau reaction, a combination of the ﬁrst and
second plateau reactions, and mainly the second plateau reaction at
approximately 1.52, 1.41, and 1.29 V (vs. Li–In), respectively.
In the L-r electrode, characterized by low reactivity, the ﬁrst
plateau component remained on the current collector side caused by
slower reaction kinetics during discharge. This reaction proceeded
simultaneously with the second plateau reaction on the separator SE
side. This is likely why no peak separation was observed in the
¦Q/¦V curve, even in the later stages of discharge. In addition, the
eﬀect of improving sulfur reactivity using a high P/S ratio SE and
high-surface-area carbon were conﬁrmed in the ¦Q/¦V analysis of
composite electrodes with compositions S : Li1.5PS3.3 : KB = 50 :
40 : 10 and S : Li4.0PS4.5 : AC = 50 : 40 : 10, as shown in Fig. S3.
Both ¦Q/¦V curves showed two peaks, but the diﬀerences in the
peaks were smaller than that observed in the H-r electrode. This
suggests that the high P/S ratio SE and high-surface-area carbon
individually aﬀect the sulfur reactivity. A larger distance between
the two ¦Q/¦V peaks typically corresponds to a higher sulfur
reactivity. When the sulfur reaction resistance is small, the relative
impact of its increase at the second plateau becomes signiﬁcant,
suppressing the SE-side reaction and allowing each plateau reaction
to proceed independently, which results in greater peak separation.
Since the eﬀect of reaction resistance is considered to be more
signiﬁcant than that of ionic conductivity resistance under the
present conditions, a larger peak separation can be regarded as an
indicator of higher reactivity. Thus, the order of eﬀectiveness for
enhancing reactivity was high P/S ratio SE + high-speciﬁc-surface-
area carbon, only high P/S ratio SE, and only high-speciﬁc-surface-
area carbon.
To verify this phenomenon and hypothesis regarding sulfur
reactivity, the change in the state of sulfur in the thickness direction
of the electrode cross-section in each discharge state was conﬁrmed
by AP-HAXPES (Fig. 5). At the discharge state of 0 mAh g¹1 (S),
S(D¹) was observed in addition to S(0), owing to the presence of the
sulﬁde SE even in the full-charge state, where only elemental sulfur
is expected as the redox species. Thus, unlike the Ref electrode,
understanding the reaction progress in the H-r electrode requires
consideration of the S(D¹) component derived from the sulﬁde SE.
At the discharge state of 100 mAh g¹1 (S), corresponding to the
broad shoulder around 1.52 V (vs. Li–In) in the ¦Q/¦V curve, S(0)
decreased and increased S(D¹) in all areas, with the amount of
change slightly higher toward the SE side. This proﬁle was similar
to that for the Ref electrode, indicating that only the ﬁrst plateau
reaction progressed in this potential region. Here, the (D¹) peak was
slightly shifted to higher energy due to mixing with the (D¹)
component of the SE and the higher-energy (D¹) species in Li2Sx.
The diﬀerence in reaction progress across the thickness direction
became more signiﬁcant in the 400 mAh g¹1 (S) discharge state,
corresponding to the ﬁrst peak near 1.41 V (vs. Li–In) in the ¦Q/
¦V curve. Toward the SE side, the decrease in S(0) became more
notable, and the proportion of S(D¹) increased. Furthermore, S(2¹)
appeared on the SE side, indicating that the second plateau reaction
progressed along with the ﬁrst plateau reaction in this potential
region. At the 1100 mAh g¹1 (S) discharge state, corresponding to
the second sharp peak at approximately 1.29 V (vs. Li–In) in the
¦Q/¦V curve, no S(0) was observed in any area, and S(2¹)
gradually increased toward the SE side. This suggests that the
second plateau reaction proceeded in this potential region, as species
containing neutral bridged sulfur (such as Li2S8 and Li2S6), which is
a reactant in the ﬁrst plateau reaction were no longer observed. At
the 1500 mAh g¹1 (S) discharge state, corresponding to the ﬁnal
stage of discharge, only the second plateau reaction proceeded.
However, the diﬀerences in S(D¹) and S(2¹) ratios were small in all
areas, as in the case of the Ref electrode, owing to the reduction in
reactivity owing to decreased reactant concentration. The overall
reaction in the H-r electrode predicted from the ¦Q/¦V curve was
consistent with the S 1s HAXPES spectra, highlighting that the two-
step plateau reaction observed in liquid-type batteries also occurs in
all-solid-state Li–S batteries.

Additionally, the ﬁrst charge–discharge curve of the H-r electrode

from the solid electrolyte below 0.8 V (vs. Li–In) (Fig. 6). The

reduction of Li2S4 and Li2S2 produced during the stepwise reduction
of S8. This is further evidence that the increased reactivity at 60 °C
leads to a more pronounced separation of the ﬁrst and second
plateau reactions, and that the S-S bonds are broken stepwise
starting from S8.
Overall, in general sulfur composite electrodes for all-solid-state
Li–S batteries, similar to the low-reactivity electrode considered in
this study, the ﬁrst plateau reactants likely remain throughout the
electrode even in the later stages of discharge, reacting simulta-
neously with the second plateau reactants during the entire discharge
process. This provides valuable insights into the reactions of sulfur
in all-solid-state Li–S batteries. Furthermore, the diﬀerence in the
degree of reaction progress in the thickness direction of the electrode
leads to the ﬁrst and second discharge plateau reactions occurring
simultaneously, attributable to the high sulfur reaction resistance.
The degree of overlap of these two plateau reactions depends on the
sulfur reactivity. As the sulfur in the electrode may be in a partial
equilibrium state after being removed from the cell, operando
measurements are necessary in future work to further understand the
charge–discharge reactions of sulfur in solid-state Li–S batteries.

Owing to the low electrical conductivity of sulfur, the diﬀerence in
reaction progress across the thickness of the electrode could be
observed using synchrotron HAXPES. However, these diﬀerence
may be even more signiﬁcant during actual discharging, which must

results and experimental procedures presented in this work will
facilitate further exploration of sulfur reactions in not only all-solid-
state Li–S batteries but also other solid-state batteries.
4. Conclusions
The progress of sulfur discharge reactions in all-solid-state Li–S
batteries was comprehensively examined using Raman spectrosco-
py, synchrotron AP-HAXPES, and ¦Q/¦V analysis of discharge
curves. We demonstrated the eﬀectiveness of evaluation of sulfur
reactions using a Ref electrode that uses LSCB, which does not
overlap with sulfur and its discharge products in Raman and
HAXPES spectra. The reaction progress in the through-thickness
direction at diﬀerent discharge states was explored. The results
highlighted that in all-solid-state Li–S batteries, as in liquid-type
systems, several lithium polysulﬁdes are involved in diﬀerent stages,
and the reaction progress varies in the thickness direction.
Furthermore, diﬀerences of sulfur reactivity aﬀect not only the
reversible capacity but also the reaction progress along the thickness
direction. The H-r electrode, characterized by high sulfur reactivity,
exhibits a clear discharge plateau at approximately 1.29 V (vs. Li–
In), indicating the progression of only the second plateau reaction.
