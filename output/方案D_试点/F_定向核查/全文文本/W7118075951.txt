ABSTRACT
All-solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) hold great promise as next-generation energy storage systems due to their
high energy density. However, the practical application of sulfur-based cathodes are largely hampered by their sluggish reaction
kinetics, especially under high current densities and long-term cycling. Herein, by employing electronic structure modulation
via same-group element doping, we successfully engineer lithium sulfide to concurrently facilitate ionic diffusion and electronic
conduction, thereby significantly boosting its reaction kinetics. The formation of Se─S bonds, achieved by partial Se substitution,
reorganizes the electronic structure of Li2S. This effect concurrently weakens sulfur’s electronegativity to facilitate Li+ diffusion
and narrows the bandgap to boost electronic conduction. As a result, the Li2Se0.2S0.8 cathode exhibits remarkable high-rate
capability, retaining 97.5% of its capacity after 1000 cycles at 1 A g−1. Moreover, a full cell combining Li2Se0.2S0.8 with a Si anode
delivers a high energy density of 1324 Wh kg−1, highlighting the feasibility of high-specific-energy and high safety ASSLSBs. This
work provides an effective strategy toward high-energy and high-power solid-state batteries.

Introduction
Nowadays, rechargeable batteries with high energy density and
enhanced safety are in urgent demand as next-generation energy
storage solutions for low-altitude aviation applications [1]. All–
solid–state lithium–sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs), which employ
non–flammable solid–state electrolytes (SSEs) in place of conven-
tional organic liquid electrolytes, effectively mitigate the shuttle
effect and align well with the requirements for high energy
density, improved safety, and long cycle life [2–4]. However, the
absence of soluble polysulfide intermediates and the obstructed
or tortuous transport pathways for both Li+ and electrons within
the S or Li2S cathode result in sluggish reaction kinetics. This
limitation hinders the practical realization of their theoretically
high energy density in ASSLSBs [5].
In fact, numerous strategies have been employed to enhance reac-
tion kinetics, including size control [6–7], structural design [8–
10], and applied pressure [11, 12]. Nevertheless, these approaches
have encountered significant limitations [13–15]. For example,
nanoparticles, despite their small size, are prone to agglom-
eration, which compromises the dispersion homogeneity of

of cathode materials offers a promising path forward. This
approach not only shortens Li+/e−transport distance, thereby
accelerating reaction kinetics, but also reduces the reliance

bility and overall energy density, as well as extend cycling
life [5]. Therefore, increasing the intrinsic conductivity of sul-
fur represents a crucial and effective strategy for realizing
ASSLSBs with high energy density, superior safety, and long cycle
life.
Previous studies have explored the strategies of introducing
highly conductive atoms into sulfur and elucidated the underly-
ing conversion mechanisms in both liquid and solid electrolyte
systems [10, 20, 21]. However, they remain based on sulfur-based
cathodes, and the problem of volume expansion of sulfur during
cycling and the consequent poor cycling stability are inadequately
addressed. Therefore, to address this issue, we propose an
electronic structure reorganization of Li2S via same-group ele-
ment doping. This approach enhances its intrinsic ion/electron
conductivity, thereby accelerating the electrode reaction kinetics
(Figure 1b). This is a novel system that directly optimizes
the lithium content, which can more effectively improve ion
transport efficiency compared to traditional SeSx. In addition,
Li2SexS1-x can be directly matched with lithium-free anodes such
as silicon/graphite, making the overall battery design safer and
more feasible.
The Li2SexS1−x (x = 0–0.3) cathode materials were synthesized
via a solid-state reaction involving ball milling and subsequent
heat treatment of Se, S, and LiH. All obtained compounds
maintain the face-centered cubic structure, with a continuous
lattice expansion observed as the Se content increases—the
lattice parameter rises from 5.72 Å (x = 0) to 5.79 Å (x = 0.3),
and the unit cell volume increases from 187.12 to 194.06 Å3.
Moreover, the formation of Se─S bonds, which modulate the
electronic structure of Li2S: (1) Reduces the electronegativity of
sulfur, weakening its interaction with Li+ and thus facilitating
Li+ diffusion; (2) Narrows the bandgap from 3.418 to 3.391 eV,
enhancing electronic conduction of Li2S. As a result, the Li+
migration barrier drops significantly from 0.42 to 0.22 eV, and
the Li+ conductivity increases nearly threefold. In all-solid-
state lithium–sulfur batteries, the Li2Se0.2S0.8 cathode exhibits a

The ion and electronic conductivities of Li2Se0.2S0.8 and Li2S were
evaluated, as shown in Figures 2f and S6. The Li+ conductivity
of Li2Se0.2S0.8 at room temperature reached 4.80 × 10−6 S cm−1,
nearly three times that of Li2S (1.64 × 10−6 S cm−1). In addition,
the electronic conductivity of Li2S is 2.26 × 10−9 S cm−1, while
that of Li2Se0.2S0.8 (4.58 × 10−9 S cm−1) is approximately twice as
high. This indicates that the introduction of Se not only effectively
enhances Li+ conduction but also concurrently improves the elec-
tron transport capability of the material, leading to a significant
improvement in its overall conductivity. In ASSLSBs with the
configuration Li2SexS1−x|Li6PS5Cl|Li, the polarization voltages of

Li2Se0.2S0.8 cathode delivered a high initial discharge capacity of
1000 mA h g−1 at 50 mA g−1, substantially exceeding that of the
undoped Li2S cathode (Figure S7).
To probe the influence of selenium to reaction kinetics, cyclic
voltammogram (CV) was employed to estimate the Li+ diffusion
coefficients in Li2SexS1−x (x = 0–0.3) cathodes. As depicted in
Figure S8, the peak currents increased with scan rate, and both
oxidation and reduction peaks exhibited a linear relationship
with the square root of the scan rate, indicating a diffusion-
controlled process. According to the Randles-Sevcik equation, the
Li+ diffusion coefficient is positively correlated with the slope
of the peak current versus scan rate1/2 [26]. The slopes for the
anodic and cathodic peaks of all Li2SexS1−x (x = 0.1–0.3) cathodes
were higher than that of pristine Li2S cathode. In particular,
the values for Li2Se0.2S0.8 were 1.82 (anodic) and 1.66 (cathodic),
more than four times that of the Li2S cathode, confirming that
the introduction of Se effectively enhances the Li+ diffusion rate
(Figure 2h).
Further analysis via Galvanostatic Intermittent Titration Tech-
nique (GITT) provided quantitative values of the Li+ diffusion
coefficients (Figures 2i; S9). The average Li+ diffusion coefficients
for Li2Se0.3S0.7, Li2Se0.2S0.8, Li2Se0.1S0.9, and Li2S were calculated to
be 2.21 × 10−12, 3.72 × 10−12, 1.31 × 10−12, and 8.28 × 10−13 cm2 s−1,
respectively. Notably, the value for Li2Se0.2S0.8 is approximately
five times greater than that of the undoped sample. The enhanced
Li+ migration kinetics and ionic conductivity can be attributed
to the introduction of partial Se, which replaces S in the lattice
to form S─Se bonds, reorganizes the electronic structure of Li2S,
the overall electrostatic binding of Li+ in the doped lattice is
weakened, facilitating more efficient Li+ deintercalation and
migration during charge and discharge processes. Furthermore,

To elucidate the conversion mechanism of Se in Li2SexS1−x
cathodes, ex situ XPS and XRD measurements were performed
on the Li2Se0.2S0.8 cathode at different stages of the first charge–
discharge cycle (Figure 3a–d). In the S 2p spectrum, the main
doublet at 161.28 eV is assigned to S in argyrodite-type Li6PS5Cl
(P─S─Li) at the Li2Se0.2S0.8/Li6PS5Cl interface [27]. Simultane-
ously, Additional signals at 166.98 and 168.08 eV correspond to
sulfate and sulfite species, respectively, resulting from surface
oxidation. At the pristine state, both S 2p and Se 3d spectra show
clear signals of Li─S─Se and Se─S bonds. Upon charging, the
intensity of the Li─S─Se signal decreases while that of Se─S sig-
nals increases. During discharge, these signals gradually revert to
their original intensities, indicating a reversible transformation.
This reversibility is corroborated by ex situ XRD patterns of the
Li2Se0.2S0.8 (Figure 3d), confirming that the conversion pathway
of Li2SexS1−x is analogous to that of Li2S, with Se remaining
incorporated in the structure throughout cycling.
In situ EIS was conducted at 50 mA h g−1 interval under a
current density of 100 mA g−1 to monitor interfacial resistance
evolution in the Li2Se0.2S0.8|Li6PS5Cl|Li cell. The Nyquist plots
exhibit semicircles in the high-frequency region and sloping
lines in the low-frequency region (Figure S10). Given the high
compatibility between the argyrodite electrolyte and the Li-metal
anode [28], the impedance changes mainly reflect processes at
the cathode/SSE interface. During charging, both the charge
transfer resistance (Rct) and diffusion resistance increase steadily.
This rise can be attributed to two factors: (1) the conversion of
highly conductive Li2Se0.2S0.8 phase into less conductive product
(Se0.2S0.8), and (2) volumetric change that degrade electrical
contact within the cathode. Upon discharge, re‑lithiation restores
conductive pathways, leading to a progressive reduction in both
interfacial and diffusion resistances.
Distribution of relaxation times (DRT) analysis of the in situ EIS
data further resolves the underlying processes. During charging,
two prominent peaks appear at time constants of 0.1 and 3.2
s, corresponding to cathode/SSE interface resistance and intra-
cathode diffusion resistance, respectively [27]. The peak at 3.2

Conclusion
To address the sluggish kinetics of sulfur cathodes in all-solid-
state lithium-sulfur batteries, we have successfully engineered the
electronic structure of Li2S. This restructuring enhances the dual
conductivity of ions and electrons, thereby accelerating the over-
all reaction kinetics. The Li2SexS1−x (x = 0–0.3) was successfully

The introduction of Se─S bonds successfully lowers the Li+
migration barrier from 0.42 to 0.22 eV and narrows the bandgap
from 3.418 to 3.391 eV. Consequently, the Li+ conductivity is
enhanced by nearly threefold, and the electronic conductivity
has increased by two times. The Li+ diffusion coefficient of the
Li2Se0.2S0.8 cathode is significantly increased to 3.72 × 10−12 cm2
s−1, which is approximately five times that of the pristine Li2S
cathode (8.28 × 10−13 cm2 s−1). These improvements collectively
enhance the Li+/e−transport dynamics. Hence, the all-solid-
state Li2Se0.2S0.8|Li6PS5Cl|Li cell not only delivers a satisfactory
reversible discharge capacity of 721.1 mA h g−1 and remarkable
capacity retention as high as 94.86% after 140 cycles at a current
density of 200 mA g−1, but also exhibits excellent rate capability
(603.2 mA h g−1 at a high current density of 1000 mA g−1). After
1000 charge–discharge cycles at current density of 1 A g−1, the
electrode material maintained a specific capacity of 545.7 mA
h g−1 with a high capacity retention rate of 97.5%. This work
provides new insights on addressing the inherently low Li+/e−
reaction kinetics and inferior rate capability of Li2S cathode in
ASSLSBs.
