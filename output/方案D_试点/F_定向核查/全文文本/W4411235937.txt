Current Status and Future Prospects of Solid-State
Lithium–Sulfur Batteries: A Focus on Reaction and Interface
Engineering
Ru Xiao, Zhuoyan Qu, Junxing Ren, Guoxiu Wang,* Zhenhua Sun,* and Feng Li*
The burgeoning development of solid-state electrolytes signiﬁcantly improves
the safty and practicality of solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries (LSBs). Based
on mature solid-state electrolytes, challenges in electrochemical performance
remain, largely due to complex reactions and interfacial issues on both sulfur
and lithium sides. This review comprehensively examines the fundamental
challenges and recent progress from the perspectives of reaction and
interface. From a reaction standpoint, it discusses the trade-oﬀbetween
shuttle eﬀect and redox kinetics, as well as the irreversible accumulation
of kinetically dead sulfur across diﬀerent electrolytes, which were often
overlooked. Regarding interfaces, it discusses the formation of interfacial
dead sulfur within the cathode and strategies to enhance the across-interface
transport of charge carriers. It also analyzes mechanisms underlying lithium
dendrite formation and interface failure, along with current solutions to
mitigate dead lithium and extend lithium anode lifespan. In pursuit of
meeting commercial demands for solid-state LSBs, engineering parameters
targeting high energy density are speciﬁed by formulations, and diﬀerences in
parameter design principles among diﬀerent electrolyte systems are
systematically analyzed. Finally, to bridge fundamental insights with practical
applications, future research directions are proposed, emphasizing reaction
and interface engineering for high-performance solid-state LSBs.

1. Introduction
The energy storage and conversion ca-
pabilities of electrochemical energy stor-
age devices are pivotal in bridging the
gap between inherently intermittent and
dispersed renewable energy sources and
practical production applications. The
evolution of commercialized secondary
battery systems, from lead-acid to nickel-
metal hydride and lithium-ion batteries
(LIBs), has signiﬁcantly transformed our
daily lives, providing us with everything
from portable electronic devices to elec-
tric vehicles. However, the energy den-
sity of LIBs based on Li+ intercalation
reactions has approached its theoretical
value, potentially limiting their applica-
tion in larger-scale energy storage and
electrical devices in the future. Lithium–
sulfur batteries (LSBs), utilizing low-cost
and abundant sulfur as the cathode ma-
terial, boost a theoretical energy density
(2600 Wh kg−1), signiﬁcantly higher than
that of commercial LIBs (387 Wh kg−1
for LiCoO2-graphite batteries).[1–4] Never-
theless, the market penetration of LSBs
based on liquid electrolytes is still hin-
dered by signiﬁcant safety issues and
a low actual gravimetric energy density, which is below
500 Wh kg−1.[5,6] Particularly under lean electrolyte conditions
(electrolyte/sulfur ratio below 3 μL mg−1), the dissolution of
lithium polysulﬁdes (LiPSs) increases the viscosity of the elec-
trolyte and restricts the reaction kinetics of the sulfur cathode.[7,8]
The shuttle of LiPSs exacerbates side reaction on lithium an-
ode, leading to continuous consumption of electrolyte, inferior
solid electrolyte interphase (SEI), and lithium dendrite growth,
which are the primary causes of short circuit and thermal
runaway.[9,10] Over the past decades, advanced sulfur host ma-
terials and electrolyte formulations have been developed to pro-
mote the redox kinetics of the sulfur cathode while protecting
the lithium anode, aiming to achieve higher actual energy den-
sity and cycle capability, although it is often challenging to rec-
oncile both simultaneously.[11–20] For example, a 700 Wh kg−1-
level Li–S pouch cell with an ultra-low electrolyte/sulfur ratio of
1.7 gelectrolyte gsulfur
−1 was developed by Li et al. based on a mod-
iﬁed electrolyte formula, which demonstrated stable operation
over three cycles.[20]

Despite the signiﬁcant eﬀorts have been made to develop
practical high-energy-density LSBs, the safety risks associated
with liquid electrolytes still exist, including leak, ﬁre, and explo-
sion accidents. These risks are exacerbated by lithium dendrites,
which are thermodynamically inevitable and the root cause of
the battery short circuits and thermal runaway.[21,22] Solid-state
LSBs, using solid-state electrolytes (SSEs), have emerged as a
promising solution among various approaches aimed at address-
ing security issues, as they eliminate liquid leakage and exhibit
low ﬂammability.[23,24] SSEs used by solid-state LSBs mainly in-
clude two types: solid-state polymer electrolytes (SPEs) and in-
organic SSEs. A comparison of the characteristics of SSEs is
presented in Figure 1a. SPEs provide a solvation environment
akin to liquid electrolytes but suﬀer from slower lithium ions
(Li+) migration due to limited polymer chain motion.[25,26] Typ-
ically, SPEs exhibit a low ionic conductivity (<10−6 S cm−1)
at room temperature (RT), necessitating operation at an ele-
vated temperature above 60 °C. In terms of mechanical prop-
erties, the inadequate mechanical strength of SPEs at opera-
tional temperatures hinders their ability to suppress lithium
dendrite growth, while their inherent ﬂexibility promotes favor-
able electrode–electrolyte interfacial contact. Compared to inor-
ganic SSEs, SPEs oﬀer lower manufacturing and device inte-
gration costs, and their lightweight nature enables the devel-
opment of high-energy-density batteries. For inorganic SSEs,
which include oxide, sulﬁde, and their derivatives, act as single-
ion conductors, facilitating Li+ transport through the hopping
mechanism between lattice sites.[24,27] Developed oxide-SSEs
primarily involve NASICON-type Li1+xAlxTi2-x(PO4)3 (LATP)
and Li1+xAlxGe2-x(PO4)3 (LAGP),[28,29] garnet-type Li7La3Zr2O12
(LLZO),[30,31] and perovskite-type Li3xLa(2/3)-xTiO3 (LLTO),[32]
which have RT ionic conductivities of over 10−4 S cm−1. Although
oxide-SSEs demonstrate superior chemical and electrochemical
stability compared to sulﬁde-SSEs and SPEs, their primary draw-
back is the high electrolyte–electrode interfacial resistance and
poor processability, stemming from their inherent mechanical
strength and fragility, which restricts their application in solid-
state LSBs.[33–39] Among all SSEs, sulﬁde-SSEs present the high-
est ionic conductivities over 10−2 S cm−1 at RT, comparable to
liquid electrolytes (10−3 to 10−2 S cm−1). They primarily com-
posed of Li2S and compounds containing Ge, P, Si, and other
elements, manifesting in forms such as glass (e.g., Li2S-P2S5,
Li2S-SiS2),[40,41] glass-ceramic (e.g., Li7P3S11, Li3PS4),[42,43] and
crystalline phases (e.g., Li10GeP2S12 (LGPS), Li6PS5Cl (LPSCl),
Li9.54Si1.74P1.44S11.7Cl0.3).[44–46] However, sulﬁde-SSEs are sensi-
tive to air and moisture, which currently limit their practical
applications. Their narrow electrochemical window also makes
them prone to decomposition at both sulfur cathode and lithium
anode, leading to failure mechanisms distinct from SPEs-based
systems.[47]

potential advantage in safety, the technology is still immature
and progress is slow (Figure 1b). According to the timeline

2. The Trade-Oﬀbetween the Shuttle Eﬀect and
Redox Kinetics
Solid-state LSBs exhibit a variety of electrochemical properties de-
pending on the state of LiPSs in SSEs. SPEs-based LSBs with sol-
uble LiPSs typically exhibit a characteristic discharge–charge pro-
ﬁle featuring two voltage plateaus, indicative of a solid–liquid–
solid reaction, similar to LSBs using ether-based liquid elec-
trolytes (Figure 3a).[49] One of the primary challenges impeding
the practical application of liquid-state LSBs is the shuttle eﬀect
of LiPSs, which can cause rapid capacity fade and corrosion of
the lithium anode. Conversely, the dissolution of LiPSs can be
advantageous for enhancing redox kinetics. Given the low elec-
tronic/ionic conductivity of elemental sulfur (S8), the dissolution
of LiPSs contributes to the redistribution of electrode and fa-
cilitates suﬃcient mass transfer between sulfur and electrolyte,
thereby reducing the activation energy (Ea) for the S8-to-Li2S con-
version (Figure 3b).[50,51] However, in SPEs-based LSBs, consid-
ering the LiPSs diﬀusion rate may not match the solvation rate,
LiPSs may accumulate at the electrode/SPEs interface and be-
come trapped within SPEs, leading to the formation and accu-
mulation of inactive dead LiPSs and hindering kinetics of charge
carrier transport.[52]
In contrast, LSBs with inorganic SSEs, which are incapable
of dissolving LiPSs, adhere to a solid–solid reaction pathway
characterized by a single voltage plateau (Figure 3c),[49,53] a fea-
ture also observed in liquid carbonate-based LSBs. The shuttle-
free electrochemistry eliminates the crosstalk between the sul-
fur cathode and lithium anode. However, the large bandgap
of S8 and Li2S, along with high Gibbs free energy change
for the S8-to-Li2S reaction, signiﬁcantly raises the kinetic bar-
rier and diminishes the electrochemical reversibility.[54] Com-
pared to SPEs-based and liquid-state LSBs, the electrochemical
mechanism of sulﬁde-SSEs-based LSBs remains elusive, often
likened to a “black box”, due to the propensity for electrochem-
ical decomposition of sulﬁde-SSEs and potential elemental sul-
fur exchange between active materials and SSEs.[55] The elec-
trochemical irreversibility of both sulfur and SSEs may result
in dead Li2S and thiophosphates-rich cathode–electrolyte inter-
phase (CEI) that block charge carrier transport and result in kinet-
ics failure.[56,57] The studies have reported the detection of solid-
state LiPSs (such as Li2S4) as the intermediate products in the dis-
charge process at temperatures exceeding 60 °C, which promote

The performance of solid-state LSBs fundamentally hinges
on the delicate balance and compromise between cyclic stabil-
ity and sulfur utilization. This dynamic can be metaphorically
represented by the Tai Chi diagram (Figure 3d), where SPEs-
based LSBs, characterized by fast conversion mediated by LiPSs
but susceptible to the shuttle eﬀect, embody the Yin aspect; con-
versely, inorganic SSEs-based LSBs, which are shuttle-free but
feature slow solid–solid conversion and kinetically dead sulfur,
represent the Yang aspect. Striking a balance between the shut-
tle eﬀect and redox kinetics is both challenging and essential for
achieving high cyclic stability and sulfur utilization with a spe-
ciﬁc type of SSEs. In this section, we critically discuss the current
strategies and potential future directions in inhibiting the shuttle
eﬀect and kinetically dead sulfur in solid-state LSBs.
2.1. Shuttle Eﬀect in Solid-State LSBs
Among various SPE systems, poly(ethylene oxide) (PEO)-based
SPEs have been extensively investigated in solid-state LSBs ow-
ing to their good interfacial compatibility with electrodes and rel-
atively high Li+ conductivity. However, it has a molecular struc-
ture and LiPSs shuttle behavior like that of polyether solvents.
Various characterization techniques have been used to reveal the
shuttle eﬀect in PEO-based LSBs. In situ, optical microscopy and
atomic force microscopy observe the phenomenon of polysulﬁde
etching and reshaping behavior on lithium anode at a working
temperature above 55 °C.[60] To further establish the relationship
between this phenomenon and electrochemical performance, a
three-electrode cell was designed to investigate the electrochem-
ical process occurring on each electrode of PEO-based LSBs
(Figure 4a,b).[61] The working (sulfur) electrode shows stable pe-
riodic voltage proﬁles over 10 cycles, indicating well-functioned
reversible sulfur redox. Conversely, on the counter (lithium) elec-
trode side, the overpotential exhibited a signiﬁcant increase and
severe ﬂuctuations with increasing cycles. It can observe substan-
tial voids and signiﬁcant detachment of lithium anode from the
PEO. These results indicate that dissolved LiPSs diﬀuse to the an-
ode, leading to the formation of an in-homogeneously passivated
interface, distinct from those in conventional liquid-state LSBs.
The morphological changes in the anode induce the formation of
lithium dendrites, which in turn cause a soft short-circuit of the
cell.
Currently, most research is dedicated to limiting the shuttle
of LiPSs to improve the cyclic stability of SPEs-based LSBs. The
primary strategy involves adjusting the functional group of poly-
mers or introducing solid-state inorganic ﬁllers to adsorb LiPSs
and inhibit its diﬀusion toward the anode (Figure 4c). It has
been reported that polymers with high-spatial-density electron-
donating groups, such as carbonyl groups, ester groups, and
amide groups eﬀectively interact with LiPSs by forming lithium
bonds.[62–64] However, these strong bindings correspond to a poor
Li+ conduction ability, which is the reason that they are generally
studied as polymer skeletons for gel polymer electrolytes. SPEs
with single polymer components are diﬃcult to simultaneously
possess high ionic conductivity and suppress shuttle eﬀects. To
address this issue, the cross-linking structure was developed to
eﬀectively reduce the crystallinity of SPEs, thereby improving the
ionic conductivity and simultaneously adsorbing LiPSs.[65–69] For
example, a PEO-PAN-LiTFSI copolymer membrane electrolyte
has been shown to enhance the room-temperature ionic conduc-
tivity of PEO-LiTFSI by one order (1.63 × 10−5 S cm−1).[69] The

Another strategy to inhibit the shuttle eﬀect is to limit the
reactivity between LiPSs and lithium anode by protecting the
lithium surface. In liquid-state LSBs, lithium nitrate (LiNO3) is
currently an indispensable electrolyte additive for adjusting the
solvation structure of LiPSs and reconstructing SEI components,
thus achieving a high Coulombic eﬃciency.[81–83] However, pos-
sibly due to the suboptimal solid–solid interface, poor diﬀusion
kinetics, and mechanical properties of SPEs, LiNO3 does not play
a crucial role as in liquid electrolytes. Therefore, regulating elec-
trolyte composition and SEI properties is a topic worth explor-
ing further for protecting lithium anode from the shuttle eﬀect
and pursuing long-life cycling (Figure 4d). In terms of lithium
salt, lithium bis(ﬂuorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) has been reported
to replace lithium bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)imide LiTFSI in
PEO-based LSBs while maintaining stable Coulombic eﬃciency
≈100% within 50 cycles.[84] This stability is attributed to the in-
organic ─SO2F group in FSI−instead of the organic ─SO2CF3
group in TFSI−, which promotes the formation of a robust LiF-
rich SEI. However, lower initial discharge capacity (800 mAh g−1
at 0.1 C and 70 °C) is caused by the poor molten-state wet-
tability of ─SO2F and an irreversible reaction with LiPSs. In
this regard, an unsymmetrical ﬂuorinated salt, lithium (ﬂuoro-
sulfonyl) (triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl) imide (LiFTFSI), featuring
both FSI−and TFSI−functionalities, has been synthesized.[85]
The SEI layer in LiFTFSI-based electrolyte achieves an optimum
organic–inorganic balance, endowing it with high mechanical
stability and Li+ conductivity. Compared to FSI−, the asymmet-
rical structure of the intermediate produced by the reaction of
FTFSI−with LiPSs improves electrochemical reversibility. As a
result, a high reversible capacity of 1394 mAh g−1 at 0.05 C and
70 °C has been achieved, once again demonstrating the critical
importance of anions. Besides, LiF as a functional SEI compo-
nent, Li3N,[86] LiH,[87] LiI,[88] LixPySz,[89] etc., derived from anions
or electrolyte additives, serve as ionic conductive components in
SEI, contributing to good rate capability. Although current re-
ports emphasize that anion derivatives dominate the SEI compo-
sition, further research is needed on how they aﬀect the role of
polysulﬁdes in SEI formation, including whether they may pref-
erentially form SEI over polysulﬁdes or combine/react with poly-
sulﬁdes to alter the composition and properties of SEI. The eﬀect
of polysulﬁde anions diﬀusion at the lithium anode/SPEs inter-
face on space charge-induced lithium dendrites also requires a
deeper understand as well.
In view of the hostless nature of lithium anode, a large vol-
ume expansion occurs during the lithium plating process. Such
large volume changes fracture the SEI with low modulus and ex-
pose fresh lithium to the SPE, make it diﬃcult to suppress the
invasion of polysulﬁde anions and lithium dendrites during long-
term cycling.[90] To address these issues, the composite SSE archi-
tecture have been developed to suppress the harmful eﬀects of
polysulﬁde shuttling. The high ionic conductivity and mechan-
ical properties of inorganic ceramic electrolytes (e.g., LLTO,[91]
LLZO,[92] and LATP[93,94]) were utilized as interlayers or physical
barriers to isolate LiPSs from lithium anode (Figure 4e). For ex-
ample, an asymmetric LLTO framework with a porous layer and
dense layer was fabricated, followed by PEO impregnation.[91]
The porous layer acts as a rapid Li+ transfer pathway, and PEO
in it ensures the S8-LiPSs-Li2S reaction pathway. Meanwhile, the
dense layer of asymmetric LLTO framework signiﬁcantly im-
proves the compression strength of the composite electrolyte and
serves as a physical barrier. A PEO/atomic layer deposition-coated
LATP/PEO (ALD-PLP) SSE with a sandwich structure was de-
veloped by Sun et al., in which the ALD-derived Al2O3 coating
eﬀectively eliminated the reduction of LATP by LiPSs.[93] The
ALD-PLP interlayer eﬀectively suppresses LiPSs shuttling and
overcharge of solid-state LSBs, showing stable Coulombic eﬃ-
ciency of ≈100% within 100 cycles at 0.1 C. These SSEs combine
the advantages of oxide SSEs and SPEs. To further reduce the
weight or thickness of the multi-layer architecture, a 10 μm-thick
solid-state laminar electrolyte was fabricated by ﬁltering vermi-
culite nanosheets, followed by intercalation of PEO-LiTFSI into
the interlayer of the nanosheets through a swelling and ﬁltration
method,[95] in which the laminar physical blocker eﬀectively in-
hibits the shuttle eﬀect of LiPSs.
2.2. Production and Elimination of Kinetically Dead Sulfur
2.2.1. Kinetically Dead Sulfur in LiPSs-Mediated Systems
The solubility of LiPSs enables faster Li–S redox kinetics com-
pared to LiPSs-insoluble systems. In this case, most research
has focused on suppressing the shuttle eﬀect and enhancing
the ionic conductivity of SPEs. However, the sulfur utilization
and capacity retention of existing SPEs-based LSBs still lag far
behind those of liquid-state LSBs (Table 1). Unlike liquid elec-
trolytes, ion transport in SPEs occurs through slow segmental
motion of ﬂexible polymer chains, which results in a large mean
free path for ion hopping. This not only aﬀects Li+ but also im-
poses diﬀusion limitations on LiPSs. Through operando spec-
troscopy, the capacity decay of polymerized 1,3-dioxolane-based
LSBs was conﬁrmed to be associated with sluggish diﬀusion
features of LiPSs, leading to electrochemical irreversible LiPSs,
also known as “dead LiPSs”.[52] Operando UV–vis spectroscopy
visualizes the presence of a portion of dead LiPSs exist in the
bulk electrolyte, which cannot shuttle back to participate in the

(Figure 5d). Although there is a lack of systematic research on the
Li2S deposition behavior in SPEs and the dynamic changes at the
cathode interface, it can be anticipated that lateral Li2S formation
will occur in ethers with longer chains, such as PEO. Drawing
inspiration from liquid systems, the dissociation ability of PEO

high Gutmann donor numbers (DN), which induced 3D growth

host motivates 3D spheroidal-like Li2S, delays the electrode passi-
vation, and enables the PEO-based LSBs with a higher capacity of
876 mAh g−1 at 0.2 C and 60 °C, retaining 80.2% after 350 cycles.
Beyond electrolyte engineering to improve the conversion ef-
ﬁciency of LiPSs to Li2S, another method to improving redox ki-
netics is the design of catalytic material, which has been widely
validated in liquid-state LSBs.[102] Recently, single-atom catalysts
and transition metal oxide have also been used in SPEs-based
LSBs.[52,103] However, in heterogeneous catalysis, catalysts are
prone to poisoning and passivation by reactants, diminishes
their catalytic eﬃciency.[104,105] Molecular catalysis is an improved
strategy to prevent catalyst deactivation.[106] A soluble quinone-
based redox mediator was developed by Cui et al. to facilitate
the oxidation kinetics of solid-state Li2S cathode.[107] During the
charge process, the oxidized form of the redox mediator (RMox)
chemically oxidizes Li2S to LiPSs, while being reduced to the
reduced form of redox mediator (RMred) (step I, chemical reac-
tion). RMred is then kinetic-favorably oxidized back to the initial
RMox state by giving electrons to current collectors (step II, elec-
trochemical reaction). As a result, the activation barrier for the
Li2S cathode is reduced from 3.4 to 2.4 V for PEO-based LSBs. It
follows that, in addition to the conversion of LiPSs, the oxidation
kinetics of Li2S are also sluggish and merit further investigation.
It is also worth noting that compared to homogeneous catalysis
in liquid systems, the rate constant of heterogeneous molecular
catalysis in SPEs may be inﬂuenced by inherent diﬀusion limita-
tions present in SPEs.
In addition to modifying the electrolyte composition and uti-
lizing external catalysts, regulating the active material itself is
expected to more directly improve reaction kinetics. Organosul-
fur polymers, as a branch of the sulfur cathode family, have sig-
niﬁcantly improved the cycling stability of liquid-state LSBs.[108]
The chemical interaction between polymer skeleton and sulfur
can inhibit the dissolution of LiPSs, and shortening the sulfur
chain can achieve solid–solid conversion.[109,110] Sulfurized poly-
acrylonitrile (SPAN), as a classic shuttle-free cathode, showed
good stability over 400 cycles in PEO, with further electrolyte im-
provement extending the lifespan to 700 cycles.[109] Although the
capacity is limited due to a low sulfur content (40–50%), the long
lifespan compensates for the primary disadvantage of S8 cath-
odes in SPEs. Moreover, the interface between the SPE and the
organosulfur polymer can be optimized by customized polar–
polar interaction to achieve faster charge carrier transport as well,
which will be discussed in Section 4.
From energy barriers of multi-step sulfur conversion, the for-
mation of dead sulfur in SPEs-based LSBs can be clariﬁed. As il-
lustrated in Figure 5f, it can be inferred that the diﬀusion barrier
in step II is larger than the solvation barrier in step I and lateral
Li2S deposition in step III may cause low LiPSs-to-Li2S conver-
sion eﬃciency, which exacerbates the retention of dead LiPSs in
the electrolyte. Moreover, the oxidation of Li2S encounters a sig-
niﬁcant barrier, increasing the risks of forming dead Li2S and
leading to kinetic degradation. To achieve high areal capacity and
cyclic stability for SPEs-based sulfur cathode, it is essential to
utilize the LiPSs-mediated fast solid–liquid–solid reaction while
suppressing the generation of dead sulfur by advanced electrolyte
engineering, electrocatalysis, and novel active materials.

2.2.2. Kinetically Dead Sulfur in LiPSs-Free Systems
The production and shuttle eﬀect of LiPSs are inhibited in solid-
state LSBs with inorganic SSEs. Among them, sulﬁde-SSEs (in-
cluding LGPS, Li7P3S11, Li3PS4, LPSCl, Li9.54Si1.74P1.44S11.7Cl0.3,
etc.) are the most promising ones for solid-state LSBs due to
their high ionic conductivity, excellent compatibility with sulfur
cathodes, and good processability.[115,116] However, the solid–solid
conversion reaction between S8 and Li2S requires higher activa-
tion energy compared to LiPSs-mediated conversion, resulting
in solid dead sulfur during the electrochemical process. X-ray
absorption spectroscopy (XAS) and time-of-ﬂight secondary ion
mass spectrometry were used to probe the ﬁnal discharge prod-
ucts of LGPS-based LSBs, which consist of Li2S and Li2S2, sug-
gesting an incomplete conversion.[54] When the discharge poten-
tial decreases to −0.2 V (Li-In/Li+), Li2S in the discharge product
causes poor electrochemical reversibility of only 50% (Figure 7a).
To inhibit dead Li2S, a lower cutoﬀvoltage of 0.6 V (Li-In/Li+)
was applied to induce a Li2S2-dominant discharge product, im-
proving electrochemical reversibility of 82% and ensuring stable,
long-term cycling performance for solid-state LSBs.
To achieve better electrochemical reversibility and inhibit dead
Li2S, additional catalytic mediators were used to accelerate solid–
solid sulfur conversion. Since soluble LiPSs are not involved, sul-
fur agglomeration is prone to occur during the cycling process,
hindering the further reaction of the larger sulfur clusters.[117]
It is essential to establish strong chemical bonding between the
catalyst and S8/Li2S. Although the conventional “catalyst on car-
bon” architecture has been widely designed to simultaneously
pursue catalytic activity and electronic conductivity in liquid-state
LSBs, the narrow electrochemical window of sulﬁde-SSEs accel-
erates the oxidative and reductive decomposition when they are
exposed to electronic conductors. As shown in Figure 7b, the
thermodynamic electrochemical stability window of LPSCl (1.7–
2.1 V vs Li/Li+) is within the voltage window of LSBs.[118] By
conducting stepwise cyclic voltammetry tests, the electrochemi-
cal behavior of LPSCl/carbon composites was revealed.[119] The
results indicate that below ≈1.2 V (vs Li/Li+, the same here-
inafter), the reductive decomposition of LPSCl occurs to pro-
duce LiCl, Li2S, and LixP. Above ≈2.6 V, the oxidative decomposi-
tion of LPSCl occurs and makes thiophosphate units be linked
(oligomerized) together by releasing sulfur or polysulﬁdes.[120]

low potentials, the potential range causing oxidative electrolyte
degradation largely overlaps with the voltage window of solid-
state LSBs, making interphase formation is unavoidable. As a
result, the redox-active decomposition products contribute to im-
proved apparent capacity, but increase the risk of interfacial in-
stability and the formation of irreversible dead sulfur species,
which can impede charge carrier transport. To suppress interfa-

LPSCl, a counterintuitive concept of using non-conductive polar

3. Production and Elimination of Interfacial Dead
Sulfur in Solid-State LSBs
3.1. Production of Interfacial Dead Sulfur
Apart from issues related to conversion reactions, the sluggish
charge carrier transport, including electrons and ions within the
sulfur cathode, can produce interfacial dead sulfur and aﬀect the
performance of solid-state LSBs as well. In an ideal electrode,
both the electronic transport between the external circuit and
the active material via a current collector and the ionic trans-
port between the bulk electrolyte and the active material, are as-
sumed to be minimal, which can be imagined as the situation
of ultra-thin electrodes in liquid batteries (Figure 9a). The intrin-
sic coupled electron/ion conduction properties within the active
material (Rin
e /Rin
ion) are believed to primarily determine the over-
all transport-induced resistivity, which aﬀects the rate capabil-
ity and energy eﬃciency of the electrode. However, in practical
solid-state LSBs, deviations from this ideal situation can be large,
nontrivial, and sometimes lead to fatal capacity fading and bat-
tery failure (Figure 9b).[135] Diﬀerent from liquid electrolytes with
good wettability and ﬂuidity, ionic transport in solid-state sulfur
cathode faces various obstacles, such as conductive carbon, active
materials, binders, and solid–solid contact among SSEs particles,
resulting in unsynchronized Li+/e−transport.[136] Furthermore,
interface properties can dynamically change and become strik-
ingly fragile with the cycling of solid-state LSBs. The volume ex-
pansion and contraction of S8/Li2S as high as 79% lead to inter-
facial incompatibility due to the formation of a space charge layer
at the interface between conductive agents and active materials.
The formation of insulating interphase caused by SSEs decom-
position further causes large extrinsic resistances (Rex
e /Rex
ion). In
addition, the reaction-interface interplay will cause severe accu-
mulation of dead sulfur. The heterogeneity of redox reactions pro-
motes the formation of kinetically dead sulfur species (e.g., Li2S)
at both the interface and within sulfur particles, which further de-
teriorates Li+/e−transport and exacerbates interfacial dead sulfur
deposition. Then the accumulation of interfacial dead sulfur may
further slowdown redox reactions, which will be greatly magni-
ﬁed under practical conditions of high sulfur loading, leading to

3.2. Elimination of Interfacial Dead Sulfur from Interface
Optimization to Integrated Design
3.2.1. Interface Optimization
In inorganic SSEs-based LSBs, the ﬁrst-order interface is be-
tween sulfur species and conductive agents, while the second-
order interface is among the separated solid-state conductive par-
ticles. For the ﬁrst-order interface, the interface between sulfur
and electronic additives can easily be solved using a melting
method at 155 °C[137] or a mechano-fusion process under high
shear and compression forces.[138] In this case, enhancing ionic
transfer from SSEs to sulfur is crucial, especially given the lim-
ited physical contact. An eﬀective strategy involves inserting ionic
conductive substances into the gaps between SSEs and sulfur
as transition layers, transforming the physical “point-to-point”
contact into a more robust chemical “point-to-face” or “face-to-
face” contact. By introducing LiTFSI into the S/KB/LPSCl cath-
ode, an in situ formed LiF/Li3N interphase during cycling re-
duces the Li+ migration barrier compared to LPSCl, enabling
high Li+ transfer eﬃciency and interfacial stability.[139] To achieve
chemical interaction between sulfur and SSEs, Sun et al. mixed
sulfur and LPSCl in an isopropyl acetate solvent, which has a
low polarity index of 4.2 and a low boiling point of 89 °C.[140]
This interfacial chemical reaction results in a thin and tight
interface composed of inorganic polysulﬁde-intermediate com-
pounds (Li3PS4+n, where n ≥0) between sulfur and LPSCl
(Figure 10a). The Li+-conducting species greatly reduces the
space charge layer and contributes to a compact solid-state sulfur
cathode with highly reduced internal pores (Figure 10b,c). The fa-
cilitated interfacial Li+ transport by functional interphase endows
the solid-state LSB with a high areal capacity (5.1 mAh cm−2) and
a promising lifetime (250 cycles). The pre-coating sulfur particles
with nano-scale ion-conducting layer by atomic layer deposition
(ALD) technology or other chemical methods can enhance Li+
transport between the active material and SSEs, maintaining ef-
fective contact during cycling.[141,142] Xie et al. pre-coated SPAN
with a 10 nm thin layer of Li7P3S11 through an in situ liquid-phase
method. Beneﬁting for the improved ionic transport at the inter-
face, solid-state LSBs exhibit excellent rate performance with a
reversible capacity of 668 mAh g−1 at 1 C.[142]
The interface between sulfur and SSEs can be improved by reg-
ulating their intrinsic properties or morphology as well. Speciﬁ-
cally, the volumetric content and particle size of SSEs determines
the suﬃciency of Li+ pathways. Considering the high density of
inorganic SSEs (typically > 2 g cm−3), a low volume ratio of SSEs
in cathode gives rise to insuﬃcient Li+ pathways and suboptimal
sulfur utilization. In a notable case, an argyrodite glass-ceramic
SSE, Li3PS4-2LiBH4 (LPB) with a low density (1.491 g cm−3),
high ionic conductivity (6 mS cm−1 at 25 °C), and small pri-
mary particle size (≈500 nm) was synthesized.[143] The density of
LPB is lower than other inorganic SSEs and comparable to con-
ventional liquid electrolytes, facilitating suﬃcient Li+ pathways
(Figure 10d,e). As a result, solid-state LSBs with high sulfur con-
tent (60 wt.%) and high areal loading (2.57 mg cm−2) exhibit high
sulfur utilization (1005 mAh g−1 at 0.5 C) by eﬀectively reduce
interfacial dead sulfur. Besides the morphology of SSE particles,
with conductive carbon as one of the barriers to Li+ transport,
their structural design must be diﬀerent from that of liquid-state
LSBs. Attributed to the excellent ﬂowability and the endowed cap-
illary eﬀects, liquid electrolytes have high accessibility to most
sulfur within the porous carbon and even the pores buried deep
inside the carbon. In contrast, SSEs in LSBs can only contact sul-
fur located at the outside surface of porous carbon, and the sulfur
conﬁned deep in the pores inside the carbon is generally inac-
cessible and ionically insulated. In this case, an N-doped carbon
ﬁber with micropores only at the surface was designed, featuring
an ultrahigh surface area of 1519 m2 g−1, created through elec-
trospinning and the KOH activation process.[144] As the porous
layer is only on the surface, the sulfur hosted in the pores can
eﬀectively contact LPSCl particles; meanwhile, the dense core
provides excellent electrical conductivity, thus dramatically im-
proves the reaction kinetics of solid-state LSBs and boosts sulfur
utilization.
SPEs has good ﬂexibility and mobility above melting tran-
sition temperature, thus exhibit better interface contact com-
pared to inorganic SSEs. However, the non-polar nature of sul-
fur and its host materials poses compatibility challenges with
the polar SPEs. To address this, organosulfur polymers, which
can integrate polar organic functional groups with active sul-
fur, are considered a promising candidate for forming a ro-
bust cathode–electrolyte interface based on the “like compatible
like” principle. Hu et al. developed a vinyl-capped hyperbranched
polymer network (PEI-GMA) that serves as a backbone struc-
ture for both the organosulfur polymer cathode and gel poly-
mer electrolytes.[145] The presence of hydroxyl groups (─OH)
and nitrogen-containing functional groups (─NH─, ─NH2) fa-
cilitates strong hydrogen bonding between PEI-GMA gel poly-
mer electrolyte and the organosulfur polymer cathode (G/PEI-
GMA@S) (Figure 10f). The customized polar–polar interaction
ensures a robust cathode–electrolyte interface and forms a con-
tinuous ionic pathway. This research is anticipated to inspire fur-
ther studies on organosulfur in SPEs and the integrated design
of cathode and SPEs. It is highly promising to integrate elec-
tronic/ionic conductive units into organosulfur polymers based
on backbone/side-chain structural design.

3.3. Elimination of Interfacial Dead Sulfur Produced During
Cycling
To ensure the long-term stability and eﬃcient performance of
solid-state LSBs, it is imperative to develop sulfur cathodes with
robust structure resisting the volume change and the interfa-
cial dead sulfur produced during discharge–charge cycles. Con-
ductive nanoporous carbon materials, such as carbon nanotube
and graphene with excellent mechanical toughness/ﬂexibility,
has been widely used as sulfur hosts, allowing for reversible
shape compression/recovery.[154,155] However, it is worth noting
that pre-immersing sulfur into nanopores may hinder the ionic
transport pathways, leading to an imbalanced supply of e−and
Li+ at triple-phase interfaces, thus restricting sulfur conversion
processes. Instead of relying solely on porous carbon materials,
integrating polymer components into solid-state LSBs can alle-
viate the electrochemical-mechanical invalid while ensure sym-
metric charge carrier transport. For example, a highly ﬂexible
thermoplastic elastomer (ethylene vinyl acetate, EVA), character-
ized by excellent stretchability and elasticity, was used to fabri-
cate a solid-state sulfur cathode.[156] EVA with a lower melting
point of 93.71 °C than PVDF (162.21 °C) was integrated by a dry-
processed ball milling method to in situ form a 3D cross-linked
network inside the composite sulfur cathode (Figure 12a), and
this method eﬀectively circumvent the deleterious eﬀects of or-
ganic solvents on sulﬁde-SSEs. In situ stack pressure monitor
of solid-state cells reveals that EVA eﬀectively mitigates internal
stress of solid-state sulfur cathode by 33.7%. To further integrate
mechanical buﬀering components with continuous electronic
conduction pathways, a ﬂexible conducting polymer, polypyrrole,
was coated on a hollow N-doped carbon nanotube through a
liquid-phase polymerization process and introduced into sulﬁde-
SSEs-based sulfur cathodes.[157] The ﬂexible buﬀer and integral
structure ensure the solid-state LSB with an areal capacity of
6.07 mAh cm−2 under a sulfur loading of 4.5 mg cm−2, main-
taining stability over 300 cycles at 0.2 C, further reﬂecting the
signiﬁcant importance of a stable and continuous charge carrier
transport network for practical solid-state LSBs.
Besides the additional mechanical buﬀer that potentially in-
creases the mass ratio of inactive materials, active materials can
also self-adapt to the interface change due to their unique struc-
ture or physical properties. For example, the ﬂexible polymer
skeleton of organosulfur can buﬀer the stress generated by the
volume expansion of sulfur.[158] Moreover, the melting point of
S@I (S9.3I), as mentioned earlier, is reduced to ≈65 °C compared
to 113 °C for S8, resulting in an ideal active material/SSEs inter-
face by easily periodical remelting of the cathode (Figure 12b).[59]
By periodically heat treating the S@I-based solid-state LSBs ev-
ery 50 cycles, a capacity of 701 mAh g−1 (87% retention) and an
average Coulombic eﬃciency of ≈99.8% are maintained after 400
cycles, outperforming other reported solid-state LSBs. Besides
the property of low melting point, the creep behavior of cath-
ode material can also be utilized to adapt to rigid inorganic SSEs.
Materials with an elevated homologous temperature (TH > 0.3),
such as alloy-type electrode materials (Se,[159] In,[160] Sn,[161] etc.),
can change shape via self-diﬀusion under shear stress, trigger-
ing a polycrystalline solid to behave like a viscous ﬂuid at the
macroscopic level. As a chalcogen element, the Se well balances
comprehensive merits, including the high theoretical volumet-
ric capacity (3253 mAh cm−3) and much higher electronic con-
ductivity (10−3 S cm−1) compared to S (5 × 10−28 S cm−1), in-
dicating its promise as an active material.[162] In this regard,
Suo et al. designed an all-solid-state Se cathode by employ-
ing electrochemical-active and mixed ionic/electronic conductive
Mo6Se8 (DLi
+: 10−8–10−10 cm2 s−1; 𝜎e
−: 32.4–154.2 S cm−1) as
a rigid framework.[163] The porosity in the cathode contracted
throughout the discharge/charge process due to Se creep, form-
ing improved contact and leasing stress. Impressively, the creep-
type Se/Mo6Se8 cathode without additional conductive agent dis-
plays a high volumetric capacity of 1183 mAh cm−3 correspond-
ing to an energy density of 2460 Wh L−1 at the cathode level and a
remarkable long-term cycling for 3000 cycles at 0.5 C and 70 °C.
From the perspective of battery manufacturing, the ≈80% vol-
ume expansion of sulfur poses challenges that typically necessi-
tate operation under high stack pressures (generally >30 MPa)
to ensure close contact and minimize interface resistance
(Table 2).[164] Expensive and heavy ﬁxtures are often required to
hold the test batteries, which is impractical and diminishes the
battery’s eﬀective energy density. Therefore, developing solid-
state LSBs capable of eﬃcient operation under lower pressures
is crucial for advancing the battery industry. By conducting a se-
ries of electrochemical tests under varying stack pressures, Qu
et al. decoupled contact losses and diﬀusion losses in the sulfur
cathode during diﬀerent electrochemical stages, revealing that
contact losses primarily occur during charging, while diﬀusion
kinetics are more sensitive to reduced stack pressure during dis-
charging (Figure 12c).[165] To mitigate this issue, metallic indium
(In) was incorporated into the cathode, promoting uniform re-

4. Production and Elimination of Dead Lithium in
Solid-State LSBs
Lithium metal is regarded as an ideal anode material for high-
energy-density batteries due to its highest speciﬁc capacity
(3860 mAh g−1) and lowest chemical potential (−3.04 V vs stan-
dard hydrogen electrode). Although LSBs using non-ﬂammable
SSEs are considered safer than liquid electrolytes, battery fail-
ure can still occur due to the unstable lithium chemistry from
prospects of reaction and interface, including uncontrollable
lithium dendrites that cause “physical dead lithium” and SSEs
decomposition on lithium anode that cause “chemical dead
lithium”. The limited current critical density and short lifespan
remain signiﬁcant bottlenecks hindering the commercialization
of solid-state LSBs.[166]
4.1. Dead Lithium Associated with Lithium Dendrites
The growth of lithium dendrites, driven by thermodynamics and
kinetics, is almost unavoidable and poses the biggest obstacle to
the commercialization of lithium metal batteries. Diﬀerent from
liquid batteries with extra separators, SSEs themselves serve dual
roles as both electrolytes and separators in solid-state batteries.
In this case, the mechanical properties of SSEs are important to
resist lithium dendrites. The kinetic model for dendrite propaga-
tion proposed by Monroe and Newman suggests that SSEs with a
shear modulus at least twice that of lithium metal can eﬀectively
suppress the protrusion of lithium dendrites.[167] Since the mea-
sured shear modulus of lithium metal is ≈3 GPa, and the stress
induced by lithium deposition can easily reach the GPa level,
dendrite penetration is expected given the low Young’s modu-
lus of SPEs (less than 100 MPa for PEO).[168] In situ optical mi-
croscopy was used to detect dendritic lithium deposition in PEO
in a symmetrical Li | PEO | Li cell that supports this expectation
(Figure 14a).[169]

properties, variations of the cell potential and the ionic concen-

role in dendrite growth. Local current inhomogeneities lead to

microstructures, such as current collector edges or hot spots,
due to non-uniform interface contact between lithium metal and
SSEs.[170,171] The time for dendrite formation can be depicted by

4.2. Dead Lithium Associated with Interface Properties
In addition to lithium dendrites, the interfacial instability on the
lithium anode side can form a large amount of chemical dead
lithium as well, leading to the pulverization of SSEs and aﬀect-
ing the cycling life of solid-state batteries. The interfacial insta-
bility originates from the coupled electrochemistry-mechanics.
Theoretical calculations display that most fast Li+ conductors
have reduction limits higher in potential than that of metal-
lic lithium, indicating that they are thermodynamically unstable
(Figure 15a).[191] Only binary ionic lithium compounds are sta-
ble at 0 V versus Li/Li+ since their anions are in the most re-
duced states (Figure 15b).[191,192] Non-metal elements such as S,
P, N, O, and halides in SSEs, including Li7P3S11,[193] Li3PS4,[194]
LPSCl,[195] LiPON,[196] anti-perovskite Li3OCl[197] are ﬁnally re-
duced to Li2S, Li3P, Li3N, Li2O and lithium halides, respectively.
These substances form a stable SEI layer that is conductive for
Li+ but insulating for e−, preventing further reaction between
lithium and the SSEs (Figure 15c). In contrast, the elements Ge
and Ti in LLTO, LATP, LAGP, and LGPS are reduced to the cor-
responding metals or lithium alloys (e.g., Ge or Li–Ge alloy for
LGPS).[192,198] The mixed ionic/electronic conductivity of these
interphase layers leads to ongoing interfacial reactions within
SSEs, causing the continuous thickening of unstable interphase
layers (Figure 15d). The active lithium loss signiﬁcantly through
the continuous side reaction, and more seriously, the injection of
electrons into SSEs promotes self-discharge or short-circuiting of
batteries.[199] Additionally, the interphase growth tends to be non-
uniform at high current density, leading to localized stress that
fractures SSEs. Tippens et al. monitored the fracture of LAGP

4.3. Construction of Stable Lithium Chemistry
4.3.1. Inhibition of Lithium Dendrites in SPEs-Based LSBs
Considering that poor mechanical strength is the primary reason
for SPEs being invaded by lithium dendrites, numerous studies
focused on enhancing the mechanical strength of SPEs through
strategies such as incorporating inorganic ﬁllers, utilizing com-
posite polymers, and employing multilayered structures. A poly-
mer of intrinsic microporosity (PIM) was introduced as an or-
ganic framework to comprehensively enhance the performance
of PEO-based LSBs.[201] The unique structure of PIM-1 enhances
the mechanical strength over the PEO electrolyte by one order,
thereby improving its stability toward the lithium anode. More-
over, the electrophilic 1, 4-dicyanooxanthrene functional groups
present in PIM-1 eﬀectively trap LiPSs, mitigating the detri-
mental shuttle eﬀect, thus leading to an improved rate perfor-
mance and cyclic stability of PEO-based LSBs. As mentioned ear-
lier, the shuttle eﬀect of LiPSs in SPEs would result in an in-
homogeneously passivated interface and deteriorate the interface
contact between lithium and SPEs.[61] Improving the interface
contact between SPEs and lithium anode during cycling is re-
quired to inhibit lithium dendrites. In situ polymerization is an
eﬀective way to address the interface issues in SPEs. However, the
related reactions are limited to a few monomer types, and the de-
gree of polymerization is often uncontrollable, leading to gels or
semisolid ﬁnal products.[202] In this regard, the rational design of
self-healing SPEs aiming to repair the pores and defects that arise
at the lithium/SPEs interface during cycling is a more appropri-
ate strategy.[68,109,203] A poly(ether-urethane)-based SPE with self-
healing capability was designed by introducing dynamic cova-
lent disulﬁde bonds and hydrogen bonds (PTMG-HDI-BHDS)
(Figure 16a, b).[109] The interface contact of the dual-integrated
solid-state electrode/electrolyte structure based on self-healing
SPEs is much better than that of traditional laminated structures.
Consequently, the enhanced interface contact enables the solid-
state SPAN cathode to deliver signiﬁcantly enhanced cycle stabil-
ity (93% capacity retention after 700 cycles at 0.3 C and 30 °C).
Based on the behavior of lithium dendrites associated with free
anions concentration in SPEs, a single-ion conductor (LiPVAOB)
was introduced as the interlayer at the lithium/SPEs interface,
which not only inhibits the nonuniform anion aggregation by
repulsion interaction on the electrode surface but also induces
uniform Li+ deposition by the lithiophilic site of polyanion.[173]
Consequently, the assembled Li | Li cell delivers an ultra-long cy-
cle for over 5400 h at 0.25 mA cm−2. Combining the advantages
of inhibited anions migration and high modulus of SPEs, a solid-
state hybrid electrolyte was created using single-ion conducting
PS(LiTFSI) nanoparticles composed of a crosslinked polystyrene
core and a poly(4-styrenesulfonyltriﬂuoromethylsulfonyl) imide
corona, and Li+-transporting PEO nanoparticles.[175] By control-
ling the size and number ratio of the binary nanoparticles, an
ionic conductivity of 10−4 S cm−1 and a Li+ transference number
of 0.94 were achieved. When implemented in solid-state LSBs, an
initial discharge capacity of 1090 mAh g−1 at 0.05 C with a cycle
life of over 200 cycles was obtained.
The above research provides solutions from the perspective of
improving the mechanical properties of SPEs, limiting free anion
migration, and constructing self-healing interfaces to inhibit the
incubation and growth of lithium dendrites. However, for SPEs
with LiPSs shuttle, it is still necessary to consider the eﬀects of
polysulﬁde anions on space charge distribution at the interface
and lithium dendrite growth and to make further eﬀorts in con-
structing stable SEI resistant to LiPSs erosion.
4.3.2. Inhibition of Lithium Dendrites and Interfacial Instability in
Sulﬁde-SSEs-Based LSBs
To improve lithium electroplating/stripping and interfacial sta-
bility in sulﬁde-SSEs-based LSBs, researchers have made eﬀorts
from the perspective of anode materials. Lithium alloys with
a suitable operating potential are viable alternatives to metallic
lithium as an anode in solid-state batteries.[204] The degradation
of sulﬁde-SSEs and lithium dendrites can be greatly mitigated
by reduced reactivity, slightly higher potential, and lower Li+ dif-
fusion barrier of lithium alloy. Li–In alloy, with a stable voltage
plateau of ≈0.62 V versus Li+/Li, has been widely used as counter
and reference electrodes in lab-scale solid-state LSBs to evaluate
the performance of cathodes and SSEs. However, the low theo-
retical speciﬁc capacity (113 mAh g−1 for Li0.5In) and high cost of
indium ($200k ton−1) limit its further application.[205] Li–Si alloy
is popular for its high theoretical speciﬁc capacity (4200 mAh g−1
for Li4.4Si), low cost of silicon ($2k ton−1), and a relatively low
delithiation voltage plateau (≈0.35 V vs Li/Li+), making it a pos-
sibility for high voltage output anodes.[206] However, concerns
arise regarding the low electronic conductivity and severe volume
change of the Si anode during cycling.[207] The addition of carbon,
as in liquid batteries, is detrimental to the anode/SSEs interface
because carbon may promote the decomposition of sulﬁde-SSEs.
In a recent study, an all-electrochemical-active electrode was pre-
pared using μ-LixSi.[208] Unlike other electrodes, the carbon-free
electrode not only maximizes the electrode-level energy density
but also minimizes the electrolyte-related interfacial degradation.
When paired with a sulfur cathode (areal loading of 3 mg cm−2)
and LPSCl SSEs, the full cell shows impressive cycling perfor-
mance (76% capacity retention after 500 cycles at 0.3 C). To fur-
ther reduce the structural instability of anode caused by large
volume changes during lithiation/delithiation while minimizing
the manufacturing cost, Li–Al alloy emerges as another promis-

LSBs

5.2.3. Rcathode and msl Associated with Dead Sulfur
At the cathode side, the current reported Rcathode (≤60%) still has
a gap from the expected 70% for achieving 500 Wh kg−1. To re-
duce interfacial dead sulfur, a high content of conductive car-
bon (20–30%) with a high speciﬁc surface area is generally re-
quired to serve as sulfur hosts and electronic conductive path-
ways. Worse in solid-state LSBs than liquid systems, sulfur cath-
odes not only require electronically conductive carbon but also
require additional SSEs to provide ionic conduction pathways. In
sulﬁde-SSEs-based LSBs, unlike the strong sulfur–carbon con-
tact achieved by the melting method, the contact between sul-
fur and sulﬁde-SSEs is weaker and highly dependent on stack
pressure. This results in Li+ transport across the interface be-
coming the rate-limiting step. As illustrated in Figure 20a, stud-
ies achieving high Rcathode (>50%) eﬀectively reduced the inter-
facial dead sulfur through strategies such as converting physical
to chemical contact,[139] designing low-density SSEs,[142] and im-
plementing mixed ionic/electronic conductors.[149] These ﬁnd-
ings underscore the importance of increasing the Li+ accessibil-
ity of sulfur for achieving high Rcathode. In SPEs-based LSBs, im-
proved interface contact and reduced interfacial dead sulfur have
led to signiﬁcant Rcathode improvement, approaching the level of
liquid-state systems (Figure 20b). However, polarity mismatches
between SPEs and carbon/sulfur, combined with limited SPE
ﬂuidity below the melting transition temperature, hinder com-
plete inﬁltration, resulting in performance gaps relative to liquid
systems.
To achieve 500 Wh kg−1 solid-state LSB, a msl of up to
7 mg cm−2 is required, a value that can be widely achieved by
liquid-state LSBs with superior electrolyte inﬁltration and ionic
conductivity. However, in sulﬁde-SSEs-based LSBs, continuous
Li+ transport pathways are challenging to establish in the thick
cathode, and improving the interface between sulfur and SSEs is
insuﬃcient for rapid sulfur conversion. In this case, reducing the

6. Summary and Outlook
6.1. Strategies for Optimizing Reaction and Interface in
Solid-State LSBs
For current solid-state LSBs based on mature SPEs or inorganic
SSEs, the electrode reaction and interface characteristics deter-
mine the apparent electrochemical performance of the cell. In
this regard, we systematically summarize the fundamental issues
and current research directions for regulating these reactions and
interfaces in solid-state LSBs (Figure 21).
From the perspective of sulfur reactions, the essence of elec-
trochemical performance lies in the balance between cyclic sta-
bility and sulfur utilization, which is contingent upon the state
of LiPSs in diﬀerent SSEs. For SPEs-based LSBs with LiPSs-
mediated reactions, the solubility of LiPSs leads to an undesir-
able shuttle eﬀect. A conventional strategy is to chemically ad-
sorb LiPSs within SPEs to decelerate the diﬀusion. However, dif-
ferent from fast LiPS diﬀusion in liquid electrolytes, the slug-
gish diﬀusion of LiPSs in SPEs may result in dead LiPSs trapped
in SPEs and lateral deposition of Li2S, which can passivate the
electrode/electrolyte interface. The highly concentrated LiPSs
at the reaction area are akin to the lean electrolyte condition
in liquid-state LSBs. The rational design of catalytic mediators
and high donor number SPEs can promote the consumption of
dead LiPSs. Moreover, novel active materials, such as shuttle-free
organosulfur compounds, are expected to achieve both high sul-
fur utilization and long lifespan. For LiPSs-free LSBs based on
inorganic SSEs, the direct S8-to-Li2S conversion theoretically ex-
hibits large kinetic hysteresis but slow capacity decay. However,
the narrow electrochemical window of sulﬁde-SSEs and conduc-
tive carbon within solid-state sulfur cathode promote the de-
composition of sulﬁde-SSEs, leading to poor electrochemical re-
versibility and the formation of dead sulfur species that suppress
successive charge carrier transport. To prevent irreversible elec-
trochemical decomposition, the voltage window should be metic-
ulously controlled. Additionally, when using catalysts to boost
the conversion reaction, the electronic conductivity of catalysts
should be moderate, diﬀering from the requirements in liquid-
state LSBs.
From the perspective of lithium reactions, the growth of
lithium dendrites is accelerated by imperfect solid–solid interface
contact and poor mechanical properties of SSEs. For SPEs-based

6.2.1. Identiﬁcation of Sulfur Species and Inhibition of Dead Sulfur
From a reaction standpoint, dead sulfur poses a signiﬁcant bar-
rier to achieving high sulfur utilization in solid-state sulfur cath-
odes, whether for SPEs or inorganic SSE systems. However,
the identiﬁcation and elimination of dead sulfur have not re-
ceived widespread attention. In the case of SPEs-based LSBs,
even at a very low sulfur loading (0.5 mg cm−2), conventional
PEO electrolytes fail to support the long lifespan of solid-state
LSBs (Table 1). The capacity decay rate is comparable to that
of liquid-state LSBs, indicating that slow diﬀusion and shut-
tle lead to additional side eﬀects. It is necessary to investigate
the proportion of dead LiPSs in electrolytes during cycling and
to understand how their presence forms, speciﬁcally, the sol-
vation structure of LiPSs in SPEs further aﬀects the cathodic
kinetics and lithium dendrite growth. The inﬂuence of con-
centrated LiPSs at the electrode/SPEs interface on the deposi-
tion and decomposition kinetics of Li2S, as well as the physi-
cal model of Li2S nucleation in SPEs, which diﬀers from liq-
uid electrolytes, remains unclear. Novel polymer electrolyte for-
mulations are expected to be developed by drawing on research
on liquid-state LSBs under lean electrolyte conditions, such
as low-concentration lithium salts, high-DN solvents, encapsu-
lated solvation structures, etc., to promote the conversion of
LiPSs-to-Li2S and reduce the reactivity of LiPSs with metallic
lithium.
For inorganic SSEs-based LSBs, the electrochemical decom-
position of sulﬁde-SSEs in the cathode indeed contributes to ad-
ditional capacity. However, the electrochemical reversibility of
sulﬁde-SSEs in relation to cutoﬀvoltage, current density, and
cycling number has not been systematically studied. LiPSs, as
discharge intermediates, have been detected in some sulﬁde-
SSEs-based LSBs at high temperatures, but the reasons for
their formation, the impact on performance, and the possibil-
ity of element exchange or chemical interaction with sulﬁde-
SSEs are still unknown. To achieve solid-state LSBs with long
cycle life, high energy density, and fast charge capability, a
deep understanding of the underlying redox mechanisms of
solid-state sulfur cathodes is required, particularly in chemi-
cal engineering and its core principles such as thermodynam-
ics, kinetics, and mass transfer. It is essential to develop ad-
vanced in situ characterization techniques, such as in situ XAS,
in situ synchrotron-based XRD, in situ magic-angle-spinning
NMR spectroscopy, etc., to better understand the redox mech-
anisms from atomic (Å) to micro (μm) resolutions. The ten-
dencies of sulfur to vaporize under electron beams and sul-
ﬁdes to decompose under ambient conditions necessitate the
use of advanced analytical techniques, including cryogenic meth-
ods (e.g., cryo-electron microscopy) and those involving airtight
cells.
6.2.2. Engineering Application of Practical Solid-State LSBs
Since the inﬂuencing factors and design principles of four key
parameters, Rcathode, msl, RE/S, and RN/P, have been analyzed in
Section 5 to pursue solid-state LSBs with high energy density,
research prospects on these parameters and potential innovation
in processes and materials are proposed as follows to accelerate
the commercialization of solid-state LSBs.
High msl and Scalable Sulfur Cathode Design:
The sluggish
charge carrier transport in thick electrodes causes a signiﬁcant
gap between the reported msl and practical needs. For SPEs-based
LSBs, the primary task is to develop novel SPEs with high ionic
conductivity (above 10−4 S cm−1 at RT) to replace the current
PEO/LiTFSI system (10−7–10−6 S cm−1). In addition, the ionic
conductivity of SPEs is currently measured in membrane form.
However, the inﬂuence of the microporous structure inside the
cathode on Li+ transport in SPEs remains poorly understood.
For inorganic SSEs-based LSBs, researchers should prioritize
strategies to enhance sulfur conversion reactions, including the
use of advanced catalysts, redox-mediating SSEs, and novel ac-
tive materials design. Among these approaches, the integrated
designs are particularly promising for extending cycle life under
high msl. In addition, increased electrode thickness exacerbates
challenges such as uneven charge carrier conduction and poor
interface contact. To address this, constructing a long-range Li+
transport network with a low-curvature architecture represents
another viable strategy.
From the perspective of electrode manufacturing optimiza-
tion, dry-processing oﬀers signiﬁcant advantages for achieving
better interface contact in thick sulfur electrodes. Unlike tradi-
tional slurry casting methods that suﬀer from capillary stress-
induced cracking during solvent evaporation (which compro-
mises mechanical integrity and increases internal resistance),[220]
solvent-free dry-processing addresses these limitations. While
powder compression remains prevalent in laboratory-scale pro-
duction, its mold-dependent nature makes it unsuitable for roll-
to-roll manufacturing. For practical solid-state LSBs, binder ﬁb-
rillation technology has emerged as one of the most viable meth-
ods for large-scale manufacturing of both sulfur cathodes and
SSEs. In this method, polytetraﬂuoroethylene (PTFE), a ther-
moplastic polymer, is typically used as the binder. Under suﬃ-
cient shear forces and elevated temperatures (80–100 °C, above
PTFE’s glass transition temperature), the PTFE binder under-
goes ﬁbrillation and holds component powders together, form-
ing a self-standing ﬁlm.[221] Recently, Yang et al. developed thick-
ened dry cathode sheets with a high msl of 4.5 mg cm−2, which
were paired with dry SSE ﬁlms and Li3.75Si alloy anodes for pouch
cell fabrication.[222] For future designs of advanced dry-processed
sulfur cathodes aimed at large-scale production and pouch-cell
applications, achieving more uniform dry-mixing of conductive
agents and sulfur is critical to enable higher msl. This can be ac-
complished by optimizing the dry-mixing conditions. Developing
novel binders suitable for dry-processing sulfur cathodes is nec-
essary to address the electrochemical deﬂuorination and ionic in-
sulation of PTFE.
High Rcathode:
The Li+ accessibility of sulfur plays an impor-
tant role in improving Rcathode, which is determined by the interfa-
cial properties. Current functional integration design, for exam-
ple, hybrid Li+/e−conductors and redox-mediated SSEs, provides

Low RE/S:
RE/S is the key parameter that determines whether
solid-state LSBs can surpass 500 Wh kg−1 or even higher. For
SPEs-based LSBs with soluble LiPSs, it needs to explore the com-
plex relationship between the npolymer/nsalt/nsulfur ratio associated
with the coordination structure of LiPSs and the electrochemical
performance. A weak coordination structure is expected to ac-
celerate the diﬀusion rate of LiPSs in SPEs and enhance mass
transfer kinetics under low RE/S conditions. However, rapid dif-
fusion of LiPSs would exacerbate the shuttle eﬀect. Inhibiting
the chemical reaction between LiPSs and lithium anodes is cru-
cial for achieving a long lifespan. Shuttle-free sulfur reactions
with a solid–solid conversion pathway can eliminate the depen-
dence of LiPSs on RE/S. In this case, a novel shuttle-free sulfur-
based cathode with high sulfur content and conversion kinetics
can be rationally designed for SPEs-based LSBs. For inorganic
SSEs-based LSBs, there is an urgent need to develop scalable
processes for ultra-thin SSE membranes to reduce RE/S and re-
place current powder cold-pressing processes. Introducing func-
tional polymers into the inorganic SSE membrane is beneﬁcial
for forming a soft-hard composite structure, further improving
interface contact, mechanical properties, and ionic conductivity.
Low RN/P:
RN/P is an important parameter for practical solid-
state LSBs associated with cycle life. However, few reports fo-
cus on low RN/P conditions or use half cells to test the Coulom-
bic eﬃciency of lithium anodes in solid-state systems. Coulom-
bic eﬃciency needs to be at least 99% to reach a level equiv-
alent to liquid-state LSBs. Advanced imaging and spectroscopy
techniques, such as cryo-electron microscopy, ﬂuorescence probe
microscopy, in situ X-ray tomography, and titration gas chro-
matography, etc. can help to understand the composition and
formation mechanism of dead lithium. To reduce RN/P, high-
speciﬁc-capacity composite lithium anode or artiﬁcial SEI can
be constructed to avoid chemical reactions between lithium and
SSEs. Broadening the reduction potential window of electrolytes
to achieve a “really stable” metallic lithium/SSEs interface and
avoiding any side reactions may be the ﬁnal solution rather than
building an insuﬃciently strong SEI layer.
Low Stack Pressure:
While the aforementioned parameters
are directly associated with the energy density of solid-state LSBs,
stack pressure represents a critical engineering parameter that
has been addressed in only a limited number of studies. There
is an urgent need to minimize stack pressure to the lowest feasi-
ble level while accounting for technological constraints and cost-
eﬀectiveness considerations. Recent progress in all-solid-state
lithium metal batteries operating under low (<2 MPa) or no exter-
nal pressure has been achieved using inorganic solid electrolytes
and lithium metal anodes. However, sulfur’s signiﬁcant volume
expansion during cycling pose challenges that typically necessi-
tate operation under higher stack pressure at least 7 MPa. Chang-
ing the mechanical properties of sulfur itself, constructing in
situ self-healing or adaptive interfaces by additives, integrated
electrode-electrolyte design, etc. are potential solutions making
sulfur cathodes more viable for practical applications in solid-
state LSBs.
6.2.3. Artiﬁcial Intelligence for Practical Solid-State LSBs
Artiﬁcial intelligence (AI), with its robust data processing and
decision-making capabilities, is poised to promote the high-
quality and rapid development of solid-state LSBs: 1) Since novel
active materials and SSEs are expected to greatly improve the re-
action kinetics and interfacial properties of solid-state sulfur cath-
odes, the advantages of AI techniques in predicting and discover-
ing materials can signiﬁcantly reduce time costs and accurately
predict and discover various material properties, bypassing the
limitations brought by conventional methods, such as DFT cal-
culations or experimental procedures. 2) Since the complex reac-
tion mechanisms of solid-state sulfur cathodes and the complex
evolution mechanisms at the lithium/SSEs interface, advanced
multi-scale characterization techniques are required. Integrating
AI techniques with characterization methods can extract and ana-
lyze data more quickly and accurately to obtain reliable structure–
performance relationships. 3) The electrochemical performance
of solid-state LSBs is related to multiple engineering parameters,
but there is still a lack of basic knowledge in physics and chem-
istry related to these parameters, making parameter optimization
complex by using traditional methods. In the case, AI techniques
can process multidimensional datasets and better understand the
interdependence of engineering parameters, further predicting
battery performance.
Overall, with the booming development of novel SSEs, ad-
dressing the scientiﬁc issues related to reactions and interfaces is
expected to make solid-state LSBs as cost-eﬀective, high-energy,
safe alternatives to current batteries, enabling their broader ap-
plication in sustainable energy storage technologies. Future re-
search should focus on the challenges posed by high energy den-
sity and long cycle life, improve from both mechanism research
and engineering application aspects, reserve diﬀerences while
seeking common ground from liquid systems, explore low-cost
and universal modiﬁcation strategies, combine advanced AI tech-
niques, to bridge the gap between fundamentals and practical ap-
plications.
