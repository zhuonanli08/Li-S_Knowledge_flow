Progress and Prospects of Inorganic Solid-State
Electrolyte-Based All-Solid-State Li–S Batteries
Tong Liu, Ronghui Liu, Chengxing Lu,* and Wenjia Song*
All-solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs), a promising alternative to
liquid lithium–sulfur batteries, are expected to alleviate the shuttle eﬀect,
reduce material loss, and achieve a compact structure. However, ASSLSBs
face challenges in ionic conductivity and stability of solid electrolytes,
optimization of sulfur cathodes, and electrolyte/electrode interfaces. This
review summarizes recent research progress and strategies addressing these
issues, focusing on oxide and sulﬁde-based electrolytes. Furthermore, it
emphasizes the crucial role of rational optimization of sulfur cathode
materials in composition, structure, and microstructure for constructing
eﬃcient ion/electron transport networks, and explores methods to solve
chemical/electrochemical and physical interface issues. Additionally, it
addresses challenges associated with the lithium anode and its interface
problems, covering strategies, such as lithium alloy formation, 3D electrode
architecture, and interfacial buﬀer layer implementation. These approaches
aim to enhance the stability and performance of the lithium anode in
ASSLSBs. Finally, this review highlights the signiﬁcance of in situ
characterization techniques for revealing reaction mechanisms, providing
insights into phase composition, elemental chemical states, and dynamic
structural transformations within the batteries, crucial for developing
high-performance ASSLSBs.

1. Introduction
Building a low-carbon society has be-
come a common goal for all of human-
ity. It is indispensable for us to amplify
our eﬀorts to strengthen innovation-led
energy development and to develop a safe
and eﬃcient modern energy system.[1,2]
Considering the variable and intermit-
tent nature of solar and wind energy,
battery technology can store and inte-
grate renewable energy into the grid,
emerges as a potent means to harness
these clean and cost-eﬀective energies
for daily use.[3] Currently, rechargeable
lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) are the pri-
mary energy systems for small/mid-sized
applications such as portable devices and
electric vehicles (EVs).[4,5] However, due
to their relatively high cost and lower en-
ergy and power densities, LIBs are not the
ideal power option for large-scale station-
ary energy storage.[6]
Owing to the cost beneﬁts of sulfur
and the high theoretical energy density of
batteries, room-temperature (RT) lithium
sulfur batteries with an ultrahigh the-
oretical capacity of 1675 mAh g−1 and
energy density of 2600 Wh kg−1 are regarded as the frontrun-
ners for future energy storage.[7,8] Li–S batteries are composed of
metallic sodium as the anode and elemental sulfur as the cath-
ode. The energy storage mechanism is based on the multielec-
tron conversion between S and Li accompanied by the generation
of various sulfur species. In the organic liquid electrolyte system,
the discharge process can be summarized into four stages: i) the
conversion from solid S8 to liquid long-chain polysulﬁde Li2S8;
ii) the conversion from liquid Li2S8 to liquid medium-chain poly-
sulﬁde Li2S4; iii) the conversion from liquid Li2S4 to solid Li2S2;
iv) the conversion from solid Li2S2 to solid Li2S. The charging
state is the reverse process of the aforementioned reactions.[10,9]
From this, numerous intrinsic problems are exposed, particu-
larly concerning the S cathodes. Speciﬁcally, the sulfur cathode
suﬀers from the shuttling eﬀect of soluble lithium polysulﬁdes
(LiPS), high activation barriers of multistep electron transfer re-
actions, leading to poor cycle stability, low-rate performance, and
low Coulombic eﬃciency, and the lithium metal anode experi-
ences lithium dendrite growth during charging, initiating un-
desirable side reactions, decreasing energy density, and posing
safety concerns of internal short-circuiting.[11,12] When designing
the sulfur cathode, it is necessary to choose an appropriate carrier

Actually, the electrochemical reaction process of Li–S batter-
ies is highly dependent on the amount of electrolyte and sul-
fur load, and can be described by the electrolyte/sulfur (E/S)
ratio. Lowering the electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio enables the re-
alization of low-cost and high-energy-density Li–S batteries.[15]
However, for most of the optimized organic liquid Li–S batter-
ies, there are still problems such as severe shuttle eﬀect, slow
reaction kinetics, and poor compatibility with lithium metal un-
der the condition of low E/S ratio.[16] To address these challenges,
researchers have shifted their focus toward inorganic solid-state
electrolyte-based Li–S battery technologies, which show great
promise for achieving high energy densities under low E/S ra-
tio, because solid electrolytes are signiﬁcantly lighter than their
liquid counterparts, which further enhances the energy density
of solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries. Additionally, solid elec-
trolytes can eﬀectively suppress the dissolution and diﬀusion
of polysulﬁdes, thereby inherently preventing the shuttle eﬀect
and improving the battery’s Coulombic eﬃciency and cycling
stability.[17–19]
The working principle of all-solid-state lithium–sulfur batter-
ies (ASSLSBs) is similar to that of traditional liquid Li–S bat-
teries, relying on the migration of lithium ions between the S
cathode and Li anode to realize the charging and discharging
cycle.[20–22] And the uniqueness of solid-state batteries lies in the
use of solid electrolyte, both lithium-ion conduction and isola-
tion of the dual function of electronic transmission, thus integrat-
ing the role of liquid electrolyte and diaphragm, so that the bat-
tery structure is more concise.[23] The performance of ASSLSBs
primarily depends on ion transport dynamics. Three key compo-
nents govern this process: the solid-state electrolyte facilitates ion
movement, electrode materials provide sites for ion storage, and
the electrolyte–electrode interface enables ion transfer. These ele-
ments collectively determine battery performance.[24] Enhancing
ASSLSB eﬃciency requires coordinated improvements: boosting
the electrolyte’s ion conductivity and stability, developing elec-
trode materials with high surface area and optimized structures
for rapid ion diﬀusion, and creating stable, low-resistance inter-
faces for eﬃcient ion transport (Figure 1).

suitable electrolyte materials, we can enhance their ionic con-

2) Design of S cathodes composition and structure. ASSLSBs
face signiﬁcant performance challenges in the sulfur cathode.
The intrinsic insulating nature of sulfur restricts electronic
transport, which not only reduces material utilization but also
compromises the rate performance of the battery. The multistep
solid–solid phase transitions of sulfur during charge–discharge
cycles, particularly the formation and decomposition of Li2S,
involve high energy barriers, resulting in slow conversion kinet-
ics and large overpotentials. Furthermore, the reaction between
sulfur and lithium during charge–discharge processes induces
a substantial volume expansion of ≈80%, severely aﬀecting the
stability of the electrode structure and long-term cycling per-
formance. Consequently, the design of sulfur cathodes should
focus on enhancing intrinsic conductivity, optimizing ionic
transport pathways, and promoting solid-phase conversion ki-
netics to overcome these critical issues. Improvement strategies
include the addition of highly conductive or polar materials,
intercalation/conversion-type materials, catalytic materials, and
optimization of the inherent properties of sulfur. On this basis,
optimizing the interaction between solid electrolytes and sulfur
cathodes is crucial for achieving high-performance ASSLSBs.
Based on the underlying causes, these interfacial issues can
be categorized into two major types: chemical/electrochemical
interfacial problems[30] and physical interfacial problems.[31] The
former primarily encompasses narrow electrochemical stability
windows of solid electrolytes, electrolyte degradation, inter-
diﬀusion of elements at the interfaces, and space charge layers,
among others.[30] The latter, on the other hand, predominantly
stems from the rigid interfacial contact between solid electrolytes
and sulfur cathodes, as well as material fracture and pulveriza-
tion induced by volume changes and stress accumulation in
the sulfur cathode during cycling processes.[31] Designing and
constructing ideal solid electrolyte/sulfur cathode interfaces can
facilitate eﬃcient ion transport while suppressing side reactions,
thereby prolonging the cycle life of the battery.
3) Design of Li anode composition and structure. Lithium
metal anodes are crucial for high-energy-density batteries due to
their exceptional theoretical capacity of 3860 mAh g−1 and lowest
standard electrochemical potential of −3.040 V versus SHE.[32]
These properties make lithium ideal for next-generation energy
storage systems. However, the practical application of lithium
anodes faces a signiﬁcant challenge: the formation and growth
of lithium dendrites. Dendrite growth, contrary to early predic-
tions, occurs in solid-state systems even at low current densities.
This phenomenon is inﬂuenced by solid electrolyte microstruc-
tures, such as grain boundaries and surface defects, leading to
nonuniform electric ﬁelds and localized ion ﬂux. Consequently,
dendrites pose substantial safety risks and degrade long-term cy-
cling stability. To overcome these challenges, researchers have
developed two main strategies. The ﬁrst involves lithium an-
ode modiﬁcation, utilizing lithium alloys (e.g., Li-In, Li-Al) or
3D porous structures.[33,34] These modiﬁcations enhance electro-
chemical stability and suppress dendrite growth while maintain-
ing high capacity. The second strategy focuses on interface en-
gineering, developing buﬀer layers between the electrode and
electrolyte.[35] Ideal buﬀer materials combine ionic conductivity,
electronic insulation, and compatibility with both lithium and
solid electrolytes. Examples include inorganic layers for mechan-
ical strength, lithium-philic interlayers to reduce nucleation over-
potential, and ﬂexible organic layers to accommodate cycling-
induced volume changes.[36–38]
In this comprehensive review, we address the recent progress
on ASSLSBs. We focus on the optimization strategies of diﬀer-
ent solid electrolytes employed in ASSLSBs systems and high-
light common strategies to mitigate the contact issues at the
solid–solid interface. And, some examples related to the in
situ/quasi-in situ characterization techniques for elucidating the
reaction mechanisms and performance optimization pathways
of ASSLSBs. Finally, we provide an overview and outlook on the
commercialization prospects and potential application scenarios
for these batteries.
2. Category of Solid Electrolytes
Solid-state electrolytes are crucial components in ASSLSBs, serv-
ing not only to prevent internal short circuits but also to pro-
vide channels for lithium-ion transport. Currently, the most com-
monly used solid-state electrolytes in inorganic ASSLSBs sys-
tems can be categorized into two main types: oxides and sul-
ﬁdes. In ASSLSBs, solid electrolyte types determine sulfur re-
action mechanisms. Upper part of Figure 2a illustrates the re-
action mechanisms of oxide solid-state electrolytes in ASSLSBs,
the electrochemical reaction process is similar to that of liquid
lithium–sulfur batteries. For example, Zhang et al. conducted an
in-depth investigation into the electrochemical reaction mech-
anism of ASSLSBs based on the LLAZO (Li7La3Zr2O12)@CNF
cathode matrix. The ﬁrst plateau appears between 2.4 and 2.1 V,
corresponding to the formation of long-chain polysulﬁdes (Li2Sn,
4 ≤n ≤8), while the second plateau in the range of 2.1–1.7 V rep-
resents the formation of lower-order Li2S.[20] During the charg-
ing process, Li2S is gradually oxidized through various interme-
diates. Whereas, as shown in lower part of Figure 2a, the electro-
chemical reaction process in sulﬁde-based ASSLSBs diﬀers sig-
niﬁcantly from oxide-based systems. Zhu et al. using a Li-In alloy
anode, S cathode, and Li5.4PS4.4Cl1.6 SSE interlayer observed only
one pair of redox peaks and one pair of reaction plateaus, which
contrasts sharply with the multistep reaction characteristics of
liquid lithium–sulfur batteries. In situ Raman spectroscopy and
ex situ X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) studies showed that
during the discharge process, S8 is directly reduced to Li2S2, and
then further reduced to Li2S. During the charging process, Li2S
is preferentially oxidized to Li2S2, and ﬁnally oxidized to S8.[21]
These mechanisms profoundly impact the performance of
all-solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs). Oxide elec-
trolytes’ multistep reactions can result in complex kinetics,
whereas sulﬁde electrolytes’ direct conversion process may

2.1. Oxides
Oxide electrolytes were among the earliest systems explored,
which can be classiﬁed into crystalline and amorphous forms.
Common crystalline types include perovskite, lithium germa-
nium phosphate (LISICON), and garnet-structured metal oxides,
while amorphous electrolytes include lithium phosphorus oxyni-
tride (LiPON). Among these, garnet-structured metal oxides have
been extensively studied in the ﬁeld of ASSLSBs. Garnets them-
selves are silicates with the general formula A3B2(SiO4)3, such
as calcium aluminum silicate Ca3Al2Si3O12. However, garnet-
type electrolytes are not silicates but rather inorganic nonmetal-
lic compounds in which silicon atoms are replaced by various
metal atoms while maintaining the same structure. The general
formula for commonly used garnet-type solid electrolytes can be
represented as Li3+xA3B2O12. In this structure (Figure 3a), atom
A is an eight-coordinated metal element, commonly using lan-
thanide elements such as La; atom B is a six-coordinated metal
element, which can be a tetravalent metal, such as Zr or a pen-
tavalent metal such as Ta. The AO8 and BO6 polyhedra are in-
terconnected in a staggered manner through coplanar sharing to
form a 3D spatial structure.[83] The interstitial spaces are com-
posed of octahedral and tetrahedral vacancies formed by oxygen
atoms, and lithium ions ﬁll these vacancies according to a certain
pattern (Figure 3b).[84]
The garnet-type Li3+xA3B2O12 has two phases: tetragonal and
cubic. The ionic conductivity of the cubic phase is ≈1–2 orders
of magnitude higher than that of the tetragonal phase. The ma-
jor distinction between the tetragonal and cubic phases of the
garnet-type Li3+xA3B2O12 lies in the arrangement of Li+. In the
cubic phase, Li+ are randomly and partially distributed across
tetrahedral 24d sites, octahedral 48 g sites, and distorted tetra-
hedral split 96 h sites. The transition to the tetragonal phase in-
duces tetragonal distortion, transforming the 24d sites into fully
occupied 8a sites and the partially occupied 48g/96 h sites into
two fully occupied 16f and 32 g sites. This tetragonal distortion
signiﬁcantly impacts the Li+ distribution and decreases the ionic
conductivity.[83]
Li7La3Zr2O12 (LLZO) is the most typical representative among
them. Weppner et al. synthesized a novel chemical composition,
Li7La3Zr2O12, based on garnet-structured oxides. Compared to
Li-La-M-O2 (M = Nb, Ta), Li7La3Zr2O12 demonstrates enhanced
lithium-ion conductivity and reduced activation energy. This

In terms of compositional tuning, elemental doping can
eﬀectively improve the ionic conductivity and structural stability
of garnet-type solid electrolytes (Figure 3c,d).[86,87] For example,
Wei et al. designed a Ca-W codoped LLZO-based solid electrolyte
and systematically investigated the eﬀects of doping on the for-
mation of the cubic phase and Li+ mobility. Rietveld reﬁnement
of HAADF-STEM (Figure 4a) and X-ray Diﬀraction (XRD) date
(Figure 4b) results conﬁrmed the partial substitution of Ca2+
and W6+ at La3+ and Zr4+ sites, respectively. DFT calculations
revealed that the local Li+ framework modiﬁcation eﬀect induced
by Ca-W codoping increased the Li+ occupancy in the 96 h sites,
which can signiﬁcantly reduce the Li+ migration barrier, thereby
enhancing the ionic conductivity by two orders of magnitude
(Figure 4c). Furthermore, to understand the stabilizing eﬀect
of Ca-W codoping on c-LLZO, ab initio molecular dynamics
simulations of the phase transition from the tetragonal to cubic
phase were performed on W- and Ca-doped LLZO. Undoped
LLZO remained in the tetragonal phase between 300 and 800 K,
and the transition to the cubic phase occurred at 1000 K. Upon
W-doping (LLZWO), the phase transition temperature decreased
to 800 K, while Ca-W codoping (LLCZWO) highly stabilized
the cubic phase and further reduced the transition temperature
to 500 K. The decrease in the phase transition temperature
can be considered a signature of cubic phase stabilization.
Although accurate transition temperatures are diﬃcult to obtain
from ab initio calculations, the stabilization trend of the cubic
phase clearly follows the order LLCZWO > LLZWO > LLZO
(Figure 4d,e). The tetragonal structures are energetically more
favorable, but the energy diﬀerence between the tetragonal and
cubic structures decreases upon Ca and W doping, indicating
that Ca and W stabilize the cubic phase. One of the key reasons
for the poor stability of the cubic phase is the distortion of ZrO6
octahedra. In t-LLZO, the average Zr─O bond length is 2.143
Å with an O─Zr─O bond angle of 180°, while in c-LLZO, the
Zr─O bonds are stretched to 2.149 Å, and the average O─Zr─O
bond angle is 178.33°. The substitution of Ca and W in LLZO
generates Li vacancies, reducing the Li–Li Coulombic repulsion.
Furthermore, the smaller ionic radii of Ca and W alleviate the
ZrO6 distortion and restore the average O─Zr─O bond angle to
179.69° in c-LLCZWO, thereby stabilizing the c-LLZO phase.[88]

increases to a certain extent, resulting in the material containing
more than 5 diﬀerent elements overall, the properties of the
material will change signiﬁcantly, exhibiting characteristics
distinctly diﬀerent from traditional single or low-level elemental
doping. This multicomponent doping system is referred to as
a high-entropy material, and its unique performance originates
from the high-entropy eﬀect.[89] For example, Gerbrand Ceder’s

conductivity by several orders of magnitude, thereby lowering the
overall battery resistance and enhancing performance. This work

The development of sulﬁde solid electrolytes is presented
in the form of a timeline, which can be traced back to 1981

a fast ionic conductor material.[98] The Li2S-P2S5 system has al-

in crystal structure, it can be divided into three categories: glassy,
crystalline, and glass-ceramic phases. Glassy electrolytes have an
open structure and large free volume, and their lithium-ion con-

easy to eliminate, and their ionic conductivity is generally 1–2
orders of magnitude higher than that of crystalline electrolytes
with the same composition. However, they usually exhibit lower
ionic conductivity compared to the corresponding glass-ceramic
sulﬁde electrolytes. Despite the lower ionic conductivity of glassy

sulﬁde electrolytes, they have better compatibility with the cath-

binary xLi2S-(100-x)P2S5 system. When x takes diﬀerent values,
the resulting electrolyte compositions diﬀer, and the electrolyte
properties also vary greatly. Among them, Li3PS4 (i.e., x = 75)
and Li7P3S11 (i.e., x = 70) have been most extensively studied.
Li3PS4 was ﬁrst reported by Tachez et al. in 1984.[106–109] Li3PS4
has three crystalline phases, including the 𝛼phase, 𝛽phase, and
𝛾phase.[110] The ionic conductivity varies greatly among the dif-
ferent crystal phases. The ionic conductivity of 𝛾-Li3PS4 is only
3 × 10−7 S cm−1, while the ionic conductivity of 𝛽-Li3PS4 can
reach 10−4 S cm−1. The main reason for this phenomenon is
the diﬀerent arrangement of PS4 tetrahedra in the various crys-
tal phases. In 𝛾-Li3PS4, the vertices of the PS4 tetrahedra are ar-
ranged in the same direction, while in 𝛽-Li3PS4, the vertices of the
PS4 tetrahedra point in diﬀerent directions and are arranged in
a zigzag structure, which gives Li+ better mobility in the tetra-
hedral and octahedral sites. Unlike the crystalline structure of
Li3PS4, when the Li2S content is reduced to 70%, the resulting
electrolyte Li7P3S11 belongs to the triclinic unit cell structure.

3. Design of Sulfur Cathode for Solid State Li–S
Batteries
The sulfur cathode in ASSLSBs faces challenges of poor con-
ductivity and sluggish conversion kinetics. The insulating na-
ture of sulfur leads to limited electron transport, low material
utilization, and poor rate capability. During cycling, sulfur un-
dergoes multistep solid–solid phase transformations with slow
kinetics and high energy barriers, particularly for Li2S forma-
tion/decomposition, resulting in large overpotentials and low ef-
ﬁciency. Therefore, designing sulfur cathodes should focus on
enhancing intrinsic conductivity, optimizing ionic pathways, and
promoting solid-phase conversion kinetics to overcome these
critical issues.
3.1. Modiﬁcation with Highly Conductive or Polar Materials
Incorporating highly ionic/electronic conductive components,
such as carbon-based materials, whose size, morphology, dimen-
sionality, and spatial distribution are crucial for the overall charge
transport properties of the sulfur cathode. For example, Zhang
et al. investigated the electron transfer process at the interface
between active sulfur and conductive carbon in an operational
cell. The unique coaxial carbon nanotube@sulfur composite with
enhanced electron contacts facilitates rapid electron transport
and minimizes interfacial charge transfer resistance, resulting in
high sulfur utilization and outstanding electrochemical behavior.
At a sulfur loading of 1.3 mg cm−2, an initial discharge capacity of
1138.7 mAh g−1 is obtained at 0.21 mA cm−2, with an impressive
capacity retention of 87.7% after 200 cycles.[70] Yao et al. designed
an advanced 2D ordered mesoporous carbon (OMC) framework

4. Strategies for Enhancing Interfacial Contact
Between S Cathode and Solid State Electrolyte
Oxide electrolytes oﬀer high mechanical strength and chemi-
cal stability but struggle to form good interfaces with sulfur
cathodes due to their rigidity. Conversely, sulﬁde electrolytes
provide excellent ionic conductivity and ﬂexibility but suﬀer
from low chemical stability and reactivity with electrodes. These
characteristics result in physical instability (high impedance,
delamination) and chemical instability (side reactions, layer
growth) at interfaces. Such issues impact the battery’s ion trans-
port, rate performance, cycling stability, and long-term eﬃ-
ciency. Enhancing all-solid-state lithium–sulfur battery perfor-
mance thus requires addressing these electrolyte-speciﬁc interfa-
cial problems through targeted engineering and material design
strategies.
4.1. Strategies for Physical Interface Issues
Notably, in the case of ASSLSBs, the phase transition between
sulfur (S) and lithium sulﬁde (Li2S) during charge and dis-
charge processes is accompanied by a drastic volume expan-
sion/contraction of ≈79%. Such a signiﬁcant volume eﬀect
can induce stress concentration and displacement accumulation
within the materials, disrupting the structural integrity of the
cathode and hindering eﬃcient ion/electron transport, thus exac-
erbating capacity fading and impedance rise in the battery. Con-
sequently, optimizing the physical properties of solid–solid inter-
faces and mitigating the volume change eﬀects of cathode ma-
terials are crucial for enhancing the long-term cycling stability
of ASSLSBs. To address the aforementioned issues, scientiﬁc re-
search has focused on three aspects: Three-phase mixture of sul-
fur cathode, sulfur cathode host, and solid-state electrolyte, layer-
by-layer fabrication processes of sulfur cathodes and solid elec-
trolytes, nanostructure engineering, and interface engineering.
The three-phase mixture of sulfur cathode, sulfur cathode
host, and solid-state electrolyte is one of the key strategies for con-
structing high-performance ASSLSBs. By rationally designing
and optimizing the interfacial structure and interactions among
these three components, the electrochemical performance and
long-term stability of the battery can be eﬀectively improved. For
example, Wang et al. reported a method for synthesizing a sulfur

5. Lithium Metal Anode
The lithium metal anode, due to its extremely high theoreti-
cal capacity (3680–3862 mAh g−1), lowest density (0.53 g cm−3),
and very low electrochemical potential (−3.040 V vs SHE), has
become key to achieving high-performance ASSLSBs.[32] How-
ever, lithium metal anodes face numerous challenges in practi-
cal applications, especially the issue of lithium dendrite growth,
which severely aﬀects the safety and long-term stability of the
battery.[134] Early theoretical models, such as the Sand’s time the-
ory in liquid battery systems and the Monroe–Newman model in
polymer electrolyte batteries, predicted that dendrite problems
would not occur in all-solid-state batteries.[135] However, actual
experimental observations showed signiﬁcant diﬀerences from
these theoretical predictions. Under relatively low current den-
sity conditions, lithium metal dendrites could still grow in solid
electrolytes and eventually lead to battery short circuits. This phe-
nomenon not only challenges our understanding of material be-
havior but also poses signiﬁcant tests for battery safety perfor-
mance and long-term stability.
The microstructural characteristics of solid electrolytes play a
crucial role in the nucleation and growth of lithium dendrites.
These characteristics mainly include grain boundaries, surface
defects, and internal voids. The inhomogeneity of these struc-
tures can lead to uneven electric ﬁeld distribution and localized
concentration of lithium ions ﬂux, providing growth sites for den-
drites. Simultaneously, these areas are often stress concentration
points, prone to generating microcracks under mechanical loads,
further promoting dendrite growth. For example, Wang et al. us-
ing neutron depth proﬁling technique, observed the nucleation
and growth of lithium dendrites inside the sulﬁde electrolyte
Li3PS4. This phenomenon primarily stems from the high ionic
conductivity of the electrolyte, which facilitates rapid lithium ion
transport, thereby leading to the formation of lithium dendrites
within the electrolyte (Figure 14a).[136] In actual battery opera-
tion, the interface between metallic lithium and solid electrolyte
exhibits complex dynamic characteristics. Charge–discharge cy-
cling may lead to microscopic changes at the interface, such as
voids and grain boundaries. These changes trigger a dual ef-
fect: ﬁrst, they weaken the interfacial contact between the elec-
trode and electrolyte, reducing ion transport eﬃciency; second,
they cause local current density to increase, exacerbating elec-
trochemical polarization phenomena. These two factors inter-
act, jointly accelerating the formation and growth process of
lithium dendrites. Rettenwander et al. combining in situ opti-
cal and ex situ dark-ﬁeld X-ray microscopy methods, investigated
the evolution process of lithium dendrites in LiSn∣single crystal
Li6.5La3Zr1.5Ta0.5O12∣LiSn symmetric cells. Experimental obser-
vations revealed the presence of dislocations in the dendrite front
region, with these defects showing a close relationship to den-
drite growth. Researchers inferred that the stress caused by den-
drite growth might induce the formation of dislocations, which
in turn inﬂuence the destruction process of the electrolyte and
dominate the growth direction and branching behavior of den-
drites (Figure 14b).[137]
In fact, the formation process of lithium dendrites is often ac-
companied by multiﬁeld coupling eﬀects. Xu et al. using LLZO as
the research subject, developed a comprehensive research frame-
work coupling battery models, mechanical models, phase-ﬁeld
models, and short-circuit models to investigate crack propagation
and lithium dendrite growth behavior in solid electrolytes. Their
research revealed several key factors aﬀecting the performance of
all-solid-state batteries: 1) Charging rate: High C-rates (>0.5C) ac-
celerate the occurrence of short circuits, caused by larger overpo-
tentials driving crack propagation and dendrite growth. 2) Elec-
trolyte conductivity: Lower conductivity leads to increased over-
potential, triggering earlier short circuits. 3) Electrolyte conduc-
tivity (𝜎LLZO): Improving conductivity can reduce internal re-
sistance, enhance battery performance, and delay crack prop-
agation, thereby postponing the time to short circuit. 4) Elec-
trolyte Young’s modulus (ELLZO): In the range of 40–100 GPa,
higher Young’s modulus accelerates crack propagation, increas-
ing the risk of short circuits. This is because Young’s modulus
simultaneously aﬀects both the driving force and resistance to
crack propagation. 5) Electrolyte fracture toughness (KLLZO):
Higher fracture toughness can eﬀectively suppress or even pre-
vent crack propagation, signiﬁcantly reducing the risk of internal
short circuits.[138]
To improve interfacial contact, a certain stacking pressure is
usually applied. Uniform external pressure can prevent stress
concentration and preferential growth of lithium dendrites in
local areas. However, the eﬀects on dendrite formation and
growth stages diﬀer signiﬁcantly, requiring precise control to bal-
ance interface stability and dendrite suppression. Bruce et al.
applied high temporal resolution X-ray computed tomography
(XCT) technology to investigate the complete evolution process
of lithium dendrites in all-solid-state batteries under in situ con-
ditions. The study revealed that lithium deposition induces com-
plex viscoplastic deformation inside dendrites, causing stress ac-
cumulation. This stress both drives dendrite growth and pro-

6. Strategies for Enhancing Interfacial Contact
Between Li Anode and Solid State Electrolyte
Strategies for improving the lithium metal interface issues in all-
solid-state lithium batteries/lithium–sulfur batteries can be pri-
marily approached from two aspects: modiﬁcation of the lithium
anode and construction of interfacial buﬀer layers.
6.1. Modiﬁcation of the Lithium Anode
6.1.1. Lithium Alloying
Lithium-based alloy anodes (such as Li-In, Li-Al, Li-Mg, Li-
Zn, Li-Si, Li-Ag, etc.) form binary or multielement structures
that maintain high speciﬁc capacity and low reduction poten-
tial while enhancing electrochemical stability and interfacial
compatibility.[140–142] They eﬀectively suppress dendrite growth,
showing promising potential as alternatives to pure lithium
metal. The solubility of alloying elements in lithium signiﬁcantly
aﬀects their interaction mechanisms: 1) High solubility elements
(e.g., Au, Ag, Zn, Mg) tend to form single-phase solid solutions
with lithium, exhibiting near-zero nucleation overpotential and
small volume expansion. 2) Medium solubility elements (e.g., Al,
Pt) show lower nucleation barriers. 3) Low solubility elements
(e.g., Si, Sn) require phase transformation reconstruction to form
alloy phases, a process demanding higher activation energy. 4) In-
soluble elements (e.g., Cu, Ni) need to overcome even higher en-
ergy barriers for nucleation. These solubility-driven characteristic
changes directly inﬂuence lithium dissolution-deposition kinet-
ics, signiﬁcantly aﬀecting lithium deposition behavior and elec-
trochemical performance. For example, Han et al. evaluated the
dendrite suppression capabilities of various lithium alloys (in-
cluding Li-Zn, Li-Si, Li-Sn, and Li-Al) and found that the Li-Al
alloy exhibited the most outstanding performance. The Li-Al al-
loy exhibits superior mechanical properties, which play a crucial
role in eﬀectively suppressing lithium dendrite formation and
growth. As a result, symmetric cells employing Li-Al alloy an-
odes demonstrated excellent performance, showing no soft short-
circuit phenomena under high current density (40 mA cm−2) and
high capacity (3 mAh cm−2) conditions. Furthermore, these an-
odes exhibited outstanding performance in solid-state lithium–
sulfur batteries with high sulfur loading (10 mAh cm−2).[141]
Zhang et al. developed a novel ternary composite lithium anode,
Li0.95Mg0.05-50 wt% Graphite, featuring stable lithium diﬀusion
pathways and a high lithium diﬀusion rate of 3.41×10−10 cm2
s−1. This composite anode structure eﬀectively suppressed inter-
facial void growth, signiﬁcantly enhancing the stability of interfa-
cial contact. After high-capacity (6 mAh cm−2) lithium stripping,
it still maintained 94.2% contact area. Its excellent performance
is mainly attributed to the stable diﬀusion pathways constructed
by the LiMg alloy substrate and the enhancement of lithium dif-
fusion speed by graphite particles, eﬀectively suppressing the
dynamic decay of interfacial contact and improving the overall
working stability of the battery (Figure 15a).[142]
6.1.2. 3D Porous Framework/Host
3D lithium metal anodes can eﬀectively suppress dendrite
growth in liquid batteries but are hindered in all-solid-state bat-
teries due to the rigidity of solid electrolytes. To address this,
Sun et al. introduced a sacriﬁcial layer of mixed sulﬁde and
halide electrolytes on the anode side. The halides in the sacri-
ﬁcial layer react with lithium to form a 3D lithium metal anode.
And the in situ generated lithium alloy structure can lower the
lithium nucleation barrier and promote uniform deposition. Its
high speciﬁc surface area reduces local current density and ac-
celerates lithium ions transport while also mitigating electrolyte
fracture and interface gap issues. The assembled symmetric cells
demonstrated stable cycling for over 500 h at a current density of
3 mA cm−2, with a single cycle capacity reaching 3 mAh cm−2
(Figure 15b).[34]
6.2. Construction of Interfacial Buﬀer Layers
Interfacial buﬀer layers play a crucial role in addressing inter-
face issues in lithium metal batteries. These buﬀer layers aim to
improve interfacial contact, promote lithium ions transport, and
suppress side reactions and lithium dendrite formation. An ideal
interface layer should possess ionic conductivity, electronic insu-
lation, electrochemical stability, and good compatibility with both
lithium metal and solid electrolytes. Based on chemical composi-
tion, interface buﬀer layers can be classiﬁed into three categories:
inorganic interface buﬀer layers, organic interface buﬀer layers,
and inorganic–organic composite interface buﬀer layers.[35]
6.2.1. Inorganic Interface Buﬀer Layers
Inorganic interface layers feature high mechanical strength and
good ionic conductivity, making it easy to achieve low thickness
and high uniformity of coatings. Among them, lithium silicates,
lithium phosphates, lithium halides, and lithium nitrides can
serve as artiﬁcial Solid Electrolyte Interphase (SEI) components.
Artiﬁcial SEI layers have high Young’s modulus, high surface
energy, and low surface diﬀusion barriers, which help resist the
stress of dendrite growth while facilitating lithium ion transport
at the interface.[35,36] Zhou et al. deposited a uniquely structured
LiI layer at the Li/SSE interface using a chemical iodine-vapor de-
position (CIVD) method. This LiI layer, composed of densely in-
terwoven elongated rice-grain-shaped nano-LiI crystals, exhibits
excellent toughness, high mechanical strength, and good ionic
conductivity, maintaining a stable Li/SSE interface during long-
term cycling. All-solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries prepared us-
ing this LiI layer showed a high capacity of 1400 mAh g−1 at 0.1C,
with 80.6% capacity retention after 150 cycles at room tempera-
ture. Under high areal capacity (1.35 mAh cm−2) and high tem-
perature (90 °C) conditions, the battery still maintained a high
capacity of 1500 mAh g−1 and achieved stable cycling for over
100 cycles (Figure 16a).[36]

ing lithium plating/stripping behavior. Interlayers based on
conversion-type metal oxides (CuO, ZnO, Al2O3, etc.) and

hexamethylene diisocyanate (HDI), and 2-hydroxyethyl disulﬁde

a high capacity of 812 mAh g−1. Using ultrasonic imaging tech-

integrated structure was signiﬁcantly better than traditional lam-
inated structures, further conﬁrming the advantages of this novel
electrolyte in improving battery interface stability.[37]

7. Solid-State Cell Design for Ex/In Situ
Characterization
Leveraging in situ and quasi in situ electrochemical characteri-
zation techniques, we can gain profound insights into the evo-
lution of electrode material components and structure, the dy-
namic changes in the electrolyte, and the underlying mech-
anisms governing electrode–electrolyte interfacial interactions.
This enables us to elucidate the intrinsic reaction behaviors and
performance degradation mechanisms of ASSLSBs during the
charge/discharge process. The in situ and quasi in situ electro-
chemical characterization techniques can be classiﬁed into two
types: imaging and spectroscopy. The former such as Optical Mi-
croscope (OM), Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM), and Trans-
mission Electron Microscope (TEM) are used to observe the sta-
bility and evolution of the morphology of electrode materials or
solid electrolyte during electrochemical processes; the latter such
as Raman, XAFS, and XPS, etc., are used to analyze the evolu-
tion of the structural components of electrode materials, as well
as the substrates, intermediates, and products of electrochemical
reactions.[133]
In situ X-ray techniques, such as XRD, nano CT, and XAFS,
can obtain key information about the phase composition, ele-
mental chemical state, and 3D structure inside the battery. For
example, Zhu et al. designed a nonin situ XAFS characteriza-
tion scheme to study the conversion mechanism between S8 and
Li2S. The experimental results showed that when comparing the
XANES at 50% charge and discharge states with the initial state, a
characteristic peak of Li2S2 was obtained at 2471.3 eV, indicating
the formation of Li2S2 during the reaction process. XANES analy-
sis of sulfur cathodes at 50% charge after diﬀerent cycle numbers
reveals that the characteristic peak of Li2S2 stably exists during
the charge–discharge process, and no long-chain polysulﬁdes are
formed during the reaction. Furthermore, analysis at 100% dis-
charge state shows that Li2S2 and S8 still appear, indicating the
incomplete reaction of S8 and verifying the poor reaction kinetics
in ASSLSBs.[21]
Surface-sensitive in situ characterization methods, such as
Raman spectroscopy (Raman), and TOF-SIMS, can monitor the
phase transformation process of electrode materials and the
evolution of the surface chemical environment in real-time. The
previous work also used in situ Raman technology to analyze
the reaction process from S8 to Li2S.[21] Raman spectroscopy can
reveal the relationship between the electrochemical behavior
and structural changes of materials by detecting the energy
levels of vibrational modes. For ASSLSB systems, the diﬀerent
chain-length sulﬁdes involved in the lithiation process corre-
spond to diﬀerent vibrational modes, thus serving to analyze
the intermediate phases in the reaction. The researchers used a
simple cross-sectional electrochemical cell in the in situ Raman
experiment, which can expose the active material while keep-
ing the entire battery under normal operating pressure for a
ASSLSB (Figure 17a). The entire Raman spectroscopy detection
was placed in a glove box, and the entire testing process was con-
ducted under an argon atmosphere to avoid the decomposition
of sulﬁde electrolytes in contact with air. Moreover, the entire
electrode does not require additional encapsulation, avoiding
signal attenuation and interference caused by the encapsula-
tion material. The experimental results showed that during
the discharge process, the Raman signal of S8 (158, 220, and
473 cm−1) gradually weakened but did not disappear completely
(Figure 17b), indicating that S8 gradually participated in the
reaction but was not fully reacted. No new characteristic peaks
appeared during the process. During the charging process, the
Raman signal of S8 gradually increased, indicating that it was
eventually oxidized to S8 (Figure 17c). More importantly, at 50%
charge state, a characteristic peak of Li2S2 appeared at 438 cm−1,
and its intensity gradually increased and then decreased with
further charging. No characteristic peaks of other polysulﬁdes
appeared, indicating the formation of short-chain Li2S2 during
the reaction process, and no long-chain Li2Sn (2<n≤8) existed.
This result is consistent with the nonin situ XAFS test results.
TOF-SIMS uses an ion beam to bombard the sample surface,
causing surface molecules or atoms to detach from the sample
and form secondary ions. These secondary ions are accelerated
and enter the time-of-ﬂight mass spectrometer. By measuring
the ﬂight time and mass of the ions, information about the com-
position and structure of the sample surface can be obtained.
This technique can be used to analyze the diﬀusion behavior
of S elements and the composition of the CEI ﬁlm generated

7.1. Future Perspectives
Solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries, as a crucial candidate for
the next generation of high-energy-density energy storage sys-
tems, aim to achieve a gravimetric energy density exceeding
800 Wh kg−1 to meet the practical application requirements
in ﬁelds, such as electric vehicles and aerospace. To reach this
goal, breakthroughs are needed in multiple aspects: the sulfur
cathode must achieve high sulfur loading and utilization; the
lithium metal anode needs to maintain long-term stability; the
solid electrolyte should combine high ionic conductivity with
good mechanical properties; simultaneously, a stable interface
between the electrodes and electrolyte must be formed. These
challenges demand higher standards in material design, inter-
face engineering, and battery manufacturing processes. Looking
ahead, breakthroughs in solid-state lithium–sulfur battery tech-
nology may come from multiple innovative directions: the devel-
opment of novel solid electrolytes could fundamentally alter the
performance ceiling of batteries; performance optimization for
speciﬁc usage scenarios may open up entirely new application
ﬁelds; in-depth understanding of the impact of external pressure
on battery performance could provide crucial guidance for pro-
cess optimization; while the advancement of commercial appli-
cations may accelerate technological innovation across the entire
industry.
7.2. Research on Novel Solid Electrolytes
To achieve eﬃcient ion migration with high sulfur loading, devel-
oping highly conductive solid electrolytes has become a necessary
research direction. Theoretical analysis indicates that for solid-
state battery systems using intercalation-type cathode active ma-
terials, the ionic conductivity needs to reach at least 10 mS cm−1.
However, due to the presence of more insulating components in
all-solid-state lithium–sulfur battery systems, higher ionic con-
ductivity is required to ensure their performance. Building upon
oxide and sulﬁde solid-state batteries, researchers are focusing
on developing new components and ultrathin structures for solid
electrolytes.
In the pursuit of solid-state electrolytes with ultrahigh ionic
conductivity, oxohalide and sulfohalide materials have demon-
strated exceptional potential for application. For example, the
Li9.54[Si1−𝛿M𝛿]1.74P1.44S11.1Br0.3O0.6 (LSiMPSBrO, M = Ge, Sn)
solid electrolyte developed by Professor Ryoji Kanno’s team
achieves a volumetric ionic conductivity as high as 32 mS cm−1,
about three times that of the original LGPS.[150] Wang et al.
successfully synthesized a series of halogen-rich thio-argyrodite
compounds with the general formula Li5.5PS4.5ClxBr1.5−x (where
0≤x≤1.5). Among this series, Li5.5PS4.5Cl0.8Br0.7 (with a conﬁg-
urational entropy ΔSconf = 1.98R) showed exceptional perfor-
mance, achieving a remarkably high ionic conductivity of 22.7
mS cm−1 at room temperature.[151]
In addition to high ionic conductivity, solid electrolytes should
possess excellent mechanical properties, particularly good duc-
tility. This ductility is crucial for maintaining the stability of the
electrode/electrolyte interface, contributing to the suppression of
lithium dendrite growth and enhancing the cycle life of batteries.
Zhou et al. manufactured ﬂexible, ultrathin, and robust solid elec-
trolytes for solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries using electrospin-
ning technology. This method combines a PU&PVDF/LLZTO
composite skeleton with a PVDF-HFP/SN/LiTFSI mixed lithium
salt solution to prepare an 18 μm thick composite polymer elec-
trolyte (CPE). This CPE exhibits excellent mechanical proper-
ties, high ionic conductivity, and a wide electrochemical window,
while forming a stable SEI layer at the lithium anode interface.
Li|SPAN batteries based on this CPE maintain a capacity reten-
tion rate of 94.0% after 350 cycles at 0.5C.[152]
7.3. The Impact of Environmental Temperature on Performance
The operation of all-solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries across a
wide temperature range is crucial for speciﬁc application sce-
narios. However, low-temperature environments pose numer-
ous challenges to batteries, including slower electrochemical re-
action kinetics, reduced ionic conductivity, and decreased ion
diﬀusion capability in electrodes. To address these issues, re-
searchers have proposed various innovative strategies: For S elec-
trode modiﬁcation, Wang et al. designed a short-chain molecular
cathode S8-SeTe using an entropy regulation strategy. At −20 °C,
the battery demonstrated excellent cycle stability of 98.4% and
an electrode energy density of 394.9 Wh kg−1 after 100 cycles.
Even at an extreme low temperature of −40 °C, the battery could
still function normally, providing a discharge capacity of 579.6
mAh g−1.[153] For lithium anode modiﬁcation, Sun et al. intro-
duced trace amounts of solid-state catalysts into the lithium metal
anode, regulating the Li2S2/Li2S mixed discharge products. At
−10 °C, the initial discharge capacity was 336 mAh g−1.[22] Re-
search on solid electrolytes mainly focuses on optimizing room-
temperature ionic conductivity and reducing thermal sensitivity.
Currently, most studies are based on polymer-based solid elec-
trolytes. For example, by introducing hyperbranched polymer
plasticizers to construct dynamic crosslinked networks, a wide-
temperature amorphous phase structure has been achieved. This
type of quasisolid-state electrolyte with high solid content exhibits
excellent lithium-ion mobility, mechanical properties, and inter-
face stability under low-temperature conditions. However, break-
throughs are still needed in the application of inorganic solid

7.4. The Impact of External Pressure on Performance
External pressure on solid-state batteries falls into two cate-
gories: preparation pressure (tens to thousands of MPa) and
stack pressure (<10 MPa). Preparation pressure primarily af-
fects the microstructure of electrodes and electrolytes, such as
porosity and ionic conductivity; stack pressure optimizes the
interfacial contact between electrodes and solid electrolytes. The
choice of stack pressure depends on the physical properties
of materials, such as Young’s modulus and shear modulus.
Appropriate pressure can compensate for volume changes
during charge–discharge cycles, reduce interfacial resistance,
and ensure uniform current distribution. However, excessive
pressure may lead to localized stress concentration and po-
tentially cause unexpected lithium metal penetration into the
solid electrolyte layer, potentially triggering short circuits and
aﬀecting battery performance. Therefore, precise control and op-
timization of pressure parameters are crucial for enhancing the
electrochemical performance of batteries. Currently, there are
few studies on the impact of pressure on solid-state lithium–
sulfur battery performance, making related research on solid-
state lithium-ion batteries highly instructive. The mechanical
response characteristics of solid electrolytes signiﬁcantly de-
pend on material composition. Sulﬁde SEs (e.g., Li6PS5Cl)
exhibit lower elastic moduli (18–25 GPa) and excellent room-
temperature plastic deformation capabilities, being highly
sensitive to stress states. Optimizing preparation pressure (e.g.,
increasing from 50 to 370 MPa) can signiﬁcantly improve their
density, ion mobility, and electrochemical performance. In con-
trast, oxide SEs (such as Li7La3Zr2O12, LLZO) have higher elastic
moduli (about 150 GPa) and intrinsic brittleness, requiring
more precise pressure parameter control to avoid microstruc-
tural damage.[155] Soboyejo et al., based on the LiBH4-LiNH2
solid electrolyte system, studied the eﬀect of stack pressure on
the interfacial characteristics of all-solid-state batteries (ASSBs)
through computational models, ﬁnite element analysis, and ex-
perimental research. The study showed that moderate pressure
(0.26 MPa) can optimize interfacial contact, compress voids,
enhance ion transport while maintaining elastic deformation
of the electrolyte, achieving the highest room-temperature con-
ductivity of 9 × 10−5 S cm−1 at this pressure. However, when
pressure increases to 1 MPa, it causes signiﬁcant axial and shear
strain, potentially leading to interfacial yielding or cracking,
damaging the electrode structure and reducing electrochemical
performance. This reveals the importance of stack pressure
optimization in ASSB design, requiring a balance between im-
proving interfacial contact and avoiding mechanical damage.[155]
The choice of solid electrolyte system determines whether exter-
nal force is necessary. Based on current optimization strategies
for electrodes/interfaces in existing solid-state batteries, most
battery tests are still conducted without applied external pres-
sure. However, it is worth noting that external pressure can serve
as an innovative in situ electrochemical characterization method.
By applying diﬀerential pressure testing techniques (dP/dV),
researchers can obtain real-time electrochemical-pressure re-
sponse data for all-solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries, providing
crucial information for deciphering the reaction mechanisms of
sulfur cathodes.[156]
7.5. Industrial Applications
Flexible pack batteries, despite their larger eﬀective area, face
challenges with uneven current density distribution, potentially
reducing their lifespan. All-solid-state ﬂexible pack batteries dif-
fer markedly from conventional liquid systems in structure and
assembly due to their solid-state electrolytes (SSEs). Manufac-
turing processes vary based on the SSE type: roll-to-roll for ul-
trathin inorganic ﬁlms, and bipolar plate stacking for compos-
ite thick electrodes and self-diﬀusing electrodes.[157,158] Currently,
the stacking method is the most commonly used method for
assembling ﬂexible pack batteries on a laboratory scale. For ex-
ample, Wu et al. developed an all-solid-state lithium sulﬁde bat-
tery using the stacking method. When assembled with a com-
mercial silicon carbide cathode, the whole battery exhibits ex-
cellent performance: it retains more than 97% of its initial dis-
charge capacity after 400 cycles at room temperature. Notably,
this stacked system achieves a high energy density of over 600 Wh
kg−1.[56] To achieve the ambitious energy density target of 800 Wh
kg−1 by 2035 for ASSLSBs, precise control, and optimization of
key engineering parameters is essential. This requires the com-
ponents to meet stringent performance speciﬁcations: the cath-
ode needs to achieve a high sulfur loading (>4 mg cm−2) and a
high sulfur content (S/C ratio >55 wt%) to obtain an area capac-
ity of more than 6 mAh cm−2 and a sulfur utilization of 90%;
and the solid-state electrolyte must have a high ionic conductiv-
ity (at least 10 mS cm−1) and a very low electronic conductivity
(<10−8 S cm−1), and the thickness should be controlled below
30 μm; for the anode, the thickness of lithium metal should be
precisely controlled in the range of 10–50 μm, and the polariza-
tion of the electrode should be limited to below 1.2 V; moreover,
the capacity ratio of the negative electrode to the positive elec-
trode (N/P ratio) in the overall design of the battery should be kept
below 1.1.[159,18,32,82]
The commercialization of solid-state lithium–sulfur batter-
ies faces multiple process challenges, urgently requiring break-
throughs in several key areas. The primary diﬃculty lies in de-
veloping large-scale production technologies, necessitating the
transformation of laboratory-level manufacturing methods into
eﬃcient industrial production processes, especially for the prepa-
ration of solid electrolyte ﬁlms and composite electrodes, while
ensuring quality, improving eﬃciency, and reducing costs. In
electrode manufacturing, traditional slurry coating techniques
for liquid batteries are no longer applicable, urgently requiring
the development of new methods, such as improved aerosol
deposition or dry powder technology to achieve high-quality,
uniform electrode layers. Battery assembly also needs innova-
tion, developing specialized stacking, pressing, and packaging
techniques to ensure tight bonding of components and main-
tain good interfacial contact. Interface treatment is another key
challenge, requiring eﬃcient surface modiﬁcation or coating
technologies to reduce interfacial impedance and improve bat-
tery eﬃciency. During production, the compatibility and sta-
bility of various materials (such as sulfur, lithium metal, solid
