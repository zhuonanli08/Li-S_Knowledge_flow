A B S T R A C T  
High ionic conductivity and excellent lithium compatibility for sulfide solid electrolytes are vital to developing 
solid-state Li-metal batteries with high energy density and safety. Li7P2S8I has attracted significant attention due 
to its low cost, good stability towards lithium metal, and low annealing temperature. However, the low con­
ductivity compared to liquid electrolyte and poor stability with Li metal at high current density limits its ap­
plications in solid-state batteries. In this work, the conductivity of Li7P2S8I is first increased from 1.53 mS/cm to 
3.08 mS/cm by Cl- doping. The enhanced ionic conductivity is due to the introduction of S2-/Cl-/I- disorder 
structure in the structure. Then, the Li-ion conductivity of Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 is enhanced up to 6.67 mS/cm via 
optimizing the pellet-making pressures and temperatures with the hot-press technique. Because of the superior 
conductivity, the assembled Li2S-LiI/Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5/Li-In battery delivers initial discharge capacities of 1051.0 
mAh/g, 1385.0 mAh/g, and 713 mAh/g under 0.13 mAh/cm2 at room temperature, 60 ◦C, and 0 ◦C, respec­
tively. Moreover, the capacity contribution of the Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 electrolyte in cathode mixture are unraveled, 
which shows reversible charge/discharge more than 80 cycles and confirms the extra capacity from the elec­
trolytes in the cathode mixture. The hot-press Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 pellet shows excellent lithium compatibility and 
dendrite suppression capability, achieving stable lithium plating/stripping up to 280 h at 0.1 mA/cm2. The 
corresponding 3Li2S-LiI/Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5/Li battery delivers an initial discharge capacity of 474 mAh/g at 0.13 
mA/cm2 and capacity retention of 83% after 30 cycles. This work provides a promising strategy to explore sulfide 
electrolytes enabling solid-state Li-Metal battery.   
1. Introduction 
Li-S batteries have attracted significant attention in recent years due 
to their high energy density and low cost. [1–5] However, the current Li- 
S batteries using organic liquid electrolytes suffer from the shuttle 
phenomenon of those soluble polysulfide intermediates, leading to the 
loss of active materials and degrading electrochemical performances. 
[6–9] Moreover, the utilization of flammable liquid electrolytes also has 
potential safety issues. [10–13] Solid-state Li-S battery using inorganic 
solid electrolytes can both avoid the shuttle effect and enhance safety, 
posing great potential as candidates for next-generation energy storage 
devices. [14,15] One of the biggest challenges to developing solid-state 
Li-S batteries is to explore new kinds of solid electrolytes with high ionic 
conductivity. [16–18] Among the current inorganic solid electrolytes, 
sulfide solid electrolytes deliver high Li-ion conductivity and low grain 
boundary resistance, providing the possibility to fabricate high- 
performance solid-state Li-S batteries. [18,19]. 
Li7P2S8I with high ionic conductivity (~10-3 S/cm), cheap precursors 
and low annealing temperature becomes an important candidate in 
sulfide electrolytes. [20–22] Although Li7P2S8I shows higher conduc­
tivity than most sulfide electrolytes, which is still far smaller than that of 
current liquid electrolytes (~10-2 S/cm). Therefore, increasing the 
conductivity of Li7P2S8I can accelerate its application in solid-state 
batteries. Previous research has reported that the anion substitution of

S with halogen elements can enhance the Li-ion conductivity of sulfide 
solid electrolytes, such as lithium argyrodite and Li2S-P2S5 solid elec­
trolytes. [23] The introduction of halogens depleted the interaction 
between the lithium ions and the surrounding anion framework. The 
increased site disorder enlarges lithium vacancy and diffusivity, 
compared with solid electrolytes doped with a single halogen, those 
doped with two halogens showed higher ionic conductivity due to the 
introduction of disordered S2-/X- structures derived from extra halide. 
[24,25] Whether the replacement of I with other halogen anions has a 
similar effect is still a problem. Another typical route to increase the 
ionic conductivity of solid electrolytes is to decrease the grain boundary 
contribution. [26] Although sulfide electrolytes exhibit much smaller 
grain boundaries than other inorganic solid electrolytes, such as oxides 
and anti-perovskites [27–29], lowering the grain boundary leads to ul­
trafast Li-ion conductivity even comparable to that of current liquid 
electrolytes, such as the hot-pressed Li7P3S11 [30]. Unraveling the 
relationship between grain boundaries and ionic conductivities of 
modified Li7P2S8I electrolytes provide a clue to design compositions 
with higher conductivities. 
S has been chosen as the common active material for solid-state Li-S 
batteries, leading to poor battery performances due to its low electronic 
and ionic conductivities. [31] To develop more facile candidate active 
materials, Li2S with higher electronic/ ionic conductivities, comparable 
discharge capacity, and the potential utilization of lithium metal-free 
anodes have drawn significant attention for high-energy solid-state 
batteries. [32–34] Recently, our group found that introducing LiX (X =
Br, I) in Li2S leads to a mixture with superior ionic conductivity, yielding 
improved electrochemical performances for solid-state Li-S batteries. 
[35] Another issue for the Li-S battery is the large volume expansion 
during the conversion reactions, which could degrade the effective 
electrical contacts in the cathode mixture. [36] For Li-S batteries using 
liquid electrolytes, the fluidity electrolytes can build ionic conducting 
connections between different parts. While the three-dimensional con­
ducting framework in solid-state Li-S batteries is based on various types 
of solid–solid connections, leading to more severe volume variations of 
solid/solid contacts during cycling, especially under different operating 
temperatures. [37] However, the evaluations of solid/solid contacts and 
electrochemical performances of Li7P2S8I-based solid-state Li-S batteries 
operated at different temperatures are unclear. Revealing those working 
mechanisms is vital to constructing high-performance all-climate solid- 
state Li-S batteries. 
In addition, solid electrolytes are introduced to act as ionic conductor 
additives to increase ionic conductivity in the cathode mixture of solid- 
state batteries. [38,39] This is especially important for solid-state Li-S 
batteries due to the poor ionic conductivity of those sulfur species. [40] 
Previous research has reported that lithium argyrodite solid electrolytes 
in the cathode mixture of solid-state batteries can participate in the 
electrochemical reaction process and provide charge/discharge capac­
ities. [41] In contrast, whether Li7P2S8I and its analogous electrolytes 
have a contribution to the capacity in Li7P2S8I-based solid-state Li-S 
batteries is still unclear. More importantly, the operating temperature 
effect on the activity of Li7P2S8I solid electrolyte in the cathode mixture 
of solid-state batteries still needs to be unraveled. 
In this work, we propose increasing the Li-ion conductivity of 
Li7P2S8I with the strategy of utilizing Cl- to replace I-. The ion conduc­
tivities and morphologies of the Cl- modified solid electrolytes are sys­
tematically studied. The hot-press technique is applied to further 
enhance the conductivities and densities of the obtained electrolytes. 
Moreover, electrochemical performances at different operating tem­
peratures of the milled- and annealed-Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 with higher ion 
conductivities are investigated by fabricating solid-state Li-S batteries 
using Li2S-LiI cathode and Li-In anode. The assembled batteries deliver 
excellent capacity and cyclability. To unravel the capacity contribution 
of different components in the cathode mixture and verify whether the 
Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 electrolyte is involved in the electrochemical reactions, a 
facile solid-state battery with the structure of Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5-C/ 
Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5/Li-In is assembled. Multiple ex-situ characterization 
methods, such as XRD, TEM, XPS, and EIS, are utilized to identify the 
phase compositions and reveal the structural evolution during cycling. 
Both the role of Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 electrolyte in the cathode mixture and 
the corresponding phase transition are unraveled. Finally, the lithium 
compatibility and lithium dendrite suppression of the hot-pressed 
Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 is investigated. The obtained Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 exhibits 
excellent stability against lithium metal and capability for dendrite 
suppression. Moreover, the as-assembled 3Li2S-LiI/Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5/Li 
solid-state Li-S battery delivers high discharge capacity and excellent 
cyclability under large current density. 
2. Results and discussion 
The phase identification of the milled- and annealed Li7P2S8I and 
Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 (denoted as LPSI and LPSI0.5Cl0.5, respectively) were 
characterized by powder XRD. The milled Li7P2S8I and Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 
show similar XRD diffraction peaks. As shown in Fig. 1a. The XRD peaks 
located at 21◦of samples are assigned to the diffraction peaks of the 
protective film to separate the contact with air/moisture. No diffraction 
peaks can be observed in the XRD patterns of those milled Li7P2S8I and 
Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 electrolytes, suggesting that the amorphous glass is 
formed after the milling process. After annealing at 180 ◦C, the char­
acteristic diffraction peaks indexed to the thio-LISICON II analog phase 
at 20.2◦, 23.6◦, 29.5◦are observed in the pattern of both annealed 
samples, indicating that introducing Cl has ignorable effect on the 
structure of Li7P2S8I solid electrolyte. SEM morphologies of the annealed 
Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 electrolyte are also characterized, and the results are 
shown in Fig. 1(b-c). Homogenous aggregates with sizes larger than 30 
μm is observed in the SEM images. As shown in Fig. 1d and Table S1, EDS 
mapping results confirm the homogenous distribution of S, Cl, P, and I in 
the obtained materials, suggesting that Cl has been uniformly doped into 
LPSI at stoichiometric ratio. Moreover, HRTEM images of the sintered 
Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 electrolyte as well as corresponding SAED pattern are 
also characterized, as shown in Fig. 1(e-h). The high-resolution trans­
mission electron microscopy (HRTEM) image of Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 in 
Fig. 1g (area 1 of Fig. 1f) possesses clear lattice fringes with interplanar 
distances of 0.295 nm, which corresponding to the diffraction peak at 
29.5◦. The structure of Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 is depicted in Fig. S2 (a, b). Two 
kinds of iodine ions, nonbinding or binding with P, are observed in the 
figure. Only Li-ions surround the nonbinding I- in the structure, while 
the binding I- ions locate at the PS3I0.5Cl0.5 tetrahedron site. Based on 
previous paper, Li-ions show a self-diffusion mode in the structure of X- 
modified Li7P2S8I and prefers to move along the c-axis rather than the a 
and b-axes. [42] As illustrated in Fig. S2c, Li ions move mostly around 
iodine ions, and iodine ions provide additional paths for Li ion diffusion. 
[43]. 
Ionic conductivities of the prepared samples were tested using the AC 
impedance. As shown in Fig. 2(a-b), the total resistances of the annealed 
Li7P2S8I electrolyte are 139 Ω at 0 ◦C, 60.9 Ω at 25 ◦C, 20.8 Ω at 70 ◦C, 
while the resistances of the heated Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 electrolyte at those 
corresponding recording temperatures are 75.6 Ω, 30.2 Ω, and 6.6 Ω, 
respectively. The smaller resistances for Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 compared to 
that of Li7P2S8I electrolyte indicates that introducing Cl in the structure 
can greatly reduce the total resistances of Li7P2S8I. The Li-ion conduc­
tivities of those materials are deduced from the impedance results and 
depicted in Fig. 2c and Fig. S1. The ionic conductivities of the obtained 
Li7P2S8I are 0.64, 1.53, and 4.47 mS/cm at 0, 25, and 70 ◦C. While the 
corresponding conductivities for Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 electrolyte are 1.24, 
3.08, and 9.79 mS/cm, respectively. The conductivity of the prepared 
Li7P2S8I in this work is comparable to that in published papers (1.53 vs. 
1.57 mS/cm), suggesting the successful synthesis of the target phase. 
[44] The annealed Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 delivers much higher conductivities 
than the pristine Li7P2S8I at different measuring temperatures, sug­
gesting that Cl-doping is an effective modification method to enhance 
the Li-ion conductivity.

Previous research has also reported that the anion disorder can 
greatly enhance the ionic conductivity of sulfide electrolytes [45,46], 
which agrees well with our results. It should be mentioned that the ionic 
conductivity of Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 at 70 ◦C is quite close to that of the 
current liquid electrolyte (9.79 vs. 10 mS/cm), indicating its great po­
tential in solid-state battery applications. The activation energy for ionic 
conduction of inorganic solid electrolytes can be deduced based on 
temperature-dependent ion conductivities. The conductivity values 
recorded at different temperatures follow the Arrhenius behavior. The 
corresponding Ea for Li7P2S8I and Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 electrolytes are 0.24 
and 0.34 eV, respectively. Typically, a lower Ea value means more 
polarized crystal lattice and faster ion migration [47], while in this work 
Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 delivers higher Li-ion conductivity and lower activation 
energy than the pristine Li7P2S8I, suggesting that Ea is not the only factor 
affecting ionic conductivity. The ionic conductivity was commonly 
characterized by pressing the obtained power materials into pellets for 
measuring. Therefore, the pressure and handling temperature play a 
vital role in the conductivity results. To investigate the pressure effect, 
different pressing pressures were applied to make the pellets. As shown 
in Fig. 3, many big cracks are observed in the SEM images of the surface 
sections for those pellets pressed under 10 and 80 MPa in Fig. 3a and 
Fig. S4a, while fewer cracks can be observed when the pressure is 
increased to 150 MPa in Fig. 3b. At even higher pressures, such as 250 
MPa in Fig. S4b, only a few cracks with much narrower gaps are 
observed in the SEM images. When the pressure is increased up to 350 
MPa, no obvious cracks can be observed in Fig. 3c, suggesting the for­
mation of a dense pellet with a higher density, all of those measurements 
were performed at 35 ◦C. The ionic conductivity variation of those ob­
tained pellets in Fig. 3d also supports our conclusions. The total Li-ion 
conductivities are 0.9, 1.7, 2.2, 2.75, and 3.08 mS/cm for 10, 80, 150, 
250 and 350 MPa, respectively. When the pressure is high enough to 
ensure the contact between different particles in the pellet, such as 250 
and 360 MPa, the pressure exhibits insignificant influence in the Li-ion 
conductivity, as shown in Fig. 3d.

Temperature is another critical factor that decides the durability of 
the solid electrolyte pellet. Hot-pressing is the typical method to control 
the temperature under a fixed pressure during the pelleting process. 
Based on above results, 350 MPa pressure is fixed in this work and the 
hot-pressing temperature varies from room temperature to 180 ◦C. Clear 
cracks are observed in the SEM images of the pellet at room temperature, 
which reduce as a function of increasing temperatures, as shown in 
Fig. 3(e-h). A dense pellet without cracks is obtained when the tem­
perature increases up to 180 ◦C in Fig. 3g. The densities of the hot- 
pressed pellets are 1.58, 1.59, 1.61, 1.63, and 1.65 g/cm3 at 30, 50, 
100, 150, and 180 ◦C, respectively (Fig. 3h). The densities of pellets 
increase with the increasing temperatures (Fig. S4e), which agrees well 
with the SEM results that the pellets obtained at higher temperatures 
exhibit fewer cracks and gaps in the surface. Moreover, the corre­
sponding Li-ion conductivities of those pellets are 3.35, 3.32, 4.12, 4.85, 
and 6.67 mS/cm for 30, 50, 100, 150 and 180 ◦C, respectively. Li-ion 
conductivity shows a clear increase as a function of the increasing 
temperatures. Additionally, the hot-pressed pellet at 180 ◦C shows much 
higher ionic conductivity than Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 annealed at the same 
temperature. Electrochemical stability of the obtained LPSI0.5Cl0.5 glass 
ceramic electrolytes was characterized with cyclic voltammetry (CV) on 
the constructed Li/ LPSI0.5Cl0.5/stainless steel asymmetric cell. As 
depicted in Fig. S3, the corresponding cathodic and anodic currents due 
to the lithium plating (Li+ + e- → Li) and the lithium stripping processes 
(Li → Li+ + e-) are detected near 0 V. Moreover, an oxidation peak is 
observed when the voltage is higher than 3.6 V, suggesting that the 
upper stable voltage for LPSI0.5Cl0.5 is 3.6 V. Therefore, the obtained 
LPSI0.5Cl0.5 shows a stable voltage window of 0–3.6 V based on the CV 
results. Considering the voltage window applied for all-solid-state Li-S 
batteries, [33,34] 0.4–3.0 V (vs. Li-In) was chosen as a suitable 
charging/discharging voltage window for the assembled battery in this 
work. 
Our previous research has reported that Li2S-LiI is a promising 
cathode for solid-state Li-S batteries. [35] The highest ionic conductivity 
is achieved when Li2S and LiI are mixed in a ratio of 3–1. [48] The KB- 
800 is a type of conductive carbon, which has a unique branch chain 
shape. The advantage of this form is that the conductive body has many 
conductive contact points and branch chains form many conductive 
paths, so conductive carbon KB-800 was chosen for the cathode mixture 
to enhance its electronic conductivity. However, the existence of carbon 
also accelerates the instability of sulfide electrolytes in the cathode 
mixture.[16] The addition of solid electrolytes in the cathode mixture is 
also vital to increase the ionic conductivity of cathode mixture, but solid 
electrolyte in the cathode mixture is not the effective capacity contrib­
utor for the whole battery. To balance the weight ratio of those three 
different components in the cathode mixture plays a key role in the 
battery performance. Considering the high ionic conductivity of Li7P2­
S8I0.5Cl0.5(or Li7P2S8I) and the experience about constructing all-solid- 
state Li-S batteries in our group, the final ratio of 5:4:1 (3Li2S-LiI: 
Solid-electrolytes:KB-800) was chosen. Therefore, all-solid-state Li-S 
batteries consisted of the milled 3Li2S-LiI and the prepared Li7P2S8I and 
Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 electrolytes were fabricated to evaluate the electro­
chemical performances of obtained solid electrolytes under different 
operating temperatures. The Li-ion conductivity of the 3Li2S-LiI-Li7P2­
S8I0.5Cl0.5-C and 3Li2S-LiI-Li7P2S8I-C cathode mixture at different tem­
peratures is shown in Fig. S5, The Nyquist plots of 3–1 Li2S-LiI, 
Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5(or Li7P2S8I) and C ball-milled at the molar ratio of 5:4:1 
show an obvious half-circle in the low-frequency range at 0 ◦C, which is 
due to the interfacial resistance between electrolytes and Li-In anode at 
low temperature. Moreover, due to higher conductivity of Li7P2­
S8I0.5Cl0.5, the bulk impedance of 3Li2S-LiI@LPSI0.5Cl0.5@C is smaller 
than that of 3Li2S-LiI@LPSI@C. As shown in Fig. 4(a-b), both batteries 
show only one charge plateaus around 2.1 V vs. Li-In at 25 ◦C, which is 
associated with the phase conversion from Li2S to S8. [49] During the 
subsequent discharge process in the first cycle, three discharge plateaus 
at ~1.8 V, ~1.5 V and ~0.55 V are observed in those figures. The first 
discharge region (1.9–1.65 V) is assigned to the reduction of elemental 
sulfur to form polysulfides and reduction of the polysulfides. The second 
discharge region (1.65–0.7 V) belongs to reduction of polysulfides to 
Li2S [50]. Moreover, according to our assumption, the third discharge 
region (0.7–0.4 V) is attributed to the decomposition of Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 
electrolyte, and the analysis will be given later. At a higher operating 
temperature (60 ◦C), both batteries exhibit lower charge plateau (~2.0 
V) and higher discharge plateaus during the 1st charge/discharge pro­
cesses. On the contrary, when the temperature decreases to 0 ◦C, they 
show higher charge plateaus and lower discharge plateaus, which is due 
to the cell polarization at lower temperatures [51]. Moreover, sulfide 
electrolytes show narrow electrochemical voltage windows based on the 
simulation results. [12] Our experiment results in this work also confirm 
this conclusion. We found the reduction of the target Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 
electrolyte during the cycling process is mainly attributed to the 
excessive charging/discharging voltage windows. Based on the calcu­
lation results, Li7P2S8I exhibits a stable voltage range of 1.8–2.2 V (vs. 
Li+/Lio) [43]. However, the working voltage chosen for all-solid-state Li- 
S batteries is in the range of 0.4–3.0 V. The assembled 3Li2S-LiI/ 
Li7P2S8I/Li-In battery delivers an initial discharge capacity of 597, 1028, 
and 1449 mAh/g at 0, 25 and 60 ◦C, respectively. While the corre­
sponding battery using Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 with a superior Li-ion conduc­
tivity shows slightly higher discharge capacities of 713, 1051, and 1385 
mAh/g at 0, 25 and 60 ◦C, respectively. In Fig. 4b, the discharge

3. Conclusion 
In summary, we demonstrate that introducing Cl- to substitute I- in 
the structure of Li7P2S8I can greatly enhance the conductivity. The 
impedance results show that the obtained Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 delivered two 
times higher conductivity than that of the pristine Li7P2S8I (3.08 vs. 1.53 
mS/cm). Solid-state Li-S batteries using the synthesized Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 
electrolyte combined with 3Li2S-LiI cathode and Li-In anode deliver 
much higher discharge capacities, better rate capability, and superior 
cyclability than that using the bare Li7P2S8I electrolyte at different 
operating temperatures, which is attributed to the improved conduc­
tivity. SEM results confirm that the electrochemical performance dif­
ference is also associated with solid–solid interface contacts. The 
capacity contribution of the Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 electrolyte is unraveled in 
the cathode mixture during cycling. Ex-situ characterization including 
XRD, TEM, and XPS results suggest that the Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 electrolyte is 
firstly decomposed to LiI, Li2S, and P, and then the yielded Li2S act as the 
active material to participate in the following cycles. An optimized hot- 
press process can further enhance the conductivity of the Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 
pellet up to 6.67 mS/cm. On the other hand, the increased density of this 
pellet yields excellent lithium compatibility and dendrite suppression 
capability. The assembled 3Li2S-LiI/Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5/Li solid-state Li- 
metal battery delivers an initial discharge capacity of 474 mAh/g at 
0.13 mA/cm2 and sustained 83% of the capacity after 30 cycles. This 
work provides a strategy for achieving both high ionic conductivity and 
optimal lithium dendritic suppressing ability between sulfide electro­
lytes and Li anode, thus promoting the development of all-solid-state Li 
metal batteries.
