ABSTRACT
All-solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) offer exceptional promise for next-generation energy storage due to their high
energy density and intrinsic safety. However, their practical application is hindered by sluggish sulfur redox kinetics and severe
interfacial degradation. Here, we report a sulfur|carbon and sulfur|solid electrolytes (SEs) interfaces-dominant cathode structure
that concurrently enhances sulfur redox kinetics and interfacial stability. Conventional sulfur cathodes rely on randomly mixed
sulfur|carbon, sulfur|SEs, and carbon|SEs three-phase boundaries, which hinder efficient sulfur redox. This work presents an
interfacial architecture that is realized through carbon host nanostructure engineering, where tailored surface area and porosity
favor the spontaneous formation of sulfur|carbon and sulfur|SEs dual interfaces, thereby establishing complementary Li+ and e−
pathways and reinforcing electrochemical performance. At the same time, this strategy alleviates the formation of carbon|SEs
interfaces, thereby blocking the initiators of electrolyte decomposition. As a result, ASSLSBs employing the dual-interface-
dominant architecture achieve a high initial capacity of 1111 mAh g−1 at 0.2 C with a sulfur loading of 5 mg cm−2, and exhibit
long-term cycling stability, retaining 1234 mAh g−1 (93.3%) over 100 cycles at a 0.1 C rate. This work highlights the critical role of
rational carbon host engineering in constructing well-defined interfaces for high-performance ASSLSBs.

Introduction
Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) based on intercalation-type elec-
trodes are approaching their theoretical energy density limits
(240–250 Wh kg−1), which are insufficient to meet the grow-
ing demands of high-energy storage applications. In contrast,
lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries offer a significantly higher the-
oretical energy density of 2600 Wh kg−1 and specific capacity
of 1672 mAh g−1, along with the advantages of low cost and
natural abundance of sulfur, making them one of the most
promising candidates for next-generation batteries [1–3]. Despite
these advantages, the practical application of liquid-state Li-S bat-
teries is plagued by lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) dissolution from
sulfur cathodes [4–6]. Replacing the flammable liquid organic
electrolyte with inorganic solid electrolytes (SEs) fundamentally
alters the sulfur redox mechanism by enabling a direct solid-solid
conversion between S8 and Li2S, thereby eliminating capacity loss
associated with intermediate LiPSs shuttling [7–9]. Additionally,
SEs greatly enhance the safety of Li-S batteries by mitigating
flammability, high volatility, and leakage risks inherent to liquid

electrolytes, as well as the potential to safely use Li metal anode
in batteries [10, 11].
Among various SEs, the sulfide-based SEs, such as argyrodites
Li6PS5Cl (LPSC), are considered very promising SEs for the
development of ASSLSBs due to their high ionic conductivity,
mechanical compliance, and chemical compatibility with sulfur
cathodes [12–14]. On the other hand, the solid-state system also
brings many challenges to the design of sulfur cathodes in the
sulfide SEs-based ASSLSBs. The first challenge is the insulating
nature of sulfur, a problem also encountered in liquid-based
batteries [15, 16]. Therefore, sulfur needs to be accompanied by
a large amount of electronic conductive agent, such as carbon
materials [17, 18]. This challenge has been well studied for two
decades in liquid-based Li-S batteries with the investigation
of various nanostructured carbon materials. It is commonly
agreed that a range of micro- to mesopores (< 10 nm) along
with high surface area (hundreds to over thousands m2 g−1)
of carbon nanomaterials is beneficial for sulfur cathodes with
uniform distribution, sufficient electronic conductivity, and self-
confinement in carbon matrix to avoid LiPSs dissolution [1,
19–21]. Unfortunately, these experiences cannot directly apply
to the sulfur cathodes in ASSLSBs with the new interfacial
challenges [22]. Furthermore, most sulfide SEs possess a narrow
electrochemical stability window in the range of 1.7–2.2 V vs.
Li/Li+ [23–27]. Researchers have reported that the decomposition
of LPSC typically occurs simultaneously with the redox of the
active materials S8/Li2S [28–30]. This results in the continu-
ous consumption of the electrolyte and the accumulation of
unwanted decomposition products, which deteriorate the cell
cycle life [31, 32]. More importantly, intimate contact between
carbon and SEs exacerbates this decomposition, since carbon,
as an electronic conductor, may further facilitate the SEs redox
reactions [1, 33, 34] Considerable efforts have been devoted to
resolving these issues, including interfacial engineering of the
carbon surface and the intentional reduction of its electronic con-
ductivity [35, 36], strategies that are conceptually straightforward
and experimentally feasible. However, sacrificing the conduc-
tivity of carbon is neither desirable nor necessary. Achieving a
balance between efficient electron conduction and suppression of
interfacial side reactions is therefore crucial. To this end, rational
nanoscale engineering on the carbon|sulfur|SEs interfaces offers
a promising approach to reconcile these competing requirements.
In carbon, sulfur, and SEs three-component-based sulfur cath-
odes, there are typically three types of interfaces: sulfur|carbon,
sulfur|SEs, and carbon|SEs [37, 38], It is easy to understand that
the interfaces of sulfur|carbon and sulfur|SEs are favorable to
build an architecture that enables simultaneous and extensive
contact of active materials with both Li+/e−conduction networks
[39]. On the other hand, the interface of carbon|SEs is detrimen-
tal because of SEs’ electrochemical degradation as mentioned
before. However, the three types of boundaries—sulfur|carbon,
sulfur|SEs, and carbon|SEs are generated randomly and uncon-
trollably since the cathode composite is usually mixed using a
rough traditional mechanical method. At the same time, carbon
nanomaterials not only act as electronic conductors but also host
materials that support sulfur uniform dispersion at the nanoscale
and volume change compensator during cycling. Therefore, it is
important to select suitable carbon hosts and rationally design
the composite electrode architecture to limit unwanted side
reactions, optimize the electrochemical activity of S8/Li2S, as well
as provide uniform dispersion and volume buffering, which are
critical but still remain unsolved in ASSLSBs.
In this study, we investigate the nanostructure effect of carbon
nanomaterials in sulfur cathodes to design a sulfur cathode
with selective sulfur|carbon and sulfur|SEs interfaces while
minimizing the carbon|SEs interface. Departing from con-
ventional approaches that focus on constructing a complete
sulfur|carbon|SEs three-phase boundary, our strategy restricts
interfacial formation to two essential interfaces: sulfur|carbon
and sulfur|SEs by employing a structured carbon host with
an appropriate structure. This rational design facilitates the
spontaneous formation of a sulfur-mediated interphase, in which
the electrochemical transformation of elemental sulfur is driven
by two electron- and ion-conductive frameworks, while the
electrolyte decomposition is alleviated by reducing the formation
of carbon|SEs boundary. Such an architecture ensures efficient
dual-path transport, thereby accelerating sulfur redox kinetics. As
a result, the ambient-temperature ASSLSBs with dual-interface-
dominant architecture reach a high initial discharge capacity of
1111 mAh g−1 at 0.2 C rate with a high sulfur loading of 5 mg cm−2.
Additionally, the cells maintain a superior discharge capacity of
1234 mAh g−1 (93.3%) at a 0.1 C rate over 100 cycles.

Results and Discussion
2.1
Structural Characterizations of Carbon Hosts
and Carbon/Sulfur (C/S) Composites
In ASSLSBs, carbon hosts are indispensable as electron conduc-
tors and structural supports. To evaluate the effect of carbon
structure on the performance of sulfur cathode in ASSLSBs,
three types of porous carbon hosts with various specific surface
areas and pore volumes were selected, including BP800 (214.9
m2 g−1, 0.720 cm3 g−1 total pore volume), BP1300 (525.5 m2 g−1,
0.826 cm3 g−1 total pore volume), and BP2000 with increased
specific surface area and pore volume (1485.5 m2 g−1, 2.763
cm3 g−1 total pore volume), as summarized in Table S1. Figure
S1a displays the nitrogen adsorption/desorption isotherms for
pristine carbon hosts. All the carbon hosts exhibit a typical IV
type isotherm with a hysteresis loop at relatively high pressures,
indicating the presence of mesopores. Among them, BP2000
shows the highest sorption capacity, consistent with its excep-
tionally large specific surface area and pore volume. Moreover,
a sharp sorption increase at P/Po ∼0 for BP2000 reflects more
extensive micropores filling than BP800 and BP1300. BP1300
displays an intermediate sorption volume, whereas BP800 shows
the lowest uptake, in line with its smallest specific surface area
and pore volume. The corresponding pore size distributions
(Figure S2a–c) further reveal the distinct hierarchical porosity of
the three carbons. BP800 shows a dominant mesopore distribu-
tion in the range of 10–50 nm, accompanied by a small percentage
of micropores at around 0.6 nm. BP1300 exhibits a coexistence
of micro- and mesopores with a wider distribution, reflecting its
more developed internal porosity. In contrast, BP2000 is strongly
enriched in micropores, as evidenced by the significant peaks
at 0.5 nm and 0.6 nm, while also containing a percentage of
macropores (> 50 nm), which is attributed to the inter-particle
voids rather than intrinsic pores within the BP2000 matrix.

Three C/S composites containing ∼70% sulfur were obtained by
hand grinding followed by a melt-diffusion method. After sulfur
infiltration, all three C/S composites (BP800/S, BP1300/S, and
BP2000/S) exhibited significant decrease in specific surface area
and pore volume (Table S1), together with a drastic reduction in
sorption capacity of porous carbons across micro- and mesopores
(as shown in Figures S1b and S2d–f), indicating that sulfur
effectively penetrates and occupies the pores of carbon hosts,
forming close surface-to-surface contact between sulfur and
carbon [40]. It is worth noting that even after the encapsulation
of sulfur, the specific surface area and pore volume of BP2000/S
(44.4 m2 g−1, 0.449 cm3 g−1 total pore volume) are still much
higher than those of BP800/S (3.7 m2 g−1, 0.055 cm3 g−1 total pore)
and BP1300/S (3.9 m2 g−1, 0.104 cm3 g−1 total pore volume) (Figure
S1b and Table S1), which indicates that more free surface area
or voids still exist in the BP2000/S composites. In addition, the
nanostructure of carbon hosts will also affect the porosity and
thickness of the electrodes. Figure S3 demonstrates the cross-
section of the pressed sulfur electrodes with the use of BP800/S
and BP2000/S by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). It clearly
illustrates that at the same mass, same component ratio, and the
same pressure, the carbon host with higher porosity and surface
area (BP2000/S) makes the electrode thicker, which is attributed
to the void spaces within the electrodes, thereby reducing the
volumetric energy density of ASSLSBs.
The actual sulfur contents of BP800/S, BP1300/S, and BP2000/S
composites were determined by thermogravimetric analysis
(TGA) in N2 at a heating rate of 5 ◦C min−1, and the results
are presented in Figure S4. For comparison, the evaporation
behavior of elemental sulfur was also measured. The elemental
sulfur starts to evaporate at ca. 130 ◦C and is complete at ca.
250 ◦C. The measured sulfur contents of BP800/S, BP1300/S, and
BP2000/S composites all agree with the nominal sulfur content of
∼70 wt.%. However, the sulfur evaporation processes in BP800/S,
BP1300/S, and BP2000/S exhibit two distinct weight loss steps.
The first weight loss step ends at ca. 260 ◦C for BP800/S, ca.
250 ◦C for BP1300/S, and ca. 231 ◦C for BP2000/S, respectively,
which are almost overlapped with the evaporation curve of
elemental sulfur. This is attributed to the evaporation of elemental
sulfur without porous structure confinement (on carbon surface
or in big pores). The second weight loss steps occur at much
higher temperatures, ending at ca. 337 ◦C, 340 ◦C, and 363 ◦C
for BP800/S, BP1300/S, and BP2000/S, respectively. Notably, the
second evaporation stage is significantly slower than the first
stage, as pore-confined sulfur is subject to the capillary force and
therefore requires a larger driving force and a higher sublimation
temperature [41, 42]. Based on the separation of evaporation
processes, we estimated the contents of sulfur loss for each stage.
The mass loss of sulfur in the first stage can be attributed to the
sulfur content associated with the non-pore-confined sulfur, and
the mass loss of sulfur in the second stage can be considered
as pore-confined sulfur content. A comparison of the calculated
results is shown in Figure S5. BP800/S shows the largest amount
of non-pore-confined sulfur content, and BP2000/S shows the
smallest amount of non-pore-confined sulfur content owing to its
extensive porous structure. The reduced pore-confined sulfur in
BP800/S is favorable for maximizing the formation of the desired
sulfur|SEs interface, enhancing the fast sulfur redox reaction
kinetics in sulfur cathodes.
X-ray diffraction (XRD) was conducted to detect the crystal
structure of sulfur in C/S composites. According to the XRD
profiles of elemental sulfur, BP800/S, and BP2000/S in Figure
S6, the relative peak intensity of sulfur in BP2000/S is much
weaker than that of elemental sulfur and BP800/S, which might
be due to the smaller crystallite size of sulfur in BP2000/S
[43, 44]. Raman spectroscopy was also used to characterize the
structure of three pure carbons and C/S composites (Figure
S7). All Raman spectra of three carbons exhibit two peaks
corresponding to the D band and G band. The intensity ratio of
ID/IG is commonly used as an indicator of structural disorder in
carbon materials and differs markedly among the three carbons.
BP800 shows the lowest ID/IG ratio, whereas BP2000 exhibits the
highest, with BP1300 lying in between. This indicates that BP2000
contains a larger proportion of amorphous carbon structure,
while BP800 possesses a more graphitized carbon structure. After
the encapsulation of sulfur (Figure S7b), the Raman spectra of
BP800/S exhibit pronounced peaks corresponding to crystalline
sulfur in the range of 100–500 cm−1. In contrast, the intensities
of those sulfur-related peaks for BP1300/S become weaker and
are nearly absent in BP2000/S, indicating that the reduced sulfur
crystallinity and successful deeper confinement of sulfur within
the pores of BP1300 and BP2000 [44]. In addition, all three
C/S composites exhibit an increased ID/IG ratio after sulfur
loading, indicating that sulfur infiltration and thermal treatment
introduce additional structural disorder in the carbon matrix.
Nevertheless, BP800/S still maintains the lowest ID/IG value
among the three composites, confirming that it retains the highest
degree of graphitization even after sulfur encapsulation. The
graphitized carbons exhibit more stable interfacial compatibility
with sulfide SEs than amorphous carbon in solid-state batteries,
as demonstrated by previous studies [40, 45, 46]. To identify
the surface functional groups of the three carbon hosts, Fourier
transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy was conducted, as pre-
sented in Figure S8. BP2000 shows FT-IR peaks at 1071, 1149,
1380, 1556, 2883, and 2980 cm−1, corresponding to C─O, C─O─C,
O─H, C═C, C─H, and C─H bonds [47–49]. In contrast, most of
the FT-IR peaks assigned to C-H bond, and oxygen-containing
C─O, C─O─C, O─H bonds observed in BP2000 are absent in
BP800 and BP1300, indicating fewer surface functional groups
for BP800 and BP1300. The fewer functional groups on BP800
and BP1300 alleviated the occurrence of oxygen-containing func-
tional groups-induced electrolyte decomposition and unstable
interfaces, which is preferable for improved electrochemical
performance [46, 50].
In a liquid-state Li-S battery system, continuous Li+ transport
is ensured by electrolyte wetting and infiltration, coupled with
all these characteristics of BP2000/S composites, such as the
high surface area of carbon, leading to highly dispersed sulfur
into porous structure; and high porosity of electrodes, which
facilitates the sulfur utilization, fast Li+/e−transport, and reduces
the shuttle effect [51, 52]. However, in solid-state batteries, the
inclusion of SEs in sulfur-based electrodes turns this story
differently. Firstly, some reported literature has demonstrated
that the use of different carbon materials may lead to varying
degrees of sulfide SEs decomposition in solid-state Li-ion bat-
teries [34, 53]. Also, there is the question of how the highly
porous structure will affect the interface formation in the sulfur
electrodes.

2.2
Electrochemical Performance: Liquid vs.
Solid-State Li-S Batteries at 0.1 C
To demonstrate the effects of BP800, BP1300, and BP2000 on the
electrochemical behaviors of sulfur in liquid-state and solid-state
systems, liquid-state Li-S coin cells and solid-state Li-S model
cells were assembled by using BP800, BP1300, and BP2000 as
carbon hosts in sulfur cathodes, and the electrochemical perfor-
mance was tested and compared under the same current density,
as shown in Figure 1. Figure 1a–d show the cycling performance
and the typical two-plateau and single-plateau charge-discharge
curves of liquid-state and solid-state batteries at 0.1 C, respectively
[54–57]. The BP2000-based liquid-state Li-S batteries exhibited
the highest capacity compared with BP800- and BP1300-based
Li-S batteries, which is attributed to the better distribution and
decreased size of sulfur particles [58–60]. The capacity retention
of BP2000/S electrode is 694 mAh g−1 after 100 cycles, which
is higher than BP1300/S (468 mAh g−1) and BP800/S (292 mAh
g−1), which suggests that BP2000’s smaller pore size is more
suitable for the confinement of soluble LiPSs, the accommodation
of volume change of S8/Li2S, and thus impeded the loss of active
materials and capacity decay [61].
Interestingly, the three C/S composites exhibit completely dif-
ferent effects on electrochemical performance in solid-state
Li-S systems (Figure 1b,d). In the initial cycles at 0.1 C, all
three carbon hosts-based electrodes exhibit a ultra-high specific
capacity which almost reach or exceeded the theoretical specific
capacity of sulfur where BP2000/S (1788 mAh g−1 at the 4th cycle)
> BP1300/S (1737 mAh g−1 at the 4th cycle) > BP800/S (1611
mAh g−1 at the 4th cycle), which shares the trend from liquid-
state Li-S batteries. However, the trend changed to BP800/S >
BP1300/S > BP2000/S in subsequent cycles. After 100 cycles,
BP800/S exhibited the most stable cycling performance and
higher capacity retention (1234 mAh g−1, 93.3% capacity retention
at the 100th cycle) compared to BP1300/S (985 mAh g−1, 63.6%
capacity retention at the 100th cycle) and BP2000/S (916 mAh
g−1, 58.5% capacity retention at the 100th cycle) electrodes. Since
the electrode volume is one of the decisive factors governing
volumetric energy density, we evaluated the volumetric energy
densities of BP800/S and BP2000/S on the basis of their electrode
thicknesses (Figure S3) and the corresponding specific capacities
after 100 cycles. The resulting volumetric energy densities of
BP800/S and BP2000/S cathodes were determined to be 441 and
290 Wh L−1, respectively (Note S1). These results demonstrate
that a highly porous carbon host, which is often considered
advantageous in liquid systems, no longer confers benefits in
the solid-state configuration. On the contrary, it directly leads
to inferior volumetric energy density. From these results, several
important questions are raised: 1. What are the reasons for
the higher capacity of sulfur electrodes in solid-state batteries
compared to liquid-state batteries? 2. What causes the electro-
chemical performance differences with the use of different porous
carbon? 3. Do different types of carbon materials exert distinct
influences on the formation of the three interfaces (sulfur|carbon,
sulfur|SEs, and carbon|SEs)?

severe electrolyte decomposition caused by BP2000 likely arises
because of a higher specific surface area and lower volumetric
density, which leads to more formation of carbon|SEs interfaces,
thus more severe electrolyte decomposition and capacity decay.
As discussed before, the surface area of the C/S composites is in
the order of BP2000/S > BP1300/S > BP800/S. In other words,
the BP2000/S composite still has extra unoccupied surface area
and more free spaces, contributing to more carbon|SEs contacts.
Hence, the initial high capacity caused by the BP2000/S cathodes
under 0.1 C can be explained by the following reasons: 1. Similar
to the liquid system, BP2000 facilitates a more homogeneous
dispersion of sulfur, as well as the formation of smaller sulfur
particles, thus enabling sufficient electron channels at the begin-
ning of cycling. 2. Besides offering an electronic pathway for
sulfur, the larger surface area of BP2000/S gives rise to more
abundant carbon|SEs contacts, enabling a pathway for electrolyte
decomposition.
The evolution of interfacial chemistry of sulfur-free and sulfur-
containing cathodes with BP800 and BP2000 at the fully dis-
charged/charged states was investigated by X-ray adsorption
spectroscopy (XAS). Sulfur-free electrodes are composed of car-
bon hosts (BP800 or BP2000) mixed with acetylene black (AB)
and LPSC electrolyte, and sulfur-containing electrodes are the
same recipe but with the addition of sulfur. Firstly, XAS was con-
ducted on the BP carbon|LPSC cathodes after discharge/charge
cycling tests to reveal the intrinsic decomposition products and
the effect of various BP carbon structures. Figure S11 shows
the S K-edge and P K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge struc-
ture (XANES) spectra of sulfur-free cathodes (BP800|LPSC and
BP2000|LPSC) at the fully discharged and fully charged states
of the 1st and 4th cycles. At the fully discharged state of the
1st and 4th cycle, the S K-edge spectra of BP2000|LPSC shows
feature at 2482.5 eV, which corresponds to the reduction product
of Li2S (Figure S11b), and P K-edge spectra of BP2000|LPSC shows
features at 2157.1, 2145.6, and 2142.8 eV (Figure S11d) at the fully
discharged state of the 1st and 4th cycle, which correspond to the
decomposition products of Li3PS4/Li4P2S6, and Li3P [9, 65, 66]
Upon full charge, the features of reduction products disappeared,
and the features at 2471.7 and 2477.1 eV of S K-edge, and 2146.9 eV
of P K-edge emerge, which are attributed to oxidation products
of elemental sulfur and P2S5, respectively [66]. These findings
were proposed and illustrated in Figure S12, which agrees with
previous studies [30, 67]. Interestingly, the S K-edge spectra of
BP800|LPSC at the discharged state of the 1st cycle have no
detectable Li2S signal (Figure 2a; Figure S11a). The difference is
marked as a dashed circle in Figure S11a,b, which suggests that
the electrochemical redox reaction of SEs is more difficult to be
induced by BP800 than that of BP2000. A similar phenomenon
was observed in the P K-edge spectra of BP800|LPSC at the
discharged state of the 1st and 4th cycle (Figure 2b; Figure
S11c,d), the features at 2142.8, 2145.4, and 2157.5 eV, which denote
decomposition products of Li3P and Li3PS4/ Li4P2S6 offer weaker
intensities than that of BP2000|LPSC, the differences are marked
as dashed circles in Figure S11c,d, indicating the poor reaction
kinetics of LPSC decomposition in BP800|LPSC half-cell. These
results are consistent with the cycling performance shown in
Figure S9, where fewer electrolyte decomposition products are
induced by BP800, which implies that the BP2000 carbon host
with a higher specific surface area and larger porosity increases
the amount of carbon|SEs interface, which plays a determinant
role in the electrochemical decomposition kinetics of the LPSC.
Therefore, the abundant carbon|SEs contact favours electrolyte
decomposition, as shown in Figure S13.
In order to gain a better understanding of the effects of the
different carbon hosts on the electrochemical behaviors of sulfur,
XANES was carried out for BP800/S and BP2000/S cathodes
in the 1st and 4th cycles at discharged and charged states, and
the results are shown in Figure 2c–f. Figure 2c indicates that
after the addition of sulfur, the cell demonstrates a typical
S8/Li2S reversible conversion mechanism that occurs during the
discharge/charge process. It is worth noting that due to the
similarity of the discharge product of sulfur and the reduction
decomposition product of LPSC SEs, it is hard to distinguish
the decomposition of LPSC in the S K-edge spectra. In P K-edge
spectra of BP800/S and BP2000/S cathodes at the discharged
state (Figure 2d–f), the features of PS4
2−/P2S6
2−units and
Li3P at 2145.3 and 2142.8 eV were detected, which correspond
to the reduction products of LPSC. Furthermore, BP2000/S
cathodes present stronger signal intensities (as shown in
Figure 2d–f; Figure S14), indicating that BP2000 with a larger
specific surface area, demonstrates a stronger ability to facilitate
LPSC decomposition, from which it can be inferred that not
only does the surface of BP2000 provide electron resource for
sulfur, but the remaining BP2000 surface which has not been
occupied by sulfur is in contact with LPSC, contributing to the
extensive carbon|SEs boundaries, forming more active areas for
electrolyte decomposition. This result explains the phenomenon
observed in Figure 1b, where the high capacity caused by the
BP2000/S electrodes in the early cycle stage is partly caused by

2.6
Carbon Nanostructure-Driven Formation of
Distinct Interfaces
Based on the obtained electrochemical performance and charac-
terization results, we propose a schematic illustration (Figure 6)
that highlights the nanostructure effect of carbon hosts on
interfacial formation in sulfur cathodes of ASSLSBs. For the
composite cathodes incorporating BP2000 with a high surface
area and highly porous structure, a large number of micropores
inside BP2000 form useless and space-wasting ineffective pores.
Even when these pores are filled with sulfur, the deactivated
sulfur is located deeply, rendering it electrochemically inacces-
sible and hindering the establishment of simultaneously effective
sulfur|carbon and sulfur|SEs reaction sites. Meanwhile, the extra
carbon surface exposed to SEs, generates abundant carbon|SEs
interfaces, which accelerate severe electrolyte decomposition.
After long cycles, the accumulation of electrolyte decomposition
products with low conductivity further blocks the charge-carrier
transport pathways, ultimately leading to cathode degradation
and capacity fading. Therefore, the corresponding cathodes con-
structed based on carbon materials with high porosity and large
specific surface area are more likely to produce dead sulfur,
thereby reducing the electrochemical performance of the battery.
In contrast to BP2000, the BP800 carbon host with relatively
lower porosity and smaller specific surface area avoids the extra
free space and pores, which benefits in promoting the domi-
nant formation of dual interfaces (sulfur|carbon and sulfur|SEs),
while effectively suppressing excessive carbon|SEs contacts. This
dual-interface-dominated configuration provides abundant elec-
trochemically active sites and facilitates efficient Li+/e−transport
pathways. As a result, BP800/S cathode maintains a robust
conductive network during cycling and minimizes electrolyte
decomposition, ensuring superior long-term electrochemical sta-
bility and excellent rate performance. This comparison highlights
that whereas highly porous carbons favor liquid-state Li-S bat-
teries, the same design principle does not directly apply to
solid-state systems. Rather than simply maximizing surface area
and porosity, rational tuning of carbon nanostructures is required
to achieve multiple functions: capable of enabling uniform sulfur
dispersion, accommodating volume changes, and, most impor-
tantly, promoting the preferential formation of dual interfaces
(sulfur|carbon and sulfur|SEs) while suppressing detrimental
carbon|SEs interface. Such a selective formation strategy of the
interface ultimately dictates the performance and durability of
ASSLSBs. More importantly, we demonstrate that rational carbon
host design provides a facile yet powerful route to regulate
interfacial formation and markedly improve battery performance.

3
Conclusion
A fast electron- and ion-transfer network and inhibited electrolyte
decomposition in sulfur cathodes are the key to promoting elec-
trochemical performance for ASSLSBs. In our study, the impact of
carbon host structures in sulfur cathodes on the electrochemical
performance was systematically investigated in both liquid-state
and solid-state Li-S batteries. Unlike liquid systems, where high
surface area carbons and highly porous structures are favored, we
reveal that tailored nanostructures, such as BP800, promote the
preferential formation of abundant sulfur|carbon and sulfur|SEs
interfaces while intrinsically minimizing detrimental carbon|SEs
contacts. This dual-interface-dominant architecture stabilizes the
solid electrolyte and thus alleviates the buildup of insulating by-
products, accelerates sulfur redox kinetics, thereby enhancing
sulfur utilization. As a result, even at a practical sulfur loading
of 5 mg cm−2, the cells deliver a high reversible capacity of 1110
mAh g−1 at 0.2 C, and exhibit long-term cycling stability, retaining
93.3% over 100 cycles at 0.1 C. These findings redefine the
design principles for sulfur cathodes in solid-state Li-S batteries,
establishing carbon nanostructure-driven interfacial regulation
as a powerful strategy toward high-capacity, long-life solid-state
batteries.

Experimental Section
4.1
Materials
Sulfur (S8, 99.5%, Sigma–Aldrich), Black Pearls 800 (BP800, Cabot
Corporation), Black Pearls 1300 (BP1300, Cabot Corporation),
Black Pearls 2000 (BP2000, Cabot Corporation), Poly(vinylidene
fluoride) (PVDF, MW = 534,000 g/mol, Sigma–Aldrich), and
Acetylene Black (AB, MSE Supplies) were used before drying at
60◦C. N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, reagent Plus99%), lithium
bis(trifluoromethane) sulfonamide (LiTFSI, 99%), lithium nitrate
(LiNO3), 1,2-Dimethoxyethane (DME, anhydrous, 99.5%,), and
1,3-dioxolane (DOL, 99%) were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich,
Li6PS5Cl (LPSC, MSE Supplies) was used as received.
4.2
Preparation of BP800/S, BP1300/S, and
BP2000/S Composites
BP carbons were mixed with sulfur at a weight ratio of 3:7 first and
then ground by hand for 50 min. Next, the powder mixture was
transferred to a sealed steel reactor and heated to 155 ◦C for 12 h
to prepare BP800/S, BP1300/S, and BP2000/S composites. In the
BP800/S, BP1300/S, and BP2000/S composites, the sulfur content
was estimated to be 70 wt.%.
4.3
Preparation of BP800/S, BP1300/S, and
BP2000/S Cathodes for Liquid-State Li-S Batteries
BP800/S, BP1300/S, and BP2000/S composites were mixed with
AB and PVDF in a weight ratio of 7:2:1, and then NMP was added
slowly drop by drop, the powder mixture was ground by hand for
40 min to make a uniform slurry, firstly. Secondly, the slurry was
coated onto carbon-coated aluminum foil and then dried at 60 ◦C
overnight. After that, the dried foil was cut into circular disks with
a diameter of 1.2 cm. The mass loading of sulfur is around 1.4 mg
cm−2. The Li foil, polypropylene membrane (Celgard 2400), and
the solvent with 1 m LiTFSI and 2 wt.% LiNO3 that were dissolved
into a solvent mixture of DOL/DME (1:1, v/v) were used as an
anode, separator, and electrolyte, respectively. The electrolyte
amount was 30–45 µL mg−1 relative to the sulfur loading.
4.4
Preparation of BP800/S, BP1300/S, and
BP2000/S Cathodes for Solid-State Li-S Batteries
The prepared BP800/S, BP1300/S, and BP2000/S composites were
mixed with solid electrolyte LPSC, and AB with a weight ratio
of 4:4:2, and the mixture was transferred into sealed ZrO2 jars.
The composite cathodes were prepared through a high-energy
ball milling method at 250 rpm for 6 h. The prepared cathodes
with BP800, BP1300, and BP2000 carbon hosts were labelled
as BP800/S, BP1300/S, and BP2000/S, respectively. For sulfur-
free electrodes, the recipe is the same but without the addition
of sulfur, which are denoted as BP800|LPSC, BP1300|LPSC, and
BP2000|LPSC.

Fabrication of Liquid-State Li-S Coin Cells

CR-2032-type coin cells were assembled in an Ar-filled glove

Li/Li+ by using Neware battery test equipment.
4.7
Fabrication of Solid-State Li-S Batteries and
Electrochemical Measurements
The solid-state Li-S batteries were assembled and tested in a
poly(ether ether ketone) (PEEK) cell die with Ti metal rods.
First, 100 mg of LPSC powder was filled and cold-pressed into
a PEEK cylinder at 2 tons (10 mm diameter). Then, ∼4 mg
of composite cathode powders (corresponding to sulfur loading
of 1.4 mg cm−2) were spread on one side of the LPSC pellet
and pressed at 2 tons. On the other side of the LPSC pellet,
Li-In anode was attached and pressed at 0.1 tons. The total
solid-state Li-S batteries were subject to constant pressure at
0.5 tons. GCD experiments were conducted in the voltage range
of 0.5-2.5 V versus Li-In alloy, corresponding to about 1.1-3.1 V
against Li/Li+. Cyclic voltammograms (CV) were performed on
a versatile multichannel potentiostation 3/Z (VMP3) with scan
rates from 0.1 mV s−1 to 0.4 mV s−1 under the voltage range of 0.5-
2.5 V. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was used
at 7 MHz to 1000 mHz with potential polarization amplitude
of 10 mV by using versatile multichannel potentiostation 3/Z
(VMP3). The theoretical capacity of sulfur was taken as 1600 mAh
g−1.
