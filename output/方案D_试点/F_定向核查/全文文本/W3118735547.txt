Conﬁning Sulfur in Porous Carbon by Vapor
Deposition to Achieve High-Performance
Cathode for All-Solid-State Lithium−Sulfur
Batteries

and Donghai Wang*

ABSTRACT: All-solid-state lithium−sulfur batteries (ASLBs) have the
potential to achieve high energy density because of sulfur’s high
theoretical capacity (1672 mAh g−1) while alleviating persistent
polysulﬁde shuttling inherent to lithium−sulfur batteries based on liquid
organic electrolyte. However, the homogenization of sulfur, carbon, and
solid electrolyte is a challenge to achieving high-performance cathodes
for ASLBs. Herein, we demonstrate a promising sulfur−carbon composite
with high sulfur content (71.4−83.3%) prepared using a sulfur vapor
deposition (SVD) approach to show enhanced discharge speciﬁc
capacities, rate performance, and cycling stability, outperforming
conventional sulfur liquid deposition (SLD) and sulfur solid deposition
(SSD) approaches. A higher discharge speciﬁc sulfur capacity of 1792.6
mAh g−1 has been achieved at 0.1C and 60 °C with improvement ascribed
to smaller particle size and more homogeneous and deeply conﬁned
sulfur in sulfur−carbon composite, in contrast to 1619.2 and 1329.3 mAh g−1 for samples prepared by conventional SLD and
SSD approaches, respectively.
L
ithium sulfur (Li−S) batteries are based on a high
energy density chemistry that could potentially replace
existing Li-ion batteries,1,2 achieving the gravimetric
and volumetric energy densities of sulfur cathode (16Li + S8 ↔
8Li2S) to 2596 Wh kg−1 and 2199 Wh L−1, respectively.3,4 As
an added beneﬁt, sulfur is produced in large quantities as a
byproduct of desulfurizing oil and natural gas, lowering the
production cost and improving its global accessibility.5
Traditional Li−S batteries based on liquid organic electro-
lyte, however, are plagued with dissolution and shuttling eﬀect
of sulfur discharge products (i.e., polysulﬁdes) in the organic
electrolyte, causing self-discharge, low round-trip Coulombic
eﬃciency, and irreversible capacity fade.6,7 Most eﬀorts to date
have been focused on developing composite sulfur cathode to
physically or chemically trap the generated polysulﬁdes.8
Despite their limited success in improving the cyclability of
Li−S batteries, these approaches do not remedy the shuttling
eﬀect because the polysulﬁdes are inevitably soluble in organic
electrolytes.
The all-solid-state lithium−sulfur battery (ASLB), using a
solid electrolyte, has been proposed to fundamentally resolve
the polysulﬁde shuttling eﬀect.9,10 Recent advances in sulﬁde-
based solid electrolytes have demonstrated ionic conductivities
comparable to organic liquid electrolyte (∼10−2 S cm−1).11
Additionally, sulﬁde-based solid electrolytes possess desirable
mechanical properties and good chemical compatibility with
sulfur.12 However, sulfur is an electrical insulator (∼5 × 10−30
S cm−1) with very low ionic conductivity, making it diﬃcult to
use as a cathode by itself.13 Henceforth, to provide electronic
and ionic pathways for the cathode, sulfur must be mixed with
an electrical conductor, such as carbon, and an ionic conductor
(i.e., solid electrolyte). Therefore, it is critical to ensure proper
homogenization between sulfur, carbon, and solid electrolyte
for making a high-performance sulfur−carbon−solid electro-

toward the three-component composite cathode is to prepare
bicomponent sulfur−carbon composites with well-controlled
sulfur distribution and particle sizes within the carbon matrix.
Conventional approaches in the synthesis of sulfur−carbon
composites for Li−S batteries based on liquid electrolyte are
via depositing sulfur into porous carbon using sulfur liquid
deposition (SLD) and sulfur solid deposition (SSD). However,
the sulfur−carbon composite prepared by the conventional
approaches falls short in achieving high-performance in ASLBs,
in terms of low speciﬁc capacity and low rate performance as
demonstrated in the literature14−17 as well as in this Letter.
This account demonstrates a promising approach to achieve
a homogeneous distribution of sulfur within a porous carbon
matrix in the form of sulfur−carbon composites to enable a
high-performance cathode for ASLBs. We applied a sulfur
vapor deposition (SVD) approach to synthesize sulfur−carbon
composites, departing from conventional SLD and SSD
approaches. We show that this sulfur−carbon composite via
SVD enables conﬁnement of both sulfur and the discharge
product of Li2S mainly within porous carbon, reducing sulfur
content outside porous carbon and improving interfacial
contact between bicomponent composites with a solid-state
electrolyte, to achieve superior electrochemical performance.
Beneﬁting from the homogeneous distribution and physical
conﬁnement of sulfur and Li2S, the high-performance sulfur−
carbon composite delivers a discharge speciﬁc sulfur capacity
of 1792 mAh g−1 at a high sulfur content of 71.4% at 0.1C at
60 °C, while enjoying a capacity retention of 92% after 100
cycles. Moreover, the sulfur−carbon composite also shows a
high discharge speciﬁc sulfur capacity of 957.3 mAh g−1 even at
a high sulfur mass loading of 4.5 mg cm−2 at 0.1C and 60 °C in
ASLB, demonstrating the applicability of this approach for a
high energy density ASLB.
The vapor deposition approach is schematically illustrated in
Figure 1a. In this approach, sulfur is ﬁrst evaporated under low
pressure and then condensed and deposited inside porous
carbon to achieve a complete impregnation of sulfur within
porous carbon. In this manner, sulfur is homogeneously
distributed and conﬁned within the porous carbon. Next, this
sulfur−carbon composite is mechanically milled with glassy
Li3PS4 solid-state electrolyte to fabricate three-component
sulfur−carbon−solid electrolyte composite to serve as the
ASLB cathode, with details described in Experimental Section
in the Supporting Information.
The sulfur−carbon composite was ﬁrst characterized by
ﬁeld-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) and
transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The morphology of
the sulfur−carbon composite prepared by SVD retains the
original morphology of porous ketjen black (KB) carbon
without observing large sulfur particles outside KB particles, as
illustrated in Figures 1b,c and S3. High-angle annular dark-ﬁeld
(HAADF) TEM imaging (Figure 1d) of sulfur−carbon
composite and the corresponding electron energy loss
spectroscopy (EELS) elemental mapping (Figure 1e) show
overlapped sulfur and carbon traces, indicating homogeneous
sulfur distribution within porous carbon at the nanoscale.
Combined with FE-SEM and TEM studies, we conﬁrm the
sulfur is impregnated and conﬁned within porous carbon and
the absence of isolated sulfur particles within the sulfur−
carbon composite.
To quantify the amount of impregnated sulfur in porous
carbon, thermal gravimetric analysis (TGA) was conducted,
shown in Figure S4, and the measured sulfur content of
sulfur−carbon composites is consistent with the designed
sulfur-to-carbon ratios. The sulfur−carbon composite samples
are designated as SVD 50−x (where sulfur:carbon ratios are
50:x). Beneﬁting from the vapor deposition approach and high
pore volume feature of KB carbon, the sulfur−carbon
composite with high sulfur content (as high as 83.3%) can
be synthesized and shows excellent electrochemical perform-
ance in the ASLB.18,19
Next, the as-synthesized sulfur−carbon−solid electrolyte
composites were used to fabricate ASLB and evaluate their
electrochemical performance. Figure 2a shows the discharge−
charge proﬁles of sulfur−carbon composites prepared by SVD,
SLD, and SSD approaches with a ﬁxed sulfur−carbon ratio of
50:20 at 0.5C and 60 °C. Among the three samples, the
composite sulfur cathode prepared by the SVD approach
notably shows the highest discharge speciﬁc sulfur capacities at
all current densities, demonstrating the advantage of the SVD
method over conventional routes of SLD and SSD, as shown in
Figure 2b. Alternatively, Figure 2c summarizes the discharge

The underlying features for the higher performance of the
SVD sample over the SLD is attributed to the uniform sulfur
distribution, deep physical conﬁnement, and smaller sulfur
particle sizes. First, the X-ray diﬀraction pattern of the SVD
sample, as shown in Figure 2d, shows a broad amorphous peak
between 20°and 30° and a couple of weak crystalline sulfur
reﬂection peaks, in contrast to strong crystalline reﬂection
sulfur peaks in the SLD and SSD samples. This indicates more
uniform and conﬁned sulfur distribution within the KB carbon
in the SVD sample compared to the SLD sample, despite
having similar sulfur content in both.21−23 The uniform
distribution can be further deduced from the TEM images
(Figure 2g,h), as the deposited sulfur particles in the sulfur−
carbon composite prepared by SVD are more homogeneously
distributed and conﬁned within the carbon matrix compared to
the sulfur−carbon composite prepared by SLD. Second,
enlarged TGA curves (Figure 2e) show delayed weight loss
response in the SVD sample compared to the SLD sample,
which implies stronger physical interaction between carbon
and sulfur; from X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy inves-
tigation (Figure 2f), no chemical bonding feature is observed
in both the SVD and SLD samples, implying deeper physical
conﬁnement of sulfur in the SVD sample.24 Third, the SVD
approach generates submicrometer sulfur particles as a result of
vapor deposition. The morphology of the impregnated sulfur
in KB could not be observed by FE-SEM because of the
semiclosed architecture of KB (Figure S7a,b); however, smaller
and homogeneous sulfur deposits are observed in SVD
prepared samples by TEM images, compared with SLD
prepared samples (Figure 2g,h). Nevertheless, to closely
mimic the morphological features of the deposited sulfur
particles prepared by diﬀerent approaches, we prepared
sulfur−carbon composite using a carbon nanoﬁber (CNF) as
a carbon host that has an open structure compared to KB.
Sulfur particles synthesized by the SVD approach are
submicrometer in size on CNF, while the SLD method
generates larger microsized sulfur aggregate particles on CNF,
as shown in Figure S8. These attributes contribute the
improved electrochemical performance and lower overpoten-
tial observed in the discharge−charge proﬁle of the samples
prepared by SVD compared to SLD (Figure 1a−c).
Furthermore, the overpotential diﬀerence at a 50% DOD
was calculated to be 0.56 and 1.02 V for the samples prepared
by SVD and SLD, respectively, at 0.5C and 60 °C.
We further studied the sulfur−carbon composites with
diﬀerent sulfur content prepared by the SVD approach. Figure
3a illustrates the discharge−charge proﬁles of the composite
sulfur cathode of SVD 50−20, SVD 50−12, and SVD 50−10 at
0.1C and 60 °C (sulfur content corresponds to 71.4, 80.6, and
83.3% respectively). Surprisingly, even at a sulfur content as
high as 83.33%, the SVD 50−10 is still able to deliver a high
third discharge capacity of 964 mAh g−1. Figure 3b shows the
cycling performance for the SVD 50−20, SVD 50−12, and
SVD 50−10 in ASLB at sulfur loadings of ∼1.5 mg cm−2.

Interestingly, it is found that increasing sulfur content results in
lower capacity retention after 50 cycles (Figure 3c), with a
capacity retention of 96.56% for the SVD 50−20 dropping to
60.30% for the SVD 50−10 sample. This observation can be
ascribed to more sulfur deposition on the outer surface of
porous KB carbon as the sulfur content increases, as indicated
by strong XRD reﬂection peaks in the SVD samples with
higher sulfur content (Figure 3d). The sulfur deposited on the
outer surface of the KB carbon may lose contact with it upon
electrochemical cycling, resulting in the capacity fade. Upon
the electrochemical conversion reaction of sulfur, the volume
increases because of the conversion of sulfur to Li2S.
Considering that the measured pore volume of KB carbon is
larger than both the volume of sulfur and Li2S, as shown in
Figure 3e, the SVD 50−20 sample thus can physically conﬁne
not only the sulfur but also the discharge product of Li2S
mainly within the porous carbon during the electrochemical
cycling. Therefore, the SVD 50−20 sample shows optimal
electrochemical performance with both higher discharge
speciﬁc capacity and capacity retention in ASLB. Figure 3f
shows long cycling performance of SVD 50−20 sulfur−carbon
composite with a sulfur loading of 1.5 mg cm−2, exhibiting an
84.5% capacity retention even after 100 cycles which
corresponds to 0.16% capacity loss per cycle. The capacity
loss is partially caused by mechanically induced loss of contact
inside the ASLB cells during cycling because of volume
pulverization22,25 and therefore can be recovered by repressing
the cell. As demonstrated in Figure 3f, the discharge speciﬁc
sulfur capacity of 1437 mAh g−1 after the 100th cycle can be
increased to 1567 mAh g−1 after repressing the cells. The
recovered discharge speciﬁc sulfur capacity of 130 mAh g−1,
accounting for 48.8% of total lost capacity, makes the actual
capacity retention 91.9% after 100 cycles.
To demonstrate the advantages of the SVD method for
fabrication of sulfur−carbon composites to be used in high-
energy-density ASLB, the optimal SVD 50−20 sample was
used to fabricate three-component sulfur−carbon−solid
electrolyte composite cathodes with high sulfur mass loading
for electrochemical evaluation. Figure 4a shows discharge−
charge proﬁles for SVD 50−20 sample with an increased sulfur
mass loading from 1.5 mg cm−2 to 4.5 mg cm−2. As shown,
increasing the mass loading of the composite cathode lowers
the discharge speciﬁc sulfur capacities. This is due to higher
charge-resistance in the cathode layer as the cathode thickness
increases.26,27 Promisingly, even at a high sulfur loading of 4.5
mg cm−2, the ASLB still produces a discharge speciﬁc sulfur
capacity of 957.3 mAh g−1. More importantly, after 50 cycles,
similar capacity retention is observed among the three samples
with diﬀerent mass loading, as shown in Figure 4b, which
further cements the previous explanation that higher capacity
retention is anticipated when the sulfur and Li2S are conﬁned
within porous carbon.
In summary, the vapor deposition of sulfur within porous
carbon has been identiﬁed as a promising method for
fabrication of sulfur−carbon composites for ASLBs, out-
performing conventional liquid and solid sulfur deposition
approaches. The improved electrochemical performance using
the vapor deposition method has been attributed to the
homogeneous distribution, the physical and deep conﬁnement
of sulfur particles, and the smaller sulfur particle size in porous
carbon. The capacity decay of the sulfur cathode has been
discovered to be tightly related to the amount of sulfur
deposited inside versus outside the porous carbon. The
optimal performance can be achieved at the conditions with
both sulfur and the discharge product Li2S being conﬁned
within the porous carbon host during cycling.
