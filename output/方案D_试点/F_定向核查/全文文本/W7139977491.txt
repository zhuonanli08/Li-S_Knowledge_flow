A B S T R A C T
All-solid-state lithium‑sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) emerge as next-generation energy storage systems due to their 
high theoretical capacity (1672 mAh g−1) and ability to mitigate sulfur shuttle effects in liquid electrolytes (LEs). 
However, interfacial charge-transfer resistance at sulfur/solid electrolyte (SE) interfaces, compounded by sulfur's 
insulating nature and volume changes over the cell cycling, limits performance. We introduce a new hybrid 
cathode for ASSLSBs incorporating tungsten sulfide (WS2) as a dual redox mediator (DRM) utilizing unprece­
dented W4+/5+/6+ redox. The hybrid cathode simultaneously enhances electron and Li+ transport in the cathode 
for facile sulfur redox and stabilizes interfacial resistance via unique redox mechanism of WS2 as phase- 
controlled DRM. Specifically, the WS2 mediator forms reversible amorphous LixWS(4-α) phase as a discharge 
product rather than LiWS2, which irreversibly reduces to W metal and Li2S. This unique WS2 redox mechanism 
further mitigated the total volume expansion of the hybrid cathode over the ASSLSB cycling. The hybrid cathode 
in ASSLSB exhibits outstanding capacity retention of ~83% with maintained discharge capacity of 831 mAh g−1 
after 50 cycles, particularly under low pressure (~0.3 MPa) and at room temperature (25 ◦C), whereas con­
ventional sulfur cathodes retain only 34% and 371 mAh g−1 under the same conditions.
1. Introduction
As many aspects of daily life, from simple household items to public 
transportation, are rapidly becoming electrified, the request for devel­
oping high energy density energy storage systems with long lifespan and 
high safety has increased significantly. In this regard, lithium‑sulfur 
batteries (LSBs) have garnered significant attention due to its high 
theoretical capacity (1672 mAh g−1), abundant resources in nature's 
crust, and low material cost. [1–7] However, the commercialization of 
lithium‑sulfur technology in liquid electrolyte batteries is constrained 
by limited sulfur utilization, volume expansion of the active material, 
the polysulfide shuttle effect and the risk of thermal runaway associated 
with flammable liquid electrolytes (LEs). [1,8,9] In particular, the 
polysulfide shuttle effect, which causes the loss of cathode active ma­
terials through polysulfide dissolution into LEs, leads to capacity decline 
during long-term cycling. [4,10,11] To address this issue, all-solid-state 
lithium‑sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) have emerged as a promising alter­
native to conventional LSBs by replacing flammable organic liquid 
electrolytes with non-flammable inorganic solid electrolytes (SEs). This 
innovative transition toward an all-solid-state system not only enhances 
the safety of sulfur-based batteries but also improves the reaction sta­
bility of sulfur within the cell, as polysulfides are insoluble in solid 
electrolytes.
Unlike conventional LSBs, sulfur in ASSLSBs undergoes a different 
redox process during cell cycling, particularly involving the formation of 
Li2S2 as an intermediate product during the conversion between sulfur

these reaction products inherently exhibit poor electrical conductivity 
and undergo inevitable volume changes (~80%) during cell cycling, 
leading to sluggish reaction kinetics and eventually deteriorating the 
cycling performance of the cell. [5–8,14] To overcome these disadvan­
tages, several approaches have been made to enhance the reactivity of 
the multi-step solid-phase sulfur redox reactions by increasing three- 
phase boundary area among the non-conductive sulfur (S8), carbon 
additive, and solid electrolyte in a composite cathode of ASSLSBs. [15]
Previous studies have developed porous carbon materials, including 
PAN-based core–shell fibers and sulfur-infiltrated porous carbon, to 
improve electrode conductivity and sulfur utilization in ASSLSBs. 
[16,17] While these architectures enhance the contact between com­
ponents and facilitate redox kinetics, their complex and costly fabrica­
tion processes hinder commercial application.
Recently, transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) have garnered 
significant attention in the field of energy storage due to their 2D 
structures, which exhibit superior electrical conductivity and catalytic 
properties. [18–23] Recent reports have shown that CoS2, which has a 
rock salt structure, is incorporated into the cathode active material of 
ASSLSBs and changes to LixCoS2 upon discharge in a one-electron re­
action. [24] Nazar's group demonstrated that a Li2S@LiVS2 structure 
enables improved kinetics through intimate interfacial contact, 
involving both intercalation and conversion reactions. [25,26] While 
TMDs present a promising strategy for cathode design, their lower 
theoretical capacity compared to sulfur limits the overall capacity when 
combined.
Among many TMDs, tungsten disulfide (WS2) has been rarely 
explored as a cathode active material for ASSLSBs. Although its 
reversible electrochemical reaction with Li2S in the cathode of ASSLSB 
was reported by H. Gamo et al., its redox mechanism has not been 
demonstrated yet. [27] WS2 has the lowest effective mass among TMDs, 
and exhibits higher electrical mobility so that it can resolve the inter­
facial contact issues between insulating reaction products in sulfur 
cathode by serving as effective charge carrier (Li+ and e−) transporter. 
[18,28,29]
In this study, we employed WS2 in composite cathode (S8 + WS2 +
carbon + solid electrolyte) for ASSLSBs and, for the first time, revealed 
its key role as a DRM that can improve the sulfur redox kinetics, reduce 
the charge transfer resistance evolution and further mitigate the volume 
expansion of the composite cathode over the cell cycling. This hybrid 
cathode enables to cycle ASSLSBs at low pressure (~0.3 MPa) [30] and 
room temperature (25 ◦C) with capacity retention of 83% over 50 cycles 
while non-hybrid cathodes perform lower than 34% over 50 cycles. A 
combination of synchrotron X-ray-based ex/in-situ analyses and elec­
trochemical characterization reveals a novel redox reaction behavior of 
the hybrid composite cathode, which produces Li2WS4 rather than W +
Li2S through a conversion reaction as final discharge product, differing 
from those previously reported. The suppressed volume charge of hybrid 
cathode is further demonstrated in-situ optical microscopy images. 
These studies offer insight for designing a novel hybrid cathode for high 
energy density ASSLSBs.
2. Experimental section
2.1. Synthesis of solid electrolyte (Li6PS5Cl, LPSCl)
For the preparation of solid electrolyte Li6PS5Cl, powder type of Li2S 
(Sigma Aldrich, 99.98%), P2S5 (Sigma Aldrich, 99%), and LiCl (Sigma 
Aldrich, 99%) were mixed by high-energy ball milling at a molar ratio of 
5:1:2 at 450 rpm for 60 h (20 min milling, 40 min pause, 60 cycles). A 
zirconia ball with a diameter of 10 mm was used, and the ball-to-powder 
ratio is 1:45. After high-energy ball milling, the mixture is placed in a 
quartz tube and annealed about 10 h. The mixture heats up to 550 ◦C at a 
rate of 2 ◦C min−1 and maintain it for 5 h. After that, it is naturally 
cooled down at room temperature. Finally, synthesized powder was 
ground and sieved to reduce the particle size and obtain 125 μm or less- 
level particles. All the preparation process were conducted in Ar-filled 
glovebox.
2.2. Preparation of the composite cathode
Sulfur (S8) (Sigma Aldrich, 99.998%) and tungsten disulfide (WS2) 
(Sigma Aldrich, 99%) were used as cathode active materials. Sulfur 
active material was pretreated by cryo-milling method (cryomill, 
Retsch) to pulverize the S8 particles into lesser than micro-size. All three 
types of ASSLSBs are synthesized in the same method. First of all, the 
cathode active material and the multi-wall carbon nanotube (MWCNT) 
are mixed in a weight ratio of 3:2 by using a ball milling. The setting is 
10 cycles of 30 min mixing at 250 rpm and 10 min of pause. Afterward, 
the mixture and LPSCl are mixed with a weight ratio of 1:1 and ball- 
milled at 300 rpm for 30 min followed by a 10 min pause for 2 cycles. 
All of the processes described above are performed in an argon atmo­
sphere. The sulfur composite cathode consists of 30 wt% elemental 
sulfur (S8) as the sole active material. In contrast, the hybrid composite 
cathode contains a total of 30 wt% active materials, comprising 15 wt% 
sulfur (S8) and 15 wt% tungsten sulfide (WS2). The tungsten sulfide 
composite cathode is prepared with 30 wt% WS2 as the active material.
2.3. Fabrication of ASSLSBs
The fabrication of ASSLSB is conducted within an Ar-atmosphere 
glovebox. Using a 15 mm diameter SUS mold, 150 mg of LPSCl is 
pressed into 30 MPa to create a solid electrolyte layer in the pellet shape. 
The 6 mg of as-prepared composite cathode was placed on top of the 
LPSCl layer and pressed at 40 MPa to bond with it, thus creating a 
cathode layer. The loading amount of the cathode active material is 
approximately 1.13 mg cm−2. Following this, 65 mg of Li–In powder 
was added in the opposite direction, and the 10 MPa of pressure resulted 
in the creation of an anode layer. The Li–In powder can be obtained by 
mixing the Li powder and In powder in a 1:2 at.% ratio at 2000 rpm for 
30 s. The resulting ASSLSB pellet is assembled with CR2032 SUS coin 
cells. In the in-situ XRD measurements conducted at the 9 A beamline, 
the ASSLSB was configured with composite cathodes, LPSCl, and Li- 
metal anodes. All other cells, except for this configuration, employed 
composite cathodes, LPSCl, and Li–In alloy anodes. The coin cell parts - 
cap, bottom, and spacer of the coin cell for in-situ XRD measurement 
have punched holes, and polyimide film is attached to the cap and 
bottom to prevent exposure to the atmosphere.
2.4. Electrochemical test of the composite cathode
CV curve and In situ impedance measurement were performed using 
an multichannel potentio-galvanostat (VMP3, Bio-Logic, France) within 
a frequency range of 0.1 Hz - 1 MHz. The cycling stability and rate 
performance were evaluated within a voltage window of 0.1–3.0 V and 
0.7–3.6 V using Li–In and Li-metal anodes, respectively. Coin cell 
cycling performance and voltage profile tests at room temperature and 
low pressure were measured with a multichannel battery tester (Maccor 
version 4000).
2.5. Material characterization
SEM (Regulus 8230, Hitachi) images of the pristine material were 
observed in Backscatter electron (BSE) mode. Ex situ X-ray diffraction 
(XRD) measurements were conducted using an R-AXIS IV++ Rigaku 
instrument (in-house) with Mo Kα radiation (wavelength of 0.7107 Å). 
Ex-situ XRD measurements were performed by cycling the cell to a 
specific voltage, followed by disassembly. The composite cathode layer 
was then scraped, and the collected material was concentrated and 
sampled in a capillary. In situ XRD was measured at the 6D (UNIST-PAL 
Crystallography Beamline) and 9 A (Ultra-Small-Angle X-ray Scattering

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Physical properties of cathode materials
The morphology and crystal structure of the prepared Li6PS5Cl 
(LPSCl), sulfur (S8) and tungsten sulfide (WS2) and their composite 
cathode were examined and displayed in Fig. 1. Scanning electron mi­
croscope (SEM) images in Fig. 1a-c show LPSCl and S8 particles with 
irregular shape and size of ≤25 μm. We note that S8 size was controlled 
by cryo-milling throughout the pretreatment to maximize the effective 
surface area for electrochemical reaction. Meanwhile, WS2 consists of 
hexagonal shaped plates with thickness of ≤200 nm implying that it can 
provide wide specific surface area and the multiple-channel networks in 
the composite cathode, which is beneficial for contact with other ma­
terials. [27] X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns in Fig. 1d confirm that the 
synthesized LPSCl is well crystallized into cubic structure (F43m) 
exhibiting a high ionic conductivity of 5.58 × 10−3 S cm−1 (at RT) 
(Fig. S1) which is comparable to previously reported results. [31–33]
Fig. 1e displays the XRD pattern of the ring-shaped S8 molecule, which 
consists of eight sulfur atoms (inset), resulting in a relatively voluminous 
cathode in ASSLSBs. As shown in Fig. 1f, the XRD pattern of WS2 reveals 
that its crystal structure consists of alternating atomic layers of W and S, 
which interact through van der Waals forces. [34] Notably, this layered 
structure makes WS2 suitable as a cathode active material, enabling 
lithium-ion intercalation. [18,34] SEM energy dispersive spectroscopy 
(EDS) measurement on the composite cathodes in Fig. 1g and h confirms 
that the cathode components are homogeneously mixed in the hybrid 
cathode by showing the well distributed element signals of S, Cl, P, and 
W.

Although WS2 enhanced the reaction activity and sulfur utilization by 
providing high electrical conductivity similar to previously reported 
TMDs, the high discharge capacity can only be explained through a new 
electrochemical reaction mechanism that would be different from those 
previously identified.
The electrochemical reaction of the three types of cathodes in 
ASSLSBs was further investigated by CV test at a scan rate of 0.05 mV/s 
and displayed in Fig. 2d-f. For reference, the CV of S8 cathode shows one 
reduction peak at 0.9 and 1.1 V and one oxidation peak at 1.9 V, indi­
cating well known sulfur redox of conversion reaction (S8 ↔ Li2S). 
[12,38] For WS2 cathode in Fig. 2f, CV result features aforementioned 
lithium intercalation and conversion reaction with exhibiting four 
reduction peaks at around 1.2, 1.3, 0.4, 0.2 V and oxidation peaks at 
around 1.9 V that can be commonly attributed to the formation of 
LixWS2 and W + Li2S. In comparison, the hybrid cathode shows three 
reduction peaks at around 1.2, 1.1 and 0.5 V and one oxidation peak at 
around 1.9 V. We note that although the CV shows seemingly the 
combination of submerged redox peaks from S8 and WS2 cathode 
together, the 0.2 V reduction peak of WS2 cathode corresponding to the 
irreversible reduction of W from LixWS2 disappeared from the hybrid 
cathode. [20,21] Given the oxidation peaks of three types of cathodes 
are all located around 1.9 V, it can be confirmed that WS2 cathode un­
dergoes a unique reduction pathways during the discharge process.
The rate performance of ASSLSBs was evaluated over a current range 
of 0.05 to 1C-rate cycling performance, as shown in Fig. 2g. Both cells 
had similar capacities at the initial 0.05C; however, as the rate 
increased, the S8 cell showed a sharp decline in capacity. After the rate 
was restored to 0.05C, the capacity seemed to recover, but as the rate 
increased to 0.1C and during cycling, the capacity decreased rapidly 
again. On the other hand, the hybrid cell showed higher capacity than 
the S8 cell at each rate, and also exhibits stable cycling performance 
when the rate was recovered to 0.05C followed by a long-term cycle at 
0.1C. Consequently, hybrid cells demonstrate superiority over conven­
tional ASSLSBs with regard to sulfur redox kinetics and interfacial 
resistance evolution. The further comparative study on the effects of 
different TMDs (WS2 and MoS2) in hybrid cathodes shown in Fig. S4 
demonstrates that WS2 plays additional roles beyond providing elec­
trical conductivity, resulting in superior ASSLSB cycling performance to 
other hybrid cathodes. The superior cycling performance of the hybrid 
cathode in comparison to the S8 and WS2 cathodes in ASSLSBs further 
demonstrated in Fig. 2h. All electrodes were first cycled 3 times at 0.05C 
for activation followed by 0.1C cycling. The hybrid cathode exhibits an 
initial discharge capacity of ~831mAh g−1 at 0.1C after 50 cycles, with 
an outstanding retention rate of ~83% compared to S8 and WS2 com­
posite cathodes performed ~371 mAh g−1 and ~ 72 mAh g−1. Consid­
ering the coulombic efficiency of all cathode samples remained close to 
100% over 50 cycles, no decomposition of sulfide solid electrolyte is 
assumed from all three electrode samples.

3.3. Crystal structure and chemical state evolution of WS2 in the hybrid 
cathode
The synchrotron/lab X-ray diffraction (XRD) analyses were con­
ducted to investigate the detailed reaction mechanism during the initial 
cycling of hybrid cathode in ASSLSB. Fig. 3 shows the initial cycling 
profile and color contour map of partial synchrotron in-situ XRD data 
zoomed from entire 2θ range (Fig. S5), demonstrating the crystal 
structure evolution of the active materials in the hybrid cathode of 
ASSLSB. To observe the change in the peak of WS2, the focus was 
enlarged by about 14–14.5 degrees. Fig. 3a and c indicates that the 
disappearance of weak (002) reflection at 14.4◦from the poorly crys­
talline ball-milled WS2 in the as-prepared cathode during the first 
discharge to 0.1 V. From the following charge from 0.1 V to 1.9 V where 
Li2S should reduce back to lithium polysulfides, no discernible XRD peak 
evolved around 14–14.5 degrees. Meanwhile, the weak (002) reflection 
of WS2 gradually becomes stronger starting from ~1.9 V (yellow dashed 
line) and most intensified at the end of charge demonstrating the for­
mation of crystalline WS2. Interestingly, the crystallized WS2 reversibly 
amorphized again during the following 2nd discharge to 0.1 V showing 
the diminished (002) reflection peak. In order to exclude the possibility 
of damaged discharge products by X-ray radiation, ex-situ Raman 
spectroscopy, non-X-ray-based analysis, is conducted as a complemen­
tary and the results is shown in Fig. S7. The result indicates that the 
absence of XRD feature of Li2WS4 in the discharged hybrid cathode is 
due to the poor crystallinity of Li2WS4 rather than the damaged 
discharge product.
The in-situ XRD peak evolution of WS2 in hybrid cathode delineates 
different structural evolution from the one of the WS2 composite cathode 
that indicates the Li (de)intercalation reaction of the layered WS2 
showing a reversible (002) peak shift over the cell cycling (Fig. 3d). The 
initially high WS2 peak and its subsequent weakening is attributed to an 
irreversible conversion of WS2 into W and Li2S during cycling, without 
complete reformation of WS2. [21] On the other hand, the (111) 
reflection of Li2S at ~26.9◦was not observed from the color contour 
map of in-situ synchrotron XRD data (Fig. S8) assumably due to its 
relatively poor crystallinity or the vulnerability of Li2S to the high en­
ergy X-ray beam. The peak corresponding to the cathode active material 
exhibits relatively low intensity compared to those of other components 
in the half-cell. Therefore, further lab-scale ex-situ XRD was performed 
as explained in experimental section. From the ex-situ XRD patterns in 
Fig. 3e, the generation of Li2S during discharge and its disappearance 
after 1.0 V are confirmed by closely examining (111) reflection (marked 
as yellow star) of Li2S from the hybrid cathode. This result corresponds 
with previous reports demonstrating the reversible generation and 
dissipation of Li2S during ASSLSBs cycling. [5,12,13] Consequently, this 
result identifies Li2S as one of the final discharge products in the hybrid 
ASSLSB.

3.4. Unraveling the redox mechanism of the hybrid cathode
A combination of electrochemistry, x-ray diffraction and spectros­
copy results demonstrates the reversible W redox between W4+ and W6+
suggesting the reaction mechanism of WS2 in the hybrid cathode. The 
entire reaction mechanism of hybrid ASSLSB can be described as 
follows: 
WS2 + S8 + 14Li+ + 14e−↔Li2WS4 + 6Li2S
(1) 
Considering the oxidation state of tungsten, the reaction can be 
described as proceeding in two distinct steps. 
( ∼1.1V) WS2 + S8 + 7Li+ + 7e−↔LixWS(4−α) + yLi2S2 (0 ≤x, a ≤1), (3
≤y ≤3.5)
(2) 
( ∼0.1V) LixWS(4−α) + yLi2S2 + 7Li+ + 7e−↔Li2WS4 + 6Li2S
(3) 
The presence of W+5 in the XPS results (Fig. 4) can be interpreted 
based on the proposed two-step reaction mechanism and the composi­
tion expressed as LixWS(4-α). Since the formation of Li₂S₂ is known to 
correspond to approximately half of the theoretical capacity in ASSLSBs, 
this relationship was used to define a range for the value of y. [5]
Following this reaction mechanism, the reduction peaks at 1.06 V and 
0.5 V in Fig. 2e can be attributed to the reduction of W4+/W5+ and W5+/ 
W6+, respectively, accompanied by the formation of Li2S2 and Li2S. Due 
to two-electron reaction of hybrid system, the theoretical capacity of the 
hybrid cathode is estimated to be 1488 mAh g−1 (Table S1), and this can 
explain the extra capacity mentioned in Fig. 2b. This distinct electro­
chemical reaction mechanism of hybrid composite cathode can be 
further demonstrated by monitoring the voltage profiles of the hybrid 
cathode cycled at slow rate of 0.01C shown in Fig. S10. The slow rate 
cycling profiles of hybrid cathode closely resembles to the one from 
cycled at 0.05C (Fig. 2b). The absence of the 0.2 V plateau, even in the 
0.01C-cycled hybrid cathode, suggests that the formation of metallic W 
is suppressed due to the thermodynamically stable reaction pathway, 
rather than being hindered by the overpotential arising from the slug­
gish reaction kinetics of the composite cathode. Given the electro­
chemistry data and synchrotron x-ray-based analysis results above, the 
spontaneous reaction of S₈ to Li2S2 occurs first. Subsequently, the 
remaining oxidizing ability of the polysulfide species drives the oxida­
tion of WS2, resulting in the formation of Li2S and Li2WS4 as final 
products in cathode. The resulting Li2WS4 was not detected in the XRD 
pattern due to its metastable and amorphous nature, which is consistent 
with the above analysis. [39] Consequently, these findings demonstrate 
that WS₂ in the hybrid cathode undergoes a distinct mechanism, 
differing from previous studies that reported the irreversible reduction 
of WS₂ to metallic W [21] as corroborated by our study (Fig. 2g).

reaction with the Li–In anode during cycling. [42]

its role as an DRM.
4. Conclusion
The utilization of intrinsically insulating sulfur by adopting TMDs as 
an ionic and electric conductive redox mediator in a cathode has 
recently been explored to improve the cycle life and energy density of 
ASSLSBs. However, their role was limited to providing ionic and electric 
conductive pathways for sulfur redox without improving electro-chemo- 
mechanical properties of the composite cathodes. Our study reveals that 
incorporating electrically conductive WS2 as a two electron dual redox 
mediator (DRM) in a cathode can maximize the utilization of sulfur in 
the hybrid composite cathode by facilitating the two-step sulfur redox 
reactions (S8 ↔ Li2S2 ↔ Li2S) and accommodate the volume change of 
the electrode over the cell cycling. The comparative study by combining 
in−/ex-situ X-ray diffraction and spectroscopy analysis with electro­
chemistry reveals that the WS2 in the hybrid cathode acts as an active
