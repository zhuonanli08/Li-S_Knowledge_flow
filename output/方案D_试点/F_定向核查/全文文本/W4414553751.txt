ABSTRACT: All-solid-state lithium−sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs), as an energy storage
system for achieving the high energy density target of 600 Wh kg−1, hold significant
importance in driving in next-generation battery technologies. This review focuses on the
key challenges of cathode materials for high energy density ASSLSBs and systematically
summarizes the recent research progress. First, the interfacial reaction mechanisms among
active materials, conductive agents, and solid electrolytes in sulfur cathodes are analyzed in
depth, revealing the fundamental causes of interface failure. Second, the advancements in
composite cathodes are summarized, including the influence of preparation processes,
material design strategies, and the structure-performance regulation mechanisms of mixed
conductors. Next, the role of interface engineering strategies in enhancing reaction kinetics
is discussed in detail. Furthermore, recently developed solutions for critical technical
bottlenecks, such as high sulfur loading and low-temperature adaptability, are reviewed.
Finally, future research directions are envisioned from the dimensions of multiscale
interface engineering, material systems, and characterization techniques. This review aims to move beyond conventional
single-component optimization approaches, developing a multicomponent framework for cathode design. The review further
provides references for developing high-energy-density, long-cycle-life ASSLSBs, offering a comprehensive reference for
advancing the practical application of this energy storage technology.

mixed ionic-electronic conductors, redox mediator
1. INTRODUCTION
Battery technology, powering the modern energy revolution
and intelligent society, continues to attract global attention.1
Although lithium-ion batteries have achieved widespread
application in consumer electronics and electric vehicles, the
market demand for new battery technologies with higher
energy density, enhanced safety, and environmental friend-
liness is growing exponentially.2 Amid the emergence of
various new battery technologies,3,4 lithium−sulfur batteries
stand out due to their unique material advantages. A sulfur
cathode (1675 mAh g−1) is paired with a lithium metal anode
(3860 mAh g−1), enabling a theoretical full-cell energy density
of 2600 Wh kg−1-nearly ten times that of conventional lithium-
ion batteries.5 Additionally, sulfur is abundant in the Earth’s
crust, low-cost, and environmentally benign. These combined
characteristics position lithium−sulfur batteries as candidates
for next-generation energy storage technologies with significant
industrialization potential.6
However, lithium−sulfur batteries employing liquid electro-
lytes suffer from inherent drawbacks. During charge−discharge
processes, polysulfides dissolve into the liquid electrolyte and
undergo irreversible shuttling between the cathode and anode.7
This phenomenon not only leads to continuous loss of active
materials but also results in decreased Coulombic Efficiency
and rapid capacity decay, severely limiting the cycle life and
practical performance of the batteries.8,9 To address this issue,
researchers have proposed a core solution: replacing liquid
electrolytes with solid electrolytes. By suppressing the
dissolution and diffusion of polysulfides, this approach

Currently, the development of ASSLSBs faces multiple

such as S/C,11 S/metal,12−14 and transition metal compo-

issues of sulfur cathodes, including poor conductivity,
inadequate interfacial contact, severe volume expansion, and
slowed reaction kinetics, through component optimization and
structural design. Although significant progress has been made

potential. The key to overcoming current performance
limitations lies in fundamentally understanding cathode
interfacial reaction mechanisms. A systematic approach to
the multicomponent coupling issues in composite cathodes

networks. Specifically, precise control over the spatial
distribution and interfacial properties of active materials,
conductive media, and solid electrolytes constructs continuous
charge transport channels at the nanoscale, thereby enhancing
interfacial contact and charge carrier transfer efficiency.

2. CHALLENGES OF SOLID-STATE LITHIUM−SULFUR
CATHODES
2.1. Special Interfacial Issues of ASSLSBs. In the solid-
state battery system, there are many interfaces. As the core
channel for lithium-ion transmission, the interface is of crucial
importance. There is a significant direct correlation between
the integrity, chemical and structural stability of the interface
and the transmission efficiency of lithium ions. A complete and
stable interface can provide a continuous and low-resistance
path for lithium-ion migration, while the damage or
degradation of the interface will lead to a sharp increase in
transmission impedance and even block ion conduction.18
However, due to the lack of fluidity of liquid electrolytes, solid-
state electrolytes cannot optimize the contact between particles
at the interface by infiltrating pores, resulting in poor physical
contact between interfaces as an inevitable consequence. At the
same time, in the assembly process, the stacking of the
cathode, electrolyte, and anode forms a multilevel interface
system, and the doubling of the number of interfaces further
exacerbates the systematic interface problems.19
Figure 1 shows the various interface situations present in all-
solid-state batteries. It is worth noting that, in addition to the
cathode/electrolyte and anode/electrolyte interfaces, there is a
unique three-phase reaction boundary on the cathode side.
Taking typical ASSLSBs as an example, their cathode is
composed of S or Li2S. The disadvantage is the extremely poor
electronic and ionic conductivity, which makes the electro-
chemical reaction only occur in the three-phase boundary
region formed by the contact of the active material, conductive
agent, and solid-state electrolyte.20 At the same time, the
nonfluidity of the electrolyte further limits the expansion of the
reaction space, resulting in the three-phase boundary becoming
a key node where mechanical, chemical, and electrochemical
problems are intertwined. The following content will analyze
these aspects separately.
2.2. Mechanical Perspectives. The mechanical properties
of the interface have a significant impact on maintaining the
integrity of the interface. The main reasons for its failure
include poor contact between solid particles themselves,
volume changes during the sulfur conversion process, and
irreversible sulfur conversion.
Solid−solid contact involves the inherent properties of the
material itself, mainly including contact area, particle size,
material stiffness, and pore structure. Theoretically, an ideal
cathode should achieve a three-dimensional (3D) uniform
distribution of the active material, electrolyte, and conductive
additive. However, in the actual preparation process, due to the
limitations of solid−solid contact, microcracks and pores often
exist at the interface, which not only significantly increases the
interfacial resistance but also cuts off the electron/ion
transport channels, causing some sulfur and discharge products
to form inert regions due to their inability to participate in the
electrochemical reaction.22 Because of this, during the
assembly of all-solid-state batteries, high pressure is applied
to reduce the pore size and porosity. However, it is still difficult
to achieve the closest packing state between the electrolyte and
the electrode, and there is still a relatively high porosity
between the two, usually between 10 and 40%, depending on
the pressure and the mechanical properties of the electrolyte
and electrode materials.23 This makes the structural
optimization design of the composite cathode side an
important aspect. A large amount of research work has been
carried out around optimizing the microstructure design of
traditional cathodes, regulating the proportion of material
components, and developing new active material systems,
which has laid the foundation for the development of solid-
state lithium−sulfur batteries.24,25
The mechanical issues of ASSLSBs not only stem from the
inherent limitations of solid−solid contact, but are also
severely affected by the volume change that sulfur undergoes
during charge−discharge cycles. When S8 is converted to Li2S,
the volume expansion rate can reach 80%, and the density
increases from 1.66 to 2.03 g cm−3. This can cause the cracking
of positive electrode particles and also lead to the
concentration of mechanical stress at the interface, triggering
the initiation of microcracks and the expansion of pores,
resulting in the gradual failure of interfacial contact.26 With the
progression of charge−discharge cycles, the periodic volume
change of the active material further exacerbates this interfacial
degradation. It not only hinders electron/ion transport and
increases the interfacial contact impedance, but also causes an
uneven distribution of local current density, inducing the
uncontrollable growth of lithium dendrites.27 More seriously,
the overall expansion of the electrode caused by volume
changes leads to a continuous decline in the volumetric energy
density of the battery, ultimately severely restricting the cycle
life and safety performance of all-solid-state batteries. This
multiscale interfacial degradation process triggered by
mechanical stress constitutes the main bottleneck for the
performance improvement of solid-state lithium−sulfur bat-
teries.
It is also worth noting that the irreversible transformation of
sulfur species during the charging process may further
exacerbate the mechanical failure of the three-phase boundary.
Theoretically, Li2S should be completely and reversibly
converted into S8 rings, but the actual conversion efficiency
is often less than 90%.28 The volume-shrinkage stress opposite
to the discharge process can lead to interfacial microcracks and
local delamination. The problem of dead sulfur will cause
irreversible fracture of S8, and the fractured structure is difficult
to be reorganized into stable S8 rings. This irreversible
structural damage will continuously weaken the mechanical
stability of the interface between the electrode and the
electrolyte, making the interface more vulnerable to stress
shock during charge−discharge cycles, accelerating interface
failure and the decline of battery performance. In response to
the above problems, Li et al.29 used TiS2 as a catalytic “solder”.
Taking advantage of its low Young’s modulus (about 70 GPa)
and good interfacial wettability, it reacts with S8 and sulfide
solid electrolyte (LPSC) during ball milling, in situ generating
amorphous TiS4 and Li-Ti-P-S-Cl interfacial phases. These
amorphous phases strengthen the solid-solid contact through
chemical “welding”, reducing interfacial defects. At the same
time, the flexibility and interlayer sliding characteristics of TiS2
can relieve the stress concentration generated during the
charge-discharge cycle of sulfur (such as the 80% volume
expansion when S8 generates Li2S, suppressing the initiation of

2.3. Chemical Perspectives. During the charge and
discharge process of lithium−sulfur batteries, chemical changes
involve the transformation of various substances. Different
from the solid−liquid−solid transformation in liquid lithium−
sulfur batteries with two reaction platforms, the voltage
distribution of ASSLSBs exhibits a single platform at ∼2.1 V
(vs Li+/Li). The single platform observed in the discharge
curve of ASSLSBs has been widely interpreted as being caused
by the direct conversion between S8 and Li2S during the
charge/discharge process, without the formation of inter-
mediate polysulfide species.30 From the perspective of the
macroscopic electrochemical reaction process, cyclic S8
molecules undergo a 16-electron/ion electrochemical charge
transfer process and are directly converted into Li2S. This
process faces two problems. One is that the thermodynamically
stable structure of S leads to a relatively high covalent bond
breaking energy barrier.31 The other is that the solid-state mass
transfer limitation and the interfacial reaction kinetic hysteresis
jointly restrict the charge transfer efficiency. Wang et al.32
developed an in situ testing platform for solid-state batteries.
Through real-time electron diffraction technology, they
observed the deposition/decomposition process of Li2S and
its structural evolution, revealed the crucial role of Li+ diffusion
kinetics in interfacial reversibility, and proposed that a heating
strategy can effectively promote Li+ conduction. This type of
research provides an important basis for understanding the
interface failure mechanism and optimizing electrode design.
However, the hypothesis of this one-step conversion
mechanism has been challenged. By combining in situ
Raman spectroscopy and ex-situ X-ray absorption spectrosco-
py, Cao et al.33 detected Li2S2. Combining the formation

Regarding the phenomenon of a single platform in the
discharge curve of ASSLSBs, Sun et al.36 proposed a possible
explanation: the Gibbs free energies of polysulfide inter-
mediates are similar, resulting in the coexistence of multiple
sulfur species in the chemical equilibrium reaction, making the
detection of specific lithium polysulfides extremely challenging.
In addition, solid sulfur reaches thermodynamic equilibrium
through a nonstepwise redox process, and intermediate sulfur
species cannot dissolve in the electrolyte. Eventually, a Li2S
layer is formed on the surface of S8. As the discharge
progresses, the interior of the sulfur particles is gradually
isolated from Li+/e−, leading to incomplete conversion. As a
result, the surface layer not only contains Li2S but may also
form on other sulfur species (such as Li2Sx, 8 ≥x ≥1). These
findings further emphasize the crucial role of Li2S2 in the
reaction mechanism of ASSLSBs and provide a new research
direction for optimizing battery performance (Figure 2b).
2.4. Electrochemical Perspectives. After the charge and
discharge start, electrochemical reactions occur at various
interfaces within the battery. For example, the redox potential
of Argyrodite is approximately 2.5 V (vs Li+/Li), while the
operating voltage window of the S/Li2S redox couple can reach
3.2 V. When the two materials come into contact, the
electrolyte undergoes electrochemical oxidation.37 Its decom-
position follows an indirect path: first, a metastable
intermediate product (such as Li4PS5Cl) is formed through
delithiation, which then rapidly transforms into insulating
Li3PS4, LiCl, and S.38 These products impede ion transport
and consume the electrolyte. The chemical potentials of the
electrode and the solid-state electrolyte are usually mis-
matched.22 As a result, a solid electrolyte interphase (SEI)
and a cathode electrolyte interphase (CEI) are formed on the
anode and cathode sides, respectively. An ideal SEI/CEI is a
passivation layer that is conductive to lithium ions and
insulating to electrons, thus preventing continuous decom-
position of the electrolyte and achieving a kinetically stable
state.39 However, the conductive agent in the composite
cathode can provide electrons, reducing the oxidation reaction
energy barrier. When the electron flux is too high, it accelerates
the decomposition of the electrolyte and the deterioration of
battery performance.
To address this issue, Nazar et al.40 found that by designing
a strong interfacial interaction between the sulfur host (a
carbon nitride/nitrogen-doped graphene composite system,
CNG) and argyrodite, the oxidation of argyrodite can be
inhibited. Specifically, the pyridine nitrogen groups in CNG
form a strong interaction with Li+ in argyrodite, which can
suppress the oxidation reaction kinetics of S2−→S0, shifting the
actual oxidation potential of argyrodite to a higher voltage
(Figure 2c). Experiments show that when the CNG-based
battery is charged to 4.1 V, the oxidation capacity of argyrodite
is only 46 mAh g−1, much lower than that of Vulcan carbon
(277 mAh g−1) and NG (200 mAh g−1), indicating that it
effectively broadens the electrochemical stability window of the
electrolyte in practical applications and slows down the
oxidative decomposition of the electrolyte at the source. This
enables the ASSLSBs to achieve 230 cycles with a capacity of 2
mAh cm−2 at room temperature and obtain a capacity of 11.3
mAh cm−2 at 60 °C with a capacity retention of 90%.
3. MULTI-COMPONENT SYNERGISTIC CATHODE
DESIGN
3.1. Effects of Fabrication Methods. Typical cathode
materials involve the collaborative design of S or Li2S-based
multicomponent compounds. The core challenge lies in
achieving effective contact among the interfaces of active
materials, ionic conductors, and electronic conductors. There-
fore, optimizing cathode manufacturing processes is crucial for
achieving uniform distribution and optimal particle sizes of the
components in composite materials. Most cathode preparation
methods borrow from liquid-phase manufacturing techniques,
such as solvent exchange, liquid-phase deposition, or vapor-
phase solidification. These methods aim to embed active
materials into conductive carbon carriers, thereby creating
intimate sulfur/carbon interfaces. In fact, different methods
result in varying utilization rates and cycle lifetimes.
In early research, the solvent exchange method was
considered an effective preparation strategy. This method
leverages the solubility differences of sulfur in various solvents
to achieve sulfur nanosizing and uniform dispersion through a
dissolution-recrystallization process. Chen et al.41 used the
solvent exchange method to prepare S-CNT composites. They
introduced carboxyl/hydroxyl functional groups by oxidizing
multiwall carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) with concentrated
nitric acid, enhancing sulfur affinity and dispersibility.
Subsequently, sulfur was dissolved in THF to form a saturated
solution, which was then mixed with an MWCNTs/SDBS
(sodium dodecyl benzenesulfonate) dispersion. Strong stirring
ensured uniform sulfur loading onto the carbon nanotubes.
Similarly, Du et al.42 used NMP as the solvent, mixing MCs/
NMP (multidimensional carbon nanomaterials/N-Methyl-2-
Pyrrolidone) colloidal suspension with S/NMP solution and
then precipitating it in water to obtain a composite with
uniform sulfur loading. Based on this method, Zhang et al.43
assembled ASSLSBs, achieving a discharge capacity of 1140.9
mA h g−1 at 0.1C.
However, the solvent exchange process involves multiple
steps, potentially increasing preparation costs and complexity,
while organic solvents also pose toxicity and environmental
pollution risks. Simpler and more effective methods, such as
liquid-phase deposition, have seen more development. Yao et
al.44 utilized sulfur-amine complexes formed by sulfur in
ethylenediamine, which were then dripped into a reduced
graphene oxide (rGO) dispersion, resulting in uniform sulfur
deposition on the rGO surface. The constructed rGO@S
nanocomposite achieved high ionic conductivity and low
stress/strain. This process effectively reduces sulfur particle

3.2. Multicomponent Cathode Design. In ASSLSBs, the
poor ionic/electronic conductivity of sulfur cathodes severely
limits their performance. To address this fundamental issue,
the introduction of carbon-based materials has become the
most direct and effective strategy. These materials form a
three-dimensional electron conduction network that facilitates
electron conduction and provides ion transport channels via its
controllable pore structure. The conductivity enhancement
effect is closely related to the specific surface area, porosity,
and heteroatom doping characteristics of carbon materials.

In recent years, mixed ionic-electronic conductors (MIEC)
have garnered significant attention as a material system due to
their ability to simultaneously facilitate lithium-ion and
electronic conductivity. Figure 5a,b illustrate the differences
in the solid-to-solid conversion reaction paths between

formed by sulfur, carbon, and the solid electrolyte. Sulfur that
moves away from this interface becomes inert sulfur, leading to
a low utilization of active materials (<50%). In MIEC-based
batteries, the MIEC provides continuous Li+ ion and e−
electron channels through its fast ion lattice and redox-active

boundary to a S/MIEC two-phase interface. Its reaction
pathway is characterized by the formation of embedded MIEC
nanodomains on the surface of sulfur particles, enabling the
nucleation and growth of Li2S inside the originally insulating
sulfur. The entire sulfur particle undergoes a layer-by-layer
conversion “from the inside out”, no longer relying on the
triple-phase interface. The key advantages brought about by
this include an increase in the reaction area, an improvement in
sulfur utilization, and an enhancement in the conversion rate of
the active material, thus significantly breaking through the
bottleneck of traditional sulfur−carbon composites limited by
the triple-phase interface. This has motivated extensive

MoS2,13 VS2,56 and TiS2.57 For instance, Nazar et al.56
developed a VS2-based MIEC to construct a core−shell

both minimize side reactions and utilize VS2’s extra capacity.

stability, maintaining Coulombic efficiency near 100% over
1000 cycles.

4.2. Interface Kinetics Optimization. 4.2.1. Electrolyte
Design. Electrolytes are key materials for the stable operation
of all-solid-state lithium−sulfur batteries. Compared with
traditional liquid electrolyte systems, the functional require-
ments of solid electrolytes are more diverse and stringent. It
needs to simultaneously shoulder multiple key functions such
as ion transport, chemical compatibility, and mechanical
integrity, which also leads to more detailed standards for its
performance indicators. An ideal solid-state electrolyte must
first possess high ionic conductivity, usually requiring a value
greater than 1 mS cm−1. This is the basis for ensuring that the
battery can efficiently conduct charge transfer. As the main
component for Li+ transport in the cathode, its volume content
and particle size determine the adequacy of the Li+ pathway.68
High ionic conductivity can ensure that the battery has good
rate performance,69 energy efficiency, and cycle stability during
charging and discharging processes. Low electronic con-
ductivity is also an essential performance requirement,70
preferably controlled below 10−10 S cm−1. Lower electronic
conductivity can effectively reduce the self-discharge phenom-
enon of the battery, avoiding unnecessary energy losses caused
by electron conduction. At the same time, this characteristic
can also prevent lithium metal deposition inside the electrolyte,
thereby reducing the probability of safety hazards such as
battery short-circuits. In terms of chemical stability, an ideal
solid-state electrolyte needs to be stable against water and air.
This means that in daily storage and use environments, it is not
easy to react chemically with the components in water and air,
resulting in performance degradation. This not only helps to
improve the storage life of the battery but also reduces the
maintenance costs and safety risks during the production,
transportation, and use of the battery. Good mechanical
properties are also crucial for solid-state electrolytes. It needs
to be able to densify the electrolyte layer under normal
temperature and low-pressure conditions to prevent problems
such as liquid leakage or short-circuits inside the battery. At the
same time, it also needs to ensure close contact with the
electrodes, reducing the interfacial resistance and improving
the overall performance of the battery.
Most notably, the design of solid-state electrolytes needs to
consider the instability of the electrode/electrolyte interface.
Therefore, antioxidant and antireduction stability are also key
properties for solid-state electrolytes to adapt to different
electrode environments. On the one hand, it needs to have
good stability against lithium metal to avoid chemical reactions
with the lithium metal electrode and damage the interface
structure.71 For the anode side, researchers mainly adopt the
strategy of constructing a SEI containing Li−In or Li−Sn alloy
phases. On the other hand, under high-voltage operating
conditions, it also needs to maintain stable chemical properties
and not decompose due to oxidation reactions, thus ensuring
that the battery can operate stably within a wide voltage
range.72 Zhu et al.73 revealed the high correlation between the
compatibility of sulfide electrolytes with sulfur cathodes and
the electrochemical behavior of ASSLSBs by comparing three
typical sulfide solid-state electrolytes, providing important
insights in this field. Research shows that the compatibility
between solid-state electrolytes and sulfur cathodes is highly
correlated with the electrochemical behavior of ASSLSBs.
Although ASSLSBs based on different electrolytes can achieve
a solid−solid interface between the cathode layer and the
electrolyte layer through good physical contact in the initial
state, when incompatible electrolytes (such as LPSI and
LGPS) are used, severe intergranular cracking occurs in the
battery, which may be caused by volume changes resulting
from electrolyte decomposition reactions.74 In addition, inert
reaction products will also hinder lithium-ion transport, leading
to a significant degradation of electrochemical performance.
An ideal solid-state electrolyte needs to achieve a synergistic
optimization in key indicators such as ionic conductivity,
electronic conductivity, chemical stability, electrochemical

theory analysis, it was found that the dz2 orbital of the Ni
single-atom transitions from an inert dsp2 hybrid state to an
active d2sp3 hybrid state. This vertical hybridization phenom-
enon significantly enhances the reaction kinetics of the
reduction from Li2S2 to Li2S. Electrochemical tests demon-
strated that ASSLSBs employing the NiNC catalyst delivered a
high capacity of 1506.9 mAh g−1 at a rate of 0.05C and
maintained over 70% capacity retention after 600 cycles at a
high rate of 2C (Figure 8b). This work, through an in-depth
study of the d-orbitals of nickel single atoms, revealed the
structure−activity relationship between the catalytic substrate
and active materials, providing new theoretical guidance for
developing high-energy-density ASSLSBs.
It is important to note that catalytic cathodes face a
fundamental challenge: the contact between the catalyst and
sulfur must occur at the atomic or molecular scale to effectively
promote electronic interactions and accelerate reaction
kinetics. Such intimate contact is difficult to achieve in solid-
state cathodes composed of mechanically mixed nanoparticles,
leading to interfacial mismatch and low reaction efficiency.
Therefore, developing strategies that simultaneously optimize
solid-state contact and minimize interfacial mismatch is crucial
for advancing the practical application of ASSLSBs. To address
this challenge, Yang et al.91 proposed a design combining
microporous confinement and single-atom cooperative catal-
ysis. This study stored sulfur in a microporous carbon matrix
with narrow pore size distribution and uniformly distributed
single-atom Co sites, achieving efficient solid-state sulfur
conversion. Experimental results showed that this design
reduced polysulfide dissolution by 98% and lowered the sulfur
conversion energy barrier by 0.35 eV. In the solid-state system,
the battery exhibited a reversible capacity of 1100 mAh g−1,
maintaining a Coulombic efficiency of 99.83% after 200 cycles,
demonstrating the effectiveness of this strategy in enhancing
reaction kinetics. Cao et al.88 also proposed an integrated
interfacial fusion strategy, which led to greater performance
breakthroughs. The team designed and prepared microporous
carbon sheets with an ultrahigh specific surface area (>3000 m2

These limitations have prompted researchers to explore redox
mediators (RMs) with global regulatory functions. RMs
represent a class of substances capable of reversible oxidation
and reduction during electrochemical cycling. They serve as
electronic bridges between electrodes and sulfur through their
redox cycles, thereby promoting sulfur activation and
conversion across broader spatial domains. In solid-state Li−
S batteries, RMs operate via a two-step mechanism: oxidized
RMs (RMox) chemically oxidize Li2S to LiPSs while being
reduced to RMred; RMred is then electrochemically reoxidized
at the electrode surface, completing the electron transfer cycle.
This mechanism enables RMs to electronically connect current
collectors with isolated Li2S particles, overcoming interfacial
transport limitations.
Recent studies have systematically investigated RM mech-
anisms in solid-state systems from multiple perspectives. Gao
et al.92 employed anthraquinone (AQT) as an organic RM,
leveraging its quinone redox properties to enhance polysulfide
redox reactions, achieving both high capacity and exceptional
cycling stability. Kwok et al.93 designed Li2S/LiVS2 core−shell
structures where the VS2 shell functions as a bifunctional
mediator, providing charge transport channels while the V4+/
V3+ redox couple serves as an electronic bridge. This
architecture delivered high areal capacity even at 6 mg cm−2
sulfur loading. Gao et al.94 developed a Cu+/I−codoping
strategy to activate Li2S kinetics, where Cu+ acts as RM while
I−optimizes Li+ diffusion paths, achieving 6.65-fold capacity
enhancement versus pristine Li2S. Yu et al.95 created Li2S−
LixIn2S3 composite cathodes where LixIn2S3 simultaneously
functions as ionic/electronic conductor and RM, demonstrat-
ing remarkable performance through this “trinity” mediation
mechanism. Pang et al.96 synthesized lithium thioborophos-
phate iodide (LBPSI) glass-phase solid-state electrolytes with
RMs incorporated into the electrolyte design. Using a melt-
quenching method, they prepared amorphous LBPSI electro-
lytes. When the molar ratio of P2S5 to (P2S5+B2S3) reached
0.17, the electrolyte achieved a room-temperature ionic
conductivity of 2.4 mS cm−1. The addition of P2S5 effectively
disrupted the original B−S network, forming mononuclear
bridging [BS4] structures that facilitate lithium-ion migration,
while weakening the interaction between iodide and lithium
ions to enhance surface diffusion. Crucially, LBPSI electrolytes
uniquely function as redox mediators in ASSLSBs. During
charging, I−at the LBPSI surface (boundary between solid
electrolyte and carbon) is electrochemically oxidized to I2 and
I3
−. These species chemically oxidize adjacent Li2S into S and
LiI via thermodynamic driving force. The regenerated I−then
rapidly diffuses back to participate in subsequent redox cycles
(Figure 9a). This surface-mediated process bypasses traditional
triple-phase boundary limitations, enabling efficient SE|Li2S
dual-phase reactions and significantly increasing active sites.
This strategy endows ASSLSBs with exceptional fast-charging
performance. At 30 °C, the battery delivers 1497 mAh g−1 at
2C and maintains 784 mAh g−1 even at 20C. Under 60 °C, it
achieves 432 mAh g−1 at an ultrahigh 150C rate (Figure 9b).
Cycling tests show 80.2% capacity retention after 25,000 cycles
at 5C (Figure 9c). While employing different RMs and designs,
these studies collectively address kinetic challenges in solid-
state sulfur batteries to achieve efficient solid−solid conversion.
5. FRONTIER CHALLENGES
5.1. High-Loading Cathodes. Li−S batteries have
emerged as promising next-generation energy storage systems
owing to their exceptional theoretical energy density. While
laboratory-scale research has demonstrated remarkable
achievements with low sulfur loadings (<2 mg cm−2), these
values remain inadequate for practical implementation.
Substantial evidence suggests that viable commercial applica-
tions require sulfur loadings exceeding 5 mg cm−2 coupled with
areal capacities surpassing 5 mAh cm−2.97,98 However, the
direct extrapolation from low-loading to high-loading config-
urations presents fundamental challenges, as it re-exposes the
intrinsic limitations of sulfur’s insulating properties and the
complex reaction interfaces. This dilemma has driven research
efforts that aim to boost sulfur loading while maintaining
electrochemical performance. The study of high sulfur loading
systems bridges lab research and real-world use, showing the
solutions can work reliably in larger systems.
The dry electrode preparation process offers the most direct
approach to increasing sulfur cathode loading. Lu et al.99
successfully fabricated a self-supporting sulfur cathode with
high flexibility and high sulfur loading (4.5 mg cm−2) using
poly(tetrafluoroethylene) (PTFE) as a binder. The high-
loading sulfur cathode prepared by the dry method
demonstrated excellent initial discharge capacity (1114.8
mAh g−1) and cycling stability (1067.4 mAh g−1 after 30
cycles), while the all-solid-state Li3.75Si/solid electrolyte/S
battery also exhibited superior electrochemical performance at
high current density (1 mA cm−2). This study not only
validates the potential of dry electrode technology in preparing
high-performance ASSLSBs but also provides crucial technical
support for the large-scale production of next-generation
energy storage systems.
To further enhance performance, beyond preparation
processes, the key lies in optimizing the electrode’s ion/
electron transport network and interface stability through
material designs. By controlling the microstructure and
component ratios of active materials, conductive agents, and
solid electrolytes, it is possible to improve charge transport
kinetics and mechanical integrity in high-loading electrodes.
Zhong et al.100 introduced a lithium phosphorus oxide coating
on carbon/sulfur particle surfaces using atomic layer
deposition technology. This coating not only suppressed
electrolyte decomposition and improved interface stability but
also served as an ion conduction layer and stress buffer,
promoting Li+ transport and mitigating contact failure issues.
They achieved high areal capacities of 4.6 mAh cm−2 (30 °C)
and 11.7 mAh cm−2 (60 °C) under high sulfur loading
conditions. Ulissi et al.101 demonstrated the potential of sulfur/
metal sulfide-based all-solid-state batteries under high loading
conditions through the design of a carbon−iron disulfide-sulfur
(C-FeS2−S) composite electrode, achieving an areal capacity
of 3.55 mAh cm−2 at 5 mg cm−2 loading. Building on this,
Whang et al.102 proposed a carbon-free transition metal sulfide
and lithium disulfide (FeS/Li2S) dual-cation−anion redox
composite cathode system. This system enhances electronic
conductivity through the cation redox of TMS while utilizing
the anion redox of Li2S to achieve high-voltage reactions,
significantly improving energy density. The study successfully
achieved an areal capacity of ∼4 mAh cm−2 with active
material loading exceeding 6.0 mg cm−2 and demonstrated the
potential for further increasing loading beyond 10 mg cm−2

5.2. Low-Temperature Performance. Achieving excel-
lent performance across all-climate operating environments
represents a critical challenge for the future development of
ASSLSBs, particularly in extreme conditions such as deep-sea,
outer space, and high-latitude regions of both hemispheres.103
The application of ASSLSBs across wide temperature ranges
(−40 to 120 °C) requires balanced performance at both high
and low temperatures, where the performance degradation of
ASSLSBs under low-temperature conditions remains a key
bottleneck hindering practical applications.
The performance degradation of ASSLSBs at low temper-
atures primarily manifests as reduced ionic conductivity,
increased interfacial impedance, and sluggish reaction
kinetics.104 Research indicates that the ion diffusion kinetics
in composite sulfur cathodes exhibit significant temperature
dependence, involving lithium-ion migration in the bulk solid
electrolyte, charge transfer at the electrode/electrolyte inter-
face, and lithium-ion diffusion within electrode materials.105 A
deep understanding of these microscopic processes is
fundamental to developing efficient low-temperature solid-
state batteries. Several studies have proposed solutions from
different perspectives. To optimize ion transport kinetics at low
temperatures, researchers have significantly accelerated charge
transfer and reduced polarization by developing amorphous
electrode materials and solid electrolytes with low activation
energy, providing insights for improving the low-temperature
performance of ASSLSBs. In the development of amorphous
cathode materials, Wu et al.106 designed a Mo0.5Ti0.5S4 cathode
material (sulfur-equivalent electrode) featuring a bimetallic
amorphous structure and sulfur-anion enrichment. By tuning
the elastic modulus through Ti doping, they promoted the
amorphization of MoS2 and suppressed S/Li2S separation from

temperatures involve multistep lithium-ion transport across
the electrolyte/interface phase and charge transfer at the

expansion coefficients between electrodes and solid electrolytes

continuous growth in interfacial impedance. However, due to
gaps in understanding the properties of inorganic electrolytes/
interface phases, the specific transport mechanisms and energy
barriers remain unclear. In reaction kinetics optimization, Sun
et al.34 proposed an approach. By precisely controlling the
discharge cutoff potential and introducing LiI as a catalyst, they
directed sulfur reduction products toward a Li2S2-dominated

maintain a reversible capacity of 979.6 mAh g−1 after 1,500

Li2S2 oxidation kinetics, effectively suppressing sulfur residue
and reducing electrode polarization.
These studies address the low-temperature performance
bottlenecks of ASSLSBs from various angles, collectively
advancing the development of high-performance ASSLSBs
for wide-temperature-range applications. Future research could
further explore the synergistic effects of these strategies to
achieve even more stable cycling performance at low
temperatures.
6. CONCLUSIONS
ASSLSBs represent a next-generation high energy density
energy storage system, and cathode material research is of great
significance. The core challenges on the cathode side primarily
stem from the unique characteristics of multiphase reaction
interfaces, specifically manifested in electrochemical degrada-
tion caused by interfacial side reactions, physical failure
induced by poor solid−solid contact, and multiscale charge
transport limitations. The key to overcoming these challenges
lies in multicomponent synergistic design, particularly through
the regulation of multifield coupling, from atomic-level
interface engineering to battery-level structural optimization,
to achieve the synergistic enhancement of high specific energy
and long cycle life. This review begins with the challenges of
cathode materials, thoroughly examining their mechanical and
(electro)chemical failure mechanisms. Then, the review
summarizes advancements in multicomponent cathode design,
interface engineering, and the two frontier challenges of high
loading and low-temperature performance. Building on these
strategies, significant progress has been made in improving the
cathode side of ASSLSBs. Nevertheless, several critical
scientific questions and technical challenges remain to be
addressed.
(1) In-depth investigation of the reaction mechanism: The
optimization of ASSLSBs requires detailed studies of the
electrochemical-mechanical-thermal coupling mecha-
nisms at the interface. Since the cathode is typically
composed of multicomponent nanocomposites, its
microstructure is complex and dynamically changing.
The nanoscale interactions (such as interface reactions
and ion transport) are difficult to directly observe.
Additionally, the formation of intermediate phases or
byproducts during the reaction further complicates the
mechanistic analysis. Therefore, a deeper understanding
of the potential redox mechanisms in ASSLSBs remains
a significant challenge in the field.
(2) Innovative materials system development: The develop-
ment and optimization of material systems focus on
three key areas: high-activity solid-state composite sulfur
cathodes, functional electrolytes, and composite lithium
metal anodes. High-activity solid-state composite sulfur
cathodes emphasize designing materials mediated by
redox reactions, promoting the solid-state sulfur reaction
kinetics through nanoscale solid−solid contact, and
reducing activation polarization. A hierarchical porous
carbon-based composite cathode is constructed to
establish an efficient 3D ion/electron transport network.
Functional sulfur-based electrolytes can be developed
with high ionic conductivity, excellent stability, and good
mechanical properties by using ion doping, anion
hybridization, and lithium vacancy optimization. Com-
posite lithium metal anodes require attention to
lightweight alloying strategies to prepare high areal
capacity and low-expansion composite lithium anodes.
(3) Battery architecture and process optimization: The
electrode preparation process requires continuous
refinement of dry processing techniques such as
calendaring to produce thick, dense electrodes. In
terms of electrolyte film formation, it is essential to
develop composite binder materials with high compat-
ibility. For the electrode interface, the development of
self-healing interface stabilization technologies or the
design of low-tortuosity mixed-conductive skeleton
structures based on electronic-ionic hybrid conductors
can be actively explored. Additionally, performance
optimization under low stack pressure during battery
assembly should also be addressed. Breakthroughs in
these areas will drive the advancement of battery
performance to new levels.
(4) Interdisciplinary advanced characterization: ASSLSBs
have long been limited by environmental constraints in

VOCABULARY
All-Solid-State Lithium−Sulfur Batteries (ASSLSBs): A
high-energy-density energy storage system that replaces
liquid electrolytes with solid electrolytes in traditional
lithium−sulfur batteries, fundamentally suppressing the
polysulfide shuttle effect, enhancing thermal safety, and
targeting a theoretical energy density of 600 Wh kg−1.
Three-Phase Boundary Reaction: The specific reaction
region in ASSLSB cathodes formed by the contact of sulfur,
conductive agents, and solid electrolytes; it is the core site
for electrochemical redox reactions, with its contact integrity
directly determining active material utilization and reaction
kinetic efficiency.
Mixed Ionic-Electronic Conductors (MIEC): Functional
materials that simultaneously enable lithium-ion and
electronic conduction, expanding the traditional “three-
phase boundary” to an “active material-MIEC two-phase
interface” to broaden the reaction area and accelerate sulfur
species conversion.
Cathode Electrolyte Interphase (CEI): A passivation film
spontaneously formed at the cathode-solid electrolyte
interface via chemical/electrochemical interactions during
cycling.
Redox Mediator (RM): Substances undergoing reversible
redox reactions during electrochemical cycling.
High Sulfur Loading: A indicator for ASSLSBs, referring to
cathode sulfur mass per unit area (>5 mg cm−2) matching
areal capacity (>5 mAh cm−2), which challenges ion/
electron transport network construction and interface
stability in thick electrodes.
