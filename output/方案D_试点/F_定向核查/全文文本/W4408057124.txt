Halide as Catholyte in Composite Cathode to Enhance Cycling
Stability of All-Solid-State Lithium−Sulfur Batteries

ABSTRACT: All-solid-state lithium−sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) using
inorganic solid-state electrolytes can effectively alleviate the polysulfide
shuttle effect in liquid electrolytes and improve the energy density.
However, the electrochemical window of sulfide-based catholytes in
composite cathodes is relatively narrow, which makes the evaluation of
electrochemical performance of sulfur cathodes in ASSLSBs complicated.
The decomposition of the sulfide catholytes increases the interfacial
resistance, thus reducing the battery cycle life. To overcome these
challenges, Li3YCl5I has been developed with a wide window of
electrochemical stability for a catholyte suitable for a composite cathode.
Its ionic conductivity is as high as 1.67 × 10−3 S cm−1, which is conducive to the rapid transport of lithium ions. The ASSLSB based
on the Li3YCl5I catholyte exhibits a discharge specific capacity of 1084.05 mAh g−1 at 45 °C. Additionally, it maintains 81.5%
capacity after 100 cycles, significantly exceeding the retention rate of 54.5% for a battery using Li6PS5Cl.
KEYWORDS: halide catholyte, cycling stability, iodine, all-solid-state, lithium−sulfur batteries
L
ithium−sulfur batteries (LSBs) have attracted consider-
able interest due to their environmentally benign active
material, sulfur, high theoretical specific capacity (1675 mAh
g−1), and high theoretical energy density (2600 Wh kg−1).1−5
However, the dissolution of lithium polysulfides formed in
liquid electrolyte-based LSBs and their diffusion to the lithium
anode are difficult to completely inhibit, resulting in active
material loss and rapid capacity decay, which hinders the
practical application of liquid LSBs.3,6−8 Furthermore, the
flammability of liquid electrolytes brings inevitable safety
risks.9,10 In contrast, all-solid-state lithium−sulfur batteries
(ASSLSBs) use solid electrolytes instead of liquid ones,
effectively mitigating the polysulfides shuttle effect and
improving safety.11,12
The solid-state catholytes (SSCEs) in the composite
cathodes largely determine the cyclic stability of ASSLSBs.13
Unlike liquid LSBs, where the liquid electrolytes can flow into
the voids of the composite cathodes composed of sulfur and
conductive carbon black to provide Li+ diffusion channels,
ASSLSBs utilize a large amount of SSCEs (approximately 50
wt %) to mix sulfur and conductive carbon black and then
cold-pressed to form the composite cathodes. To date, sulfide
SSCEs have been widely used in the composite cathodes of
ASSLSBs due to their ultrahigh ionic conductivity, minimal
interfacial side reactions with sulfur, and good compatibility
with Li−In alloy anodes.4,14−18 However, in the process of
charge and discharge, both sulfide SSCEs and sulfur participate
in the reactions, which affects the cyclic performance of sulfide-
based SSCE ASSLSBs.19−21 The sulfide SSCE can be used as a
cathode at 1.6−2.7 V vs Li+/Li,22 which overlaps with the
charge−discharge window of ASSLSBs. The decomposition of
sulfide SSCE results in the loss of its intrinsic high ionic
conductivity. For example, Li10GeP2S12,22 Li6PS5Cl,23 and
Li6PS5Cl0.5Br0.5
24 have been reported to undergo decom-
position reactions during charge−discharge to form ionic
insulating materials such as LiCl, Li3P, P2S5, GeS2, Li15Ge4, and
LiBr. Thus, to improve the cyclic stability of ASSLSBs, it is
necessary to develop SSCEs that are more stable than sulfides
to promote ionic conduction in the composite cathodes.
Compared with sulfides, halide solid-state electrolytes not
only have similar deformability and high ionic conductivity but
also have larger voltage windows, avoiding participating in the
charge−discharge reactions of ASSLSBs.25,26 Notably, during
the cycles, due to the migration of In3+ to the Li3YCl6 lattice, a
stable anode−electrolyte interphase layer composed of an In-
doped Li3−xInxYCl6 phase can be in situ formed, which makes
Li3YCl6 and the Li−In alloy anode have good compatibility.27
However, its ionic conductivity is limited to approximately
10−4 S cm−1, resulting in slow Li+ transport. More importantly,

Moreover, LiI is commonly used as an additive to the
composite cathodes of ASSLSBs to promote the electro-
chemical oxidation of Li2S2/Li2S.29 The introduction of I2
produces S9.3I, which has higher electronic conductivity than

cathode material for ASSLSBs, and can be heated to recover

appear to be effective alternative materials for constructing
composite cathodes with a long cycle stability in ASSLSBs.
In this work, halide solid-state electrolytes Li3YCl6−xIx (x =
0−3) were successfully synthesized by a mechanochemical
method. The ionic conductivity of the representative
Li3YCl6−xIx sample is as high as 1.67 × 10−3 S cm−1 at room
temperature. When used as an SSCE in the composite
cathodes of ASSLSBs, halide does not participate in the
charge−discharge reactions, resulting in a capacity retention
rate of 81.5% after 100 cycles, which is significantly higher than
54.5% of sulfide-based batteries. Furthermore, the good
compatibility with the Li−In alloy anode allows the halide to
effectively act as a solid-state electrolyte layer in ASSLSBs
without the need for a sulfide interlayer. The use of halide
SSCEs significantly improves the cycle stability and promotes
practical application of ASSLSBs.

by mechanical ball-milling method using a stoichiometric
mixture of LiCl, YCl3, and LiI. First, the ionic conductivity of
cold-pressed as-milled Li3YCl6−xIx (x = 0−3) pellets was
evaluated by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS).

To elucidate the mechanism of the ionic conductivity, the
structure and composition of Li3YCl6−xIx (x = 0−3) were
analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD). XRD patterns show that
Li3YCl6−xIx (x = 0−3) have obvious amorphous structures with
only a few characteristic peaks of the crystal phase (Figures 2a
and S4). The XRD peaks of Li3YCl6−xIx at x = 0 are consistent
with those of the reference Li3YCl6 phase (PDF#44-0286).
However, when x ≤1, the characteristic peaks at 31.3°, 40.8°,
and 48.8° shift to smaller angles as the iodine content
increases, suggesting that doping of larger iodine ions (r(Cl−)
= 167 pm, r(I−) = 206 pm32) leads to the expansion of the unit
cell. The wide peak at about 42° in the XRD pattern of
Li3YCl5I matches that of Li3YI6.33 For Li3YCl6−xIx (x = 1.5−3),
more new peaks corresponding to Li3YI6 appear next to the
peaks of Li3YCl6. Although Li3YI6 exhibits characteristic peaks
similar to those of LiI in the XRD patterns, no precipitated LiI
phase was observed in the scanning electron microscopy
(SEM) images and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy
(EDS) spectra of Li3YCl6−xIx (x = 0−3) (Figure S2). The
room-temperature ionic conductivity of Li3YI6 was reported to
be 7.0 × 10−4 S cm−1,33 which is lower than that of Li3YCl5I
(1.67 × 10−3 S cm−1). By observing the ionic conductivity
change of Li3YCl6−xIx (x = 0−3) (Figure 1c), it can be inferred
that when x ≤1, due to the larger ionic radius and the weaker
electronegativity of I−than Cl−, the doped unit cell volume
expands and the lattice confinement on Li+ is weakened,34 thus
increasing the ionic conductivity. When x ≥1, I−can no longer
incorporate into the lattice of Li3YCl6, forming Li3YI6 with a

The solid-state electrolyte materials Li3YCl6−xIx (x = 0−3)
have shown a high ionic conductivity and excellent
deformability. However, the SSCE of an ASSLSB with a
composite cathode or a standalone solid-state electrolyte layer
must have a wide electrochemical stability window to prevent
participation in the charge−discharge reactions of the battery.
To investigate the electrochemical stability window of
Li3YCl6−xIx (x = 0−3), linear sweep voltammetry (LSV)
tests were performed (Figure S5a). The results show that the
electrochemical window of Li3YCl5I is approximately 0.89−
3.25 V vs Li+/Li, which exceeds the operating voltage range of
sulfur (1.5−2.8 V vs Li+/Li),39 so Li3YCl5I can be stably used
as an SSCE of the ASSLSB composite cathode. To further
confirm the stability between Li3YCl5I and the Li−In alloy
anode, we constructed the Li−In|Li3YCl5I|Li−In symmetric
battery, which can stably deposit and strip for 200 h at
constant current (Figure S5b), demonstrating the stability of
Li3YCl5I on the Li−In alloy anode. Additionally, in situ analysis
has been used to investigate the interfacial morphological/
chemical evolution and dynamics of the Li metal/solid
electrolyte interface.40,41 For the Li−In|Li3YCl5I|Li−In sym-
metric battery, in situ EIS for the first 20 cycles and X-ray
photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis of the Li3YCl5I|Li−
In interface after cycles were performed. The interfacial
resistance between the Li3YCl5I and Li−In electrode remains
basically stable during the cycles, and no byproducts are
observed in the Y 3d high-resolution XPS spectrum of the
Li3YCl5I|Li−In interface (Figure S6). The stability of Li3YCl5I
for the Li−In alloy anode avoids the use of a buffer layer
between the halide and the Li−In alloy anode, which reduces
the thickness of the electrolyte layer and increases the energy
density of the ASSLSB.
The halide Li3YCl6−xIx (x = 0−3) has a wider electro-
chemical stability window than that of the sulfide Li6PS5Cl,
making it an SSCE in ASSLSBs and a solid-state electrolyte
layer with a Li−In alloy anode. The decomposition of sulfide
SSCE in the cathode leads to the loss of ion transport
pathways, further reducing the overall performance of the
batteries,21 so the cyclic stability of the halide-based SSCE
ASSLSBs is expected to exceed that of sulfide-based ASSLSBs.
As shown in Figure 3a, the S+Li3YCl5I|Li3YCl5I|Li−In battery
presents two discharge plateaus of about 2.03 and 1.93 V,
representing the electrochemical reactions from S8 to Li2S2 and
then to Li2S,42 with a specific capacity of 818.14 mAh g−1. The
two charge plateaus at 2.44 and 2.66 V represent the reversible
reactions, producing a specific capacity of 735.85 mAh g−1 with
a first cycle Coulombic efficiency of 90%. Under the identical
test conditions, the specific discharge and charge capacity of
the S+Li6PS5Cl|Li6PS5Cl|Li−In battery are 938.83 mAh g−1
and 1136.18 mAh g−1, respectively, and the first cycle
Coulombic efficiency is 121%. Notably, in all-solid-state
batteries with a single cathode active material, the first cycle
Coulombic efficiency is usually less than 100% due to the loss
of part of the lithium during lithium-ion deintercalation. The
first cycle Coulombic efficiency of the S+Li6PS5Cl|Li6PS5Cl|
Li−In battery is 121%, indicating that multiple active materials
are involved in the charge−discharge reactions. Compared
with the S+Li3YCl5I|Li3YCl5I|Li−In battery, the S+Li6PS5Cl|
Li6PS5Cl|Li−In battery exhibits similar discharge plateaus, but
more obvious charge plateaus, which may be related to a
second active material in the composite cathode (Figure 3b).
During the charging process, the sulfide SSCE in the cathode,
with the carbon additive, decomposes into electrochemically
active species on the highly electrochemically conductive
surface.21 To evaluate whether Li6PS5Cl provides additional
capacity for the S+Li6PS5Cl|Li6PS5Cl|Li−In battery, we
assembled a Li6PS5Cl|Li6PS5Cl|Li−In battery using Li6PS5Cl
as the cathode material. The specific discharge and charge
capacity of the battery at 0.17 mA g−1 are 119.96 mAh g−1 and
171.86 mAh g−1, respectively, and the specific discharge
capacity of 114.03 mAh g−1 is maintained after 200 cycles
(Figure S7). This suggests that in the S+Li6PS5Cl|Li6PS5Cl|Li−
In battery, Li6PS5Cl does indeed break down and participate in
the charge−discharge reactions. During the charging process,
Li6PS5Cl can be oxidized to produce sulfur, P2Sx (x ≥5),
lithium polysulfides, and LiCl. Then during the discharge
process, decomposition products including sulfur, P2Sx (x ≥
5), and lithium polysulfides can be reduced to form Li2S and
Li3P.43−46 These ionic insulation materials lead to the loss of
the Li+ transport pathways in the composite cathode of the S
+Li6PS5Cl|Li6PS5Cl|Li−In battery, and the capacity retention
rate is only 54.5% after 100 cycles (Figure 3c). In contrast, the
S+Li3YCl5I|Li3YCl5I|Li−In battery retains 81.5% capacity after
100 cycles, because sulfur is the only active material.
Furthermore, the batteries before and after the cycles were
analyzed by using the galvanostatic intermittent titration
technique (GITT), as it accurately reflects the changes in Li+
mobility and internal resistance within the ASSLSBs. For the S
+Li3YCl5I|Li3YCl5I|Li−In battery, the cycle performance
observed by the GITT curves after 50 cycles is more stable
than that of the S+Li6PS5Cl|Li6PS5Cl|Li−In battery (Figure 3d
and g). In addition, the S+Li3YCl5I|Li3YCl5I|Li−In battery has
significantly less difference in overpotential related to the open-
circuit voltage before and after the cycles (Figure 3e, f, h, and
i), indicating less variation in interface resistance and more
stable lithium-ion transport kinetics. The results demonstrate
that the decrease of the Li+ mobility in the composite cathode
caused by Li6PS5Cl decomposition is the main factor leading to
the deterioration of the battery cycle performance.

Additionally, the XPS spectra of the composite cathode before

For the S+Li3YCl5I|Li3YCl5I|Li−In battery, there is almost no

The development of SSCEs that do not participate in the
charge−discharge reactions of the composite cathode is critical
for the cyclic stability of ASSLSBs. Halides have demonstrated
promising potential in this regard. To further evaluate the
electrochemical performance of Li3YCl5I SSCE in the
composite cathode, the rate and long-cycle performances of
the S+Li3YCl5I|Li3YCl5I|Li−In battery were tested at 45 °C. At
0.01 C (1C = 1675 mA g−1), the initial discharge specific
capacity is 1084.05 mAh g−1 and the Coulombic efficiency is
91% (Figure 5a). At rates of 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4, and 0.5 C, the
discharge specific capacities of the battery are 930.9, 734.03,
611.66, 534.4, and 476.49 mAh g−1, respectively (Figure 5b
and c). When the rate is reduced from 0.5 C to 0.1 C, the
battery can recover the discharge specific capacity of 919.6
mAh g−1. After 200 cycles at 0.5 C, the battery capacity
retention rate reaches 70% (Figure 5d), which is significantly
higher than the about 46% of other similar batteries.47 It is
worth noting that all tests of the battery were carried out at a
relatively low temperature of 45 °C, which is conducive to
practical application of ASSLSBs. Additionally, the cycle
performance of the S+Li3YCl5I|Li3YCl5I|Li−In battery at
room temperature was tested at 0.1 C. The initial discharge
capacity is 385.65 mAh g−1, and the 50th cycle discharge
capacity is 365.03 mAh g−1 (Figure S10).
In summary, we demonstrate a strategy that uses halide
SSCEs to replace sulfide SSCEs to improve the cycling stability
of ASSLSBs. The halide Li3YCl5I with a room-temperature
ionic conductivity of 1.67 × 10−3 S cm−1 was prepared by
doping iodine into pristine Li3YCl6 using a simple mechanical
ball-milling. Compared with the Li6PS5Cl-based SSCE
composite cathode, the Li3YCl5I SSCE composite cathode
has a smaller interfacial resistance change and more stable
lithium-ion transport kinetics during the cycles due to the
inhibition of decomposition reactions. Consequently, the
ASSLSB with Li3YCl5I SSCE demonstrates excellent cyclic
stability, and the capacity retention rate is 81.5% after 100
cycles at 0.1 C. Using halide with a wide electrochemical
window as SSCE of the ASSLSB composite cathode, the cycle
stability of the battery is significantly improved.
