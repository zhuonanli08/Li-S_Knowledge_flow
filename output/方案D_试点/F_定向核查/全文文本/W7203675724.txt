ABSTRACT
Transition-metal sulfide (TMS) catalysis is a promising strategy for accelerating sulfur redox kinetics, 
yet its catalytic mechanisms in all-solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) remain poorly 
understood. Here, two distinct catalytic failure modes are identified in conversion-type and 
intercalation-type TMS catalysts, namely, phase-separation-driven catalyst self-poisoning and 
transition-metal-migration-induced electrolyte degradation, respectively. Guided by these insights, we 
develop an all-in-one high-entropy sulfide (HES) catalyst that undergoes a solid-solution reaction to 
form a stable Lix HES phase. This Lix HES functions as both a dual ion-electron conductor, expanding 
the electrochemically active reaction zone, and an interfacial redox mediator, driving spontaneous Li2 S 
oxidation, thereby promoting extensive and rapid S ↔ Li2 S conversion. With only 3 wt% HES, the 
S@HES cathode (40 wt% S) achieves 93.4% sulfur utilization and 93.0% conversion efficiency. Even at 
50 wt% sulfur, the cell retains a capacity of 900 mAh g−1 at 5 mA cm−2 (2C) over 250 cycles. 
Furthermore, a high areal capacity of 9.68 mAh cm−2 is delivered under demanding conditions (8.5 mg 
cm−2 , 30°C), highlighting the great promise of the HES catalytic strategy for energy-dense ASSLSBs. 
Keywords: all-solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries, catalytic mechanisms, high-entropy sulfide 
catalyst, high-sulfur-content cathodes 
INTRODUCTION 
The urgent demand for advanced energy stor- 
age systems has accelerated the development of 
post-lithium-ion technologies. Among them, 
conversion-chemistry-based lithium–sulfur bat- 
teries (Li–S) are particularly attractive owing to 
their exceptionally high theoretical energy density 
( ∼2600 Wh kg−1 ) and the natural abundance 
of sulfur [1 ,2 ]. However, the practical applica- 
tion of liquid-electrolyte-based Li–S batteries 
has been plagued by the notorious polysulfide 
shuttle and associated safety concerns [3 ]. Sub- 
stituting liquid electrolytes with non-flammable 
inorganic solid-state electrolytes (SSEs) offers 
a viable route to fundamentally eliminate the 
shuttle effect and ensure safety, while additionally 
enabling bipolar stacking to maximize energy 
density. Despite these advantages, the realiza- 
tion of high-performance all-solid-state Li–S 
batteries (ASSLSBs) is still markedly constrained 
by the sluggish solid-state sulfur redox kinetics. 
Such kinetic limitations arise primarily from the 
insulating nature of sulfur species that confines 
reactions to sparse triple-phase boundaries (TPBs) 
[4 ], as well as the high energy barrier for solid-to- 
solid conversion. More critically, sluggish kinetics 
induce uncontrolled and inhomogeneous Li2 S 
growth, aggravating the intrinsic volume fluctua- 
tions and generating localized stress. These effects 
lead to severe mechanical failure, disruption of

the conductive network and ultimately inferior

To overcome these kinetic issues, extensive ef- 
forts have focused on enhancing ionic and elec- 
tronic conduction throughout the sulfur cathode 
alongside maximizing active reaction interfaces, 
which involves strategies such as developing SSEs 
with superior ionic conductivity [6 ,7 ], construct- 
ing high-surface-area conductive networks [8 ,9 ] 
and introducing mixed ionic-electronic conduc- 
tors [10 ,11 ]. Although current advances have de- 
livered near-theoretical sulfur capacity, efficient 
sulfur conversion at high content ( > 40 wt%) 
and elevated rates ( ≥1C) remains challenging 
( Table S1), severely limiting the practical energy 
and power densities of ASSLSBs. The high sul- 
fur content in the composite cathode inevitably 
reduces the proportion of conductive carbon or 
SSE. In such scenarios, sulfur tends to aggregate, 
which disrupts ionic and electronic percolation 
networks and diminishes accessible reaction inter- 
faces [10 ]. These deficiencies, compounded by the 
substantial energy barrier for S/Li2 S transforma- 
tion, further exacerbate the sluggish redox kinet- 
ics [12 –14 ]. Consequently, unlocking high-sulfur- 
content cathodes necessitates a synergistic strategy 
that ensures efficient carrier transport and interfa- 
cial availability, while simultaneously lowering the 
reaction energy barrier. 
For liquid Li–S systems, the incorporation of 
catalysts has proven to be highly beneficial for 
simultaneously enriching reaction interfaces and 
lowering kinetic barriers [15 ,16 ]. Among them, 
transition-metal sulfides (TMSs; e.g. TiS2 [17 ], 
MoS2 [18 ], CoS2 [19 ], VS2 [20 ] and LiFeS2 [21 ]) 
are widely recognized as promising candidates due 
to their high electronic/ionic conductivity and 
strong catalytic capability, which can effectively 
promote sulfur redox kinetics. However, extend- 
ing such success to ASSLSBs remains far from 
trivial, as the catalytic processes in liquid systems 
are predicated on intimate interfacial wetting, a 
feature inherently absent in solid-state environ- 
ments. This disparity renders the catalytic mecha- 
nisms of TMSs in ASSLSBs largely unknown, par- 
ticularly regarding solid-state catalytic pathways, 
catalyst–SSE interfacial compatibility and catalyst 
failure, which are critical to the rational design 
of catalysts for ASSLSBs with high-sulfur-content 
cathodes [22 ]. 
In this work, we systematically investigate rep- 
resentative TMS catalysts within the operating 
voltage range of sulfur conversion, identifying 
two different failure mechanisms: phase sepa- 
ration in conversion-type FeS2 and transition- 
metal (TM) migration in intercalation-type TiS2 . 
These phenomena induce catalyst self-poisoning 
and electrolyte degradation, thereby fundamen- 
tally compromising electrochemical performance. 
To address these limitations, we propose an all-in- 
one strategy by rationally designing and synthe- 
sizing a pyrite-type high-entropy sulfide (HES) as 
a catalyst for high-sulfur-content cathodes. Com- 
bined experimental and theoretical analyses re- 
veal that HES evolves into a stable Lix HES phase 
via a solid-solution reaction. Lix HES integrates 
dual ion-electron conductivity with intrinsic Li2 S- 
oxidizing capability, functioning as an all-in-one 
redox mediator to promote sulfur conversion 
across extensive two-phase interfaces, accelerate 
redox kinetics and maximize sulfur utilization. As 
a result, merely 3 wt% of HES catalyst endows 
the S@HES cathode with excellent rate capability 
and cycling stability at high sulfur contents (40–
50 wt%) and broad mass loadings. Moreover, the 
catalytic robustness of HES is further validated in 
a practical full cell paired with a Li3.75 Si anode, 
delivering a high gravimetric energy density of 
571 Wh kg−1 (N/P ratio = 1.6). 
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 
Failure mechanisms of conventional TMS 
catalysts 
Based on different lithium storage mechanisms, 
TMSs are generally categorized into conversion- 
type and intercalation-type materials [23 ]. 
Conversion-type TMSs typically deliver high 
specific capacities through multi-electron redox 
reactions, albeit at the cost of electrochemi- 
cal reversibility. In contrast, intercalation-type 
TMSs operate via single-electron redox processes 
within a stable anionic framework, enabling fast 
kinetics but limited capacities ( < 250 mAh g−1 ) 
( Fig. S1). To evaluate the catalytic potential of 
TMSs in ASSLSBs, we investigated the intrin- 
sic electrochemical behaviors of prototypical 
conversion-type FeS2 and intercalation-type TiS2 
cathodes within the typical voltage window of 
1.0–3.0 V (potentials vs. Li+ /Li unless otherwise 
stated) [10 ,24 ]. As shown in Fig. 1 a, and Fig. S2a
and b, both cells experience severe capacity decay 
during the initial five discharge–charge cycles 
and exhibit severe polarization when return- 
ing to 0.1C after high-rate cycling at 1C. The 
origins of their performance degradation were 
subsequently probed using differential capacity 
(d Q /d V ) analysis. For the cell with FeS2 cathode, 
the discrete and intense redox peaks confined to 
two narrow voltage regions ( Fig. S2c) suggest a 
conversion-dominated mechanism involving the

Rational design and 
mechanical–electrochemical properties 
of HES catalysts 
The catalyst self-poisoning and electrolyte degra- 
dation discussed above shed light on the intrinsic 
limitations of binary TMSs in ASSLSBs, and also 
highlight the necessity of preventing phase separa- 
tion and TM migration during sulfur conversion 
to preserve structural integrity for effective solid- 
state sulfur catalysis. Motivated by these insights, 
we propose an all-in-one high-entropy strategy 
for TMSs, as illustrated in Fig. 2 a. In principle, 
high configurational entropy tends to improve 
the thermodynamic stability of the solid-solution 
phase against phase separation, while sluggish 
diffusion helps to kinetically hinder long-range 
TM migration, both of which are beneficial for 
maintaining a structurally stable framework with 
continuous ionic and electronic transport during 
cycling [28 ]. Moreover, the high-entropy frame- 
work is expected to impart enhanced chemome- 
chanical robustness to accommodate the strain 
induced by sulfur volume fluctuations, ensuring 
durable interfacial contact [29 ]. Beyond structural 
stability, the chemically diverse local environment 
is capable of improving surface sulfiphilicity and 
regulating the electronic structure, thereby facili- 
tating sulfur adsorption, activation and interfacial 
charge transfer [30 ]. Together, these effects 
are believed to enable faster solid-state sulfur 
conversion. 
To implement this strategy, we selected the 
pyrite-type lattice as the structural prototype, 
due to its compositional flexibility and intrinsic

catalytic capability [31 ]. A systematic screening 
of binary pyrite-type TMSs was conducted using 
the Materials Project database to identify candi- 
date HESs. The key selection criteria include (i) 
thermodynamic formability, defined by formation 
energies within 50 meV atom−1 above the con- 
vex hull to ensure synthetic feasibility [32 ], and 
(ii) lattice compatibility, quantified by a lattice 
mismatch parameter ( δ) that guarantees the for- 
mation of a single-phase solid solution. By em- 
ploying this screening protocol, a quinary (Fe, 
Co, Ni, Cu, Ru)S2 system was determined to be a 
suitable formulation (Fig. 2 b). Within this multi- 
component framework, the constituent elements 
play distinct yet complementary roles. Specifi- 
cally, Fe, Co, Ni and Cu serve as redox-active 
centers, providing a continuous reduction voltage 
range (1.3–2.15 V, experimentally evidenced by 
the d Q /d V profiles in Fig. S6). This range overlaps 
well with the voltage window for sulfur reduc- 
tion (1.3–2.0 V, inset in Fig. 2 b), which favors ef- 
ficient charge transfer. Although Ru remains elec- 
trochemically inactive within this voltage range 
[33 ], its higher valence state and stronger metal–
sulfur bond render it a critical structural anchor, 
reinforcing the rigidity of the high-entropy frame- 
work. 
Guided by the aforementioned bottom-up de- 
sign rationale, the quinary Fe-Co-Ni-Cu-Ru HES 
was synthesized via high-energy ball milling of the 
constituent metal powders and elemental sulfur, 
followed by a high-temperature, high-pressure 
treatment [34 ]. Notably, the redox-active centers 
(Fe, Co, Ni and Cu) were weighed in a near- 
equimolar ratio, while the Ru content was in- 
tentionally reduced, as Ru is redox-inactive un- 
der the operating conditions and primarily serves 
as a structural stabilizer. SEM images in Fig. S7
show that the as-synthesized product has an ir- 
regular morphology, with the particle sizes read- 
ily controlled by post-ball milling. XRD analy- 
sis confirms the formation of a high-purity cubic 
pyrite-type HES phase (space group P a¯3 ), as evi- 
denced by the complete absence of impurity peaks 
( Fig. S8). Furthermore, compared with the con- 
stituent binary sulfides, the diffraction patterns 
exhibit a slight peak shift, which indicates success- 
ful multi-element integration and the resulting 
variation in the lattice parameter ( Fig. S9). To de- 
termine the elemental stoichiometry, inductively 
coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy was 
performed. The result reveals a metal composi- 
tion of Fe0.23 Co0.21 Ni0.24 Cu0.2 Ru0.12 , which closely 
matches the designed stoichiometry ( Table S2). 
According to this composition, the configura- 
tional entropy ( Sconf ) is calculated to be 1.58R 
per unit cell, exceeding the empirical threshold 
for high-entropy materials (1.5R), which there- 
fore substantiates the high-entropy nature of the 
synthesized HES. The atomic-level structure of 
HES was further investigated using high-angle an- 
nular dark-field (HAADF) imaging in scanning 
transmission electron microscopy (STEM) mode. 
As presented in Fig. 2 c, the energy-dispersive 
X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mappings of 
Fe, Co, Ni, Cu and Ru present nearly homoge- 
neous atomic-scale distribution without notice- 
able clustering or segregation. This observation 
supports the formation of a single-phase solid so- 
lution featuring a homogeneously mixed cationic 
arrangement. Complementing the imaging re- 
sults, the extended X-ray absorption fine struc- 
ture (EXAFS) spectra of the HES demonstrate pre- 
dominant coordination of the constituent metals, 
with M–S bond lengths mainly distributed around 
∼2.2 Å (in R-space) ( Fig. S10), suggesting a highly 
uniform local coordination environment. 
After the successful synthesis of HES, we sys- 
tematically investigated the intrinsic advantages 
conferred by this high-entropy configuration. 
Rietveld refinement of the XRD patterns deter- 
mined a lattice parameter of a = 5.609 Å for the 
HES. The corresponding unit-cell volume is larger 
than that of FeS2 , which reflects a distorted lattice 
caused by the size mismatch of the multiple in- 
corporated cations ( Tables S3 and S4). Crucially, 
this complex cationic incorporation also induces 
the formation of sulfur vacancies, as confirmed 
via an electron paramagnetic resonance signal at 
g = 2.01 ( Fig. S11). Together, the expanded lat- 
tice and sulfur vacancies are anticipated to ac- 
celerate Li+ ion diffusion and buffer volumetric 
strain. To assess the strain-buffering capability, 
single-particle compression tests were conducted. 
As shown in Fig. S12, the HES shows substantially 
higher fracture resistance (1018 MPa), with pro- 
gressive deformation and delayed crack propaga- 
tion. In comparison, FeS2 fractures rapidly break- 
ing into fine debris under much lower applied 
stress (260 MPa). This improved mechanical ro- 
bustness allows the HES to accommodate the se- 
vere volume expansion of sulfur during conver- 
sion, preventing structural collapse. 
Along with these physical structural benefits, 
density functional theory (DFT) calculations were 
carried out to elucidate the electronic structure 
evolution induced by the high-entropy configu- 
ration. As plotted in Fig. S13a, FeS2 has a semi- 
conducting band gap of 0.96 eV, whereas HES 
presents a non-zero density of states (DOS) at 
the Fermi level, indicative of a metallic nature. 
Further projected DOS analysis clarifies that this

semiconductor-to-metal transition stems from 
electronic reconstruction, where the incorpora- 
tion of diverse TM species introduces additional 
d -orbital states near the Fermi level ( Fig. S13b
and c). Such a metallic framework promises a 
markedly accelerated kinetics for charge transfer, 
thereby driving electrochemical reactions. To ex- 
perimentally verify these theoretical predictions, 
in situ EIS measurements were performed, which 
revealed negligible resistance growth during the 
first cycle in the HES-based cell ( Fig. S14a). These 
findings highlight the multifunctionality of the 
HES design: its metallic electronic structure pro- 
vides high electronic conductivity, while its ex- 
panded, defect-rich lattice enables rapid ionic dif- 
fusion. 
Driven by these structural and electronic mod- 
ifications, the overall lithium-storage mechanism 
of the HES is fundamentally altered. As evidenced 
in Fig. S14b, the HES cell displays a continuous 
and sloping voltage profile, in sharp contrast to 
the distinct plateaus of the conversion reaction, 
consistent with a solid-solution mechanism rather 
than a discrete two-phase process. This mecha- 
nism is also corroborated by in situ XRD, showing 
that the pristine HES diffraction peaks gradually 
attenuate during discharge without the appear- 
ance of new peaks ( Fig. S15a and b). Notably, even 
upon deep discharge to 1.3 V, crystalline Li2 S, the 
typical conversion product of TMS, remains un- 
detectable in XRD patterns of the discharged HES 
( Fig. S15c). The absence of detectable Li2 S reflec- 
tions suggests that the discharge products are pre- 
dominantly amorphous or nanocrystalline, falling 
below the detection limit of XRD. To further de- 
termine whether Li2 S was formed, solid-state nu- 
clear magnetic resonance (ssNMR) was employed. 
Specifically, a T2 -filtering sequence was imple- 
mented to separate the overlapping signals. By se- 
lectively suppressing the short-T2 7 Li signal while 
retaining the longer-T2 component, the filter en- 
ables reliable spectral discrimination. As presented 
in Fig. 2 d and Fig. S16, 7 Li ssNMR with T2 fil- 
tering clearly distinguishes the Li+ ion signals at 
−0.12 ppm in the HES lattice and 0.45 ppm in the 
solid Li7 P3 S11 (LPS) [35 ]. Crucially, the absence 
of Li2 S resonance ( ∼2.5 ppm [36 ]) after discharg- 
ing HES to 1.3 V rules out a conversion reaction 
and instead supports a solid-solution process to 
form the Lix HES phase. Upon recharging to 3.1 V, 
the intensity of the Lix HES signal decreases signif- 
icantly, indicating substantial and reversible Li+ 
ion extraction. Moreover, EXAFS analysis reveals 
that the local metal–sulfur coordination environ- 
ment in HES remains largely preserved during cy- 
cling, with no signal of extensive bond rearrange- 
ment or metallic clustering (Fig. 2 e and Fig. S17). 
Collectively, these measurements substantiate that 
HES operates via a robust solid-solution pathway, 
effectively circumventing the detrimental phase 
separation and large-scale atomic reconstruction 
typically seen in conversion reactions. 
At the macroscopic electrode level, a di- 
rect consequence of this non-destructive solid- 
solution mechanism is its capability to signifi- 
cantly mitigate volume expansion during cycling. 
As expected, the operando electrochemical 
pressure measurements demonstrate minimal 
pressure fluctuations and excellent mechanical 
reversibility in the HES cell (Fig. 2 g and Fig. S18). 
Remarkably, the HES cell exhibits a pressure 
increase rate of only 1.8 N h−1 during the first 
discharge, comparable to that of the intercalation- 
type TiS2 cell (2.0 N h−1 ) but an order of mag- 
nitude lower than that of the conversion-type 
FeS2 cell (14.3 N h−1 ). Such alleviated chemo- 
mechanical strain ultimately helps to maintain 
the morphological and compositional integrity of 
the electrodes. This is visually demonstrated by 
cross-sectional FIB-SEM imaging, which shows 
intact particles without phase separation after full 
discharge (Fig. 2 f). Furthermore, ToF-SIMS map- 
ping ( Fig. S19) verifies the chemical uniformity at 
a microscopic level without TM migration. 
Benefiting from the synergy of fast kinet- 
ics, a favorable solid-solution reaction mechanism 
and robust mechanical toughness, the cell with 
a micron-sized HES cathode maintains stable cy- 
cling across all tested rates, and most notably, it 
preserves the excellent cyclability upon returning 
to 0.1C after prolonged operation at 1C ( Fig. S20). 
According to the above results, we believe that 
HES holds considerable potential as a catalyst for 
extending the operation of ASSLSBs.

Origin of superior electrochemical 
performance 
To elucidate the origin of the superior electro- 
chemical performance, d Q /d V analysis was con- 
ducted to probe the redox behavior of the S@HES 
and S@AB cathodes. As shown in Fig. 4 a, two 
well-defined cathodic peaks at ∼1.92 and ∼1.84 V 
(labeled as peaks i and ii) are detected in the 
S@AB cathode, corresponding to the stepwise re- 
duction of sulfur to Li2 S2 intermediates and finally 
to Li2 S, consistent with previous studies. In con- 
trast, the cathodic peaks in the S@HES cathode 
present an ∼36 mV positive shift and increased 
intensity, reflecting reduced polarization and en- 
hanced sulfur utilization. These combined advan- 
tages are also observed in the subsequent charging 
process. Specifically, compared to the broad an- 
odic peak at ∼2.40 V (peak iv) in the S@AB cath- 
ode, the anodic peak of the S@HES cathode be- 
comes sharper and more intense at a slightly lower 
potential, indicating faster and more thorough ox- 
idation of Li2 S back to sulfur. We attribute this 
notable difference in the S@HES cathode to the 
redox-activated catalytic mechanism of HES. Cru- 
cially, the catalytic activity of HES is unlocked by a 
redox reaction that precedes sulfur reduction and 
Li2 S oxidation, as evidenced by the cathodic peak 
at ∼2.06 V (peak iii). During this process, HES 
transitions via a solid-solution pathway, forming a 
dual-carrier conducting (DCC) Lix HES phase that 
provides robust active sites for subsequent sulfur 
conversion. Consequently, the sulfur redox reac- 
tion zone expands from the limited TPBs to abun- 
dant S/Li2 S|Lix HES interfaces, thereby facilitat- 
ing rapid redox kinetics and more complete sul- 
fur utilization. The unique catalytic advantage of 
HES is further supported by the d Q /d V analysis of 
S@FeS2 and S@TiS2 ( Fig. S34). Although S@FeS2 
also exhibits an initial lithiation feature, it pro- 
vides little relief of the subsequent cathodic polar- 
ization, indicating limited catalytic promotion for 
sulfur reduction. This limitation can be explained 
by the intrinsic conversion-type degradation of 
FeS2 discussed above, including irreversible phase 
separation, particle cracking and loss of interfa- 
cial contact, all of which progressively diminish 
the effective catalytic interface. For S@TiS2 , the 
sulfur reduction peaks become increasingly polar- 
ized relative to S@AB, accompanied by continu- 
ously weakened anodic peaks upon cycling, indi- 
cating sluggish sulfur reduction and deteriorating 
Li2 S oxidation. These effects are likely driven by 
Ti migration and SSE degradation, which disrupt 
the catalytic interface and increase transport resis- 
tance. 
Sulfur K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge 
structure (XANES) spectra further provide direct 
chemical evidence for the efficient sulfur conver- 
sion enabled by the redox-activated catalysis in 
the S@HES cathode. As plotted in Fig. S35, while 
the fully discharged S@HES cathode displays a 
slightly stronger Li2 S-related peak intensity than 
the S@AB cathode, the sulfur-related signal in the 
S@HES cathode upon full charging intensifies sig- 
nificantly, with its peak center closely matching 
that of pristine sulfur. This spectral recovery ex- 
plicitly indicates the more complete Li2 S oxida- 
tion in the S@HES cathode, which is essential 
for achieving high-rate capability and stable cy- 
cling in ASSLSBs, as the oxidative decomposition 
of insulating Li2 S is widely regarded as the rate- 
limiting step [6 ]. The enhanced degree of Li2 S oxi- 
dation was further visually confirmed by top-view 
SEM imaging (Fig. 4 b), which exhibits a more uni- 
formly dispersed crystalline sulfur in the S@HES 
cathode after full charge. Consistent with these 
observations, the corresponding discharge–charge 
profiles reveal that the S@HES cell achieves over 
twice the sulfur utilization of the S@AB cell un- 
der a high areal loading of 4.5 mg cm−2 at 1C 
( Fig. S36). 
To complement the chemical and electrochem- 
ical analyses, we quantified the catalytic acceler- 
ation effect for sulfur redox kinetics via in situ 
EIS and galvanostatic intermittent titration tech- 
nique (GITT) measurements. Before cycling, the 
Nyquist plots for both cells show comparable 
ohmic resistance ( R), meaning similar carrier 
transport properties ( Fig. S37). However, the evo- 
lution of R differs significantly upon cycling 
(Fig. 4 c). The S@AB cell suffers from an up- 
ward trend in R, which is caused by the pro- 
gressive blockage of electronic and ionic transport 
pathways by the accumulation of insulating Li2 S.

resolution transmission electron microscopy and

a uniform size distribution ( < 10 nm), compara-

maintains a well-ordered state rather than becom-

crystalline lithiated phase, radial intensity pro-

tion data of fully lithiated HES align well with the 
simulated pattern of the lithiated pyrite FeS2 (i.e. 
the Li2 FeS2 phase) (Fig. 4 f). This strong crystallo- 
graphic correspondence implies that the lithiation 
mechanism of HES closely parallels the commonly 
accepted conversion of pyrite FeS2 to Li2 FeS2 , a 
metastable phase formed electrochemically and ac- 
companied by a symmetry transition from cubic 
to trigonal (space group: P¯3 m1 ) [40 ]. Ultimately, 
it is this robust, highly crystalline Li2 HES phase 
that provides the durable DCC network and active 
sites for sulfur conversion. Based on DFT calcula- 
tions, the potential structural evolution from HES 
to Li2 HES, followed by irreversible delithiation 
to Li0.22 HES, is schematically shown in Fig. S40, 
which includes the energetically stable Li1.0 HES 
intermediate phase. The structural robustness of 
Li2 HES is further supported by thermodynamic 
calculations, and the Gibbs free energy change for 
the Li2 HES decomposition reaction is calculated 
to be 53.39 kJ mol−1 ( Table S6), rendering such 
a pathway thermodynamically unfavorable. In ad- 
dition to structural stability, the DFT calculations 
reveal that the DOS of Lix HES ( x = 2, 1, 0.22) 
exhibits a high carrier density at the Fermi level 
( Fig. S41). This suggests that these lithiated phases 
retain high electrical conductivity, which under- 
pins the enhanced kinetics observed in in situ EIS 
and GITT. 
To gain a deeper insight into the redox ac- 
tivation within the HES lattice, XANES mea- 
surements were performed at the K-edges of all 
five constituent TMs (Fe, Co, Ni, Cu and Ru) 
to monitor their individual redox contributions 
during cycling. As illustrated in Fig. 4 g and Fig.
S42, the K-edges of Fe, Co, Ni and Cu shift si- 
multaneously to lower energies during discharge 
and largely recover upon charging, confirming 
their reversible redox activities. Among them, the 
Cu K-edge exhibits the most pronounced energy 
shifts ( ∼3 eV), consistent with partial reduction 
toward a Cu+ state, reflecting its role as the pri- 
mary redox-active center. Notably, the Cu K-edge 
also shows incomplete recovery upon charging, 
whereas the Fe K-edge shifts to a higher energy 
than in the pristine state, indicating an asym- 
metric valence redistribution among the TMs. In 
contrast, the Ru K-edge remains essentially un- 
changed throughout the cycle, indicating a highly 
stable oxidation state. To verify the stabilizing role 
of Ru, a Ru-free HES was synthesized as a con- 
trol. Unlike the Ru-containing system, this coun- 
terpart exhibits pronounced electrochemical in- 
stability ( Fig. S43a), including rapid voltage decay 
and a two-step oxidation profile resembling that 
of FeS2 ( Fig. S43b). Ex situ XRD reveals the for- 
mation of Li2 S after lithiation ( Fig. S43c), indi- 
cating that the absence of Ru drives the HES to- 
ward a conversion-like reaction pathway similar 
to that of the FeS2 cathode. To further understand 
the structural stabilization provided by Ru, bond 
strengths across various metal–sulfur pairs were 
quantified via minus Integrated Crystal Orbital 
Hamilton Populations. The results identify the 
Ru–S bond as the most robust bonding interaction 
within the lattice across all lithiation states ( Fig.
S44), which contributes to maintaining the struc- 
tural integrity of the HES during cycling. Conse- 
quently, these element-specific spectroscopic re- 
sults validate our compositional design of the HES, 
in which functionality is distributed across the 
multication lattice: Cu serves as the main electron 
reservoir, Co and Ni participate cooperatively in 
reversible redox reactions, Fe modulates the local 
electronic structure through coordination and co- 
valency evolution, and Ru functions as a redox- 
inactive structural stabilizer. 
To further elucidate the catalytic action of 
Lix HES in facilitating Li2 S oxidation, interfa- 
cial electron transfer between Lix HES and Li2 S 
was investigated using DFT calculations. Bader 
charge analysis in Fig. 4 h reveals that Li2 S do- 
nates more electrons to the highly delithiated 
Li0.22 HES (4.3e–) compared to the intermediate 
Li1.0 HES (3.9e–), implying that Li2 S undergoes 
spontaneous chemical oxidation driven by the 
Lix HES matrix. This process can be understood 
as Lix HES acting as a solid–solid interfacial re- 
dox mediator for Li2 S oxidation. Specifically, 
as illustrated in Fig. 4 i, the electronically insu- 
lating Li2 S cannot respond immediately to the 
applied potential during charging. Instead, the 
highly conductive Li2 HES network is activated 
first, experiencing electrochemical delithiation 
to generate ‘Li-deficient state’ intermediate states 
(Li2 HES- x e−→ Li2- x HES + x Li+ ). Driven by 
their strong chemical oxidizing capability, these 
newly formed intermediate Li2- x HES phases 
Page 11 of 14

=== 第 12 页 ===
Natl Sci Rev, 2026, Vol. 13, nwag501
spontaneously extract electrons from the adjacent 
Li2 S (Li2- x HES + y Li2 S→ Li2- x + 2 y HES + y S) 
[20 ]. Consequently, the strong Li–S bonds in 
Li2 S are substantially weakened, triggering Li2 S 
decomposition and releasing Li+ ions into the 
DCC Lix HES network. Importantly, this process 
is self-accelerating: as the charging proceeds, 
continuous lithium removal yields progressively 
more Li-deficient states (approaching Li0.22 HES). 
As corroborated by the DFT results, these deeply 
delithiated phases possess a stronger thermody- 
namic driving force for electron extraction from 
Li2 S, thereby markedly accelerating Li2 S oxida- 
tion kinetics. To experimentally corroborate this 
spontaneous charge transfer, a physical mixture of 
Li2 S and pristine HES (representing the extreme 
Li-deficient state, x = 0) was examined using 
7 Li ssNMR. The spectrum exhibits a distinct 
chemical shift and broadening relative to pure 
Li2 S (Fig. 4 j), reflecting highly heterogeneous 
local Li environments. This observation provides 
direct evidence of the Li2 S oxidation chemically 
driven by HES. Collectively, both theoretical and 
experimental insights establish that the Lix HES 
matrix is not merely as a passive DCC, but actively 
functions as a highly efficient solid-state redox 
mediator. This dynamic chemical–electrochemical 
coupling fundamentally overcomes the kinetic 
barriers of insulating Li2 S oxidation, ensuring 
highly reversible and rapid sulfur redox reactions. 
CONCLUSION 
In summary, this work reveals two failure mecha- 
nisms of conventional TMS catalysts in ASSLSBs, 
including phase separation and TM migration, 
and introduces a high-entropy strategy to over- 
come these intrinsic limitations. Compared with 
the conventional counterparts, the rationally de- 
signed pyrite-type HES utilizes entropy stabi- 
lization to form a highly stable Lix HES solid- 
solution phase during cycling, thereby avoid- 
ing irreversible catalyst self-poisoning and elec- 
trolyte degradation. This robust, all-in-one cat- 
alytic platform overcomes the spatial limita- 
tions of traditional TPBs by providing expan- 
sive dual ion-electron pathways, while thermody- 
namically weakening Li–S bonds to drive spon- 
taneous Li2 S oxidation. By translating these fa- 
vorable microscale interfacial kinetics into macro- 
scopic electrode performance, we effectively miti- 
gate the persistent challenge of low sulfur utiliza- 
tion in ASSLSBs with high-sulfur-content cath- 
odes. Specifically, with an ultra-low HES addi- 
tion of only 3 wt%, the obtained S@HES cathode 
(40 wt% S) achieves exceptional sulfur utilization 
of 93.4% and a conversion efficiency of 93.0%. 
It also sustains superior high-rate cycling perfor- 
mance (900 mAh g−1 at 2C over 250 cycles) even 
with an elevated sulfur fraction (50 wt%). Notably, 
the outstanding catalytic efficacy is preserved un- 
der demanding constraints (8.5 mg cm−2 , 30°C), 
yielding a remarkable areal capacity of 9.68 mAh 
cm−2 . The practical viability of HES is further 
demonstrated in a S@HES|LPSC|Li3.75 Si full cell, 
which delivers a highly competitive gravimetric 
energy density of 571 Wh kg−1 . This study offers 
profound mechanistic insights into solid-state cat- 
alytic failures and underscores the significant po- 
tential of the high-entropy strategy for advanced 
solid-state Li–S catalysis. 
METHODS 
Synthesis of HES 
HES was synthesized using the high-pressure and 
high-temperature Cubic Press (GY420, Guilin, 
China) at HPSTAR. All chemicals, including iron, 
cobalt, nickel, copper, ruthenium nanopowder 
and sulfur powder, were sourced from Alfa Aesar 
and used without additional purification. First, the 
raw materials were weighed in an argon glovebox 
in the desired stoichiometric ratio, and then ball- 
milled using a high-energy mechanical mill (SPEX 
8000D). Subsequently, the homogenized powders 
were prepressed at 20 MPa into a cylinder, fol- 
lowed by encapsulation within a hexagonal boron 
nitride (h-BN) capsule. Then, the assembled block 
was sintered at 1400°C under 5 GPa for 40 min. 
Preparation of composite cathodes 
HES nanopowders were obtained by further refin- 
ing the sintered samples using high-energy me- 
chanical milling. The milling was performed at a 
rotation speed of 1725 rad min−1 for 10 h under 
an argon atmosphere. 
Sulfur-based composite cathodes were pre- 
pared via a two-step ball-milling process using 
a planetary micro mill (Fritsch Pulverisette 7). 
Sulfur and AB (TIMCAL Graphite & Carbon) 
were dried prior to use and transferred into 
an argon-filled glove box (MIKROUNA, O2 and 
H2 O < 0.01 ppm). In the first step, sulfur and AB 
were mixed with mass ratios of S:AB = 3:2, 2:1 
and 5:2, which were designed to yield final sul- 
fur contents of 30 wt%, 40 wt% and 50 wt%, re- 
spectively, in the composite cathodes. The mix- 
tures were loaded into a ZrO2 milling jar with 
ZrO2 balls and sealed under an inert argon at- 
mosphere. Ball milling was carried out at 500 rad 
min−1 for 10 min, followed by a 5-min rest period 
Page 12 of 14

=== 第 13 页 ===
Natl Sci Rev, 2026, Vol. 13, nwag501
for cooling; this sequence was repeated for a total 
of 48 cycles. In the second step, the as-prepared 
AB/S mixtures were combined with LPS (Hefei 
Kejing Materials Technology Co., Ltd.) solid elec- 
trolyte at mass ratios of 1:1, 3:2 and 7:3 to ob- 
tain composite cathodes with sulfur contents of 
30 wt%, 40 wt% and 50 wt%, respectively. The 
mixtures were further milled at 400 rad min−1 for 
36 cycles to ensure homogeneous dispersion. For 
the preparation of S@HES composite cathodes, a 
modification was applied only in the initial ball- 
milling step, where 15 wt% of AB was replaced 
by HES, while all other processing parameters re- 
mained identical. This substitution resulted in an 
HES content of ∼3 wt% in the final composite 
cathodes. 
For composite cathodes based on HES, TMS2 
and TMS, a similar preparation procedure was 
adopted. Here, TMS2 denotes metal disulfides 
(TM = Ti or Fe), and TMS represents metal 
monosulfides (TM = Fe, Co, Ni or Cu). The metal 
sulfide powder (active material), LPS and vapor- 
grown carbon fiber (Shenzhen Kejing Star Tech- 
nology Co., Ltd.) were mixed at a weight ratio 
of 31:62:7, respectively, and loaded into a ZrO2 
milling jar. Mechanical milling was conducted at 
150 rad min−1 for 1 h.
