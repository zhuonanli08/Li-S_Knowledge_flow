a b s t r a c t
With the emergence of some solid electrolytes (SSEs) with high ionic conductivity being comparable to
liquid electrolytes, solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries (SSLSBs) have been widely regarded as one of
the most promising candidates for the next generation of power generation energy storage batteries,
and have been extensively researched. Though many fundamental and technological issues still need
to be resolved to develop commercially viable technologies, SSLSBs using SSEs are expected to address
the present limitations and achieve high energy and power density while improving safety, which is very
attractive to large-scale energy storage systems. SSLSBs have been developed for many years. However,
there are few systematic discussions related to the working mechanism of action of various electrolytes
in SSLSBs and the defects and the corresponding solutions of various electrolytes. To ﬁll this gap, it is very
meaningful to review the recent progress of SSEs in SSLSBs. In this review, we comprehensively investi-
gate and summarize the application of SSEs in LSBs to determine the differences which still exist between
current progresses and real-world requirements, and comprehensively describe the mechanism of action
of SSLSBs, including lithium-ion transport, interfacial contact, and catalytic conversion mechanisms.
More importantly, the selection of solid electrolyte materials and the novel design of structures are
reviewed and the properties of various SSEs are elucidated. Finally, the prospects and possible future
research directions of SSLSBs including designing high electronic/ionic conductivity for cathodes, opti-
mizing electrolytes and developing novel electrolytes with excellent properties, improving electrode/-
electrolyte interface stability and enhancing interfacial dynamics between electrolyte and anode, using
more advanced test equipment and characterization techniques to analyze conduction mechanism of
Li+ in SSEs are presented. It is hoped that this review can arouse people’s attention and enlighten the
development of functional materials and novel structures of SSEs in the next step.

1. Introduction

demand for energy has been growing, and accessing to clean,
affordable and reliable energy has been the cornerstone of the
world’s growing prosperity and economic growth [1]. The adoption
and development of cleaner, more affordable and more reliable

electrochemical energy storage systems providing electricity on
demand is the core and key of today’s mobile electronic devices
and electric vehicles, and also plays an important role in the ﬁeld
of energy storage [5,6].

2.1. Mechanism of lithium ion transport
Depending on SEs materials, the mechanism of lithium ion
transport and conductivity of the composed SSLSBs can vary
signiﬁcantly. At present, their mechanisms are mainly elaborated
from two aspects: polymer electrolyte and inorganic electrolyte.
2.1.1. Li-ion transport in polymer electrolytes
Most SPEs have low ionic conductivity (10-7  10-5 S/cm) and
poor lithium ion mobility number (tLi
+, 0.2–0.4) at room tempera-
ture, which are difﬁcult to meet the practical use requirements.
This is because the low dielectric constant of the polymer matrix
cannot promote the dissociation of a large number of free ions
from the lithium salts. At the same time, the transport of Li+ along
the polymer molecular chain is disordered at the leap site, which
does not allow efﬁcient ion transport to occur during the charge–
discharge processes [59]. In the past few decades, many studies
have been carried out on the ionic conduction mechanism of solid
polymer electrolytes. Generally speaking, lithium ion transport
arises from three primary contributions [60]: (a) Intrachain lithium
ion motion along the polymer backbone, (b) Cooperative motion of
lithium ions coordinated with the polymer segments such as PEO,
and (c) Intersegmental hopping of lithium ions from one chain to
the other.
Ion transportion in polymer electrolytes mainly depends on the
number of freely mobile ions. The number of freely migrating
lithium ions can increase the ionic conductivity of the electrolyte
system. Meanwhile, the more charges carried by individual ions
can further improve the ionic conductivity. The number of freely
migrating ions mainly depends on the degree of dissociation of
lithium salts in polymer electrolytes. To carry out fast ion transfer,
polymer electrolyte must ﬁrst contain a Lewis base (usually ether
oxygen), which is used to coordinate the cations, thus facilitating
the dissolution of the salt. For example, the oxygen atom on PEO
has two lone pair electrons, which have strong coordination ability
and can interact with lithium to form a complex based on Lewis
acid-base theory. The chain segments of the PEO polymer are con-
stantly moving, leading to the continuous complexation and disso-
ciation of Li+. Lithium ions migrate from one coordination site to
another new coordination site along the polymer segment, or jump
from one segment to another, thereby realizing the migration of
lithium ions. The directional motion is realized under the inﬂuence
of the external electric ﬁeld, which is called the ion conduction
process [61–63].
The ion transport in the polymer electrolyte is also affected by
the chain segment movement of the amorphous region. In solid

polymer electrolytes, ion conduction usually occurs only in the
amorphous region of the polymer. Therefore, lithium ion transport
can be promoted by appropriately increasing the operating tem-
perature of the polymer electrolyte. In particular, when the tem-
perature is higher than the glass transition temperature (Tg),
more amorphous regions in the electrolyte are more favorable for
the movement of polymer chain segments, thus improving ion
conductivity of polymers [64]. In addition, by polymer blending,
co-polymerization, grafting and cross-linking, etc., the amorphous
region also can be increased, and the advantages of various poly-
mers can be combined to improve the overall performances. Add-
ing liquid plasticizers, such as ionic liquids, electrolytes, etc., to
polymer system can also effectively improve its ionic conductivity
and enhance the contact properties of the interface [65].
In addition to the most common mechanism of conduction in
the amorphous region, some scholars have proposed the theory
of Li+ conduction in the crystalline region of PEO for some polymer
electrolytes. The results of Bruce’s group concluded that in the
crystalline phase of P(EO)6-LiX (X = PF6, AsF6, SbF6) electrolyte sys-
tem [66], each of PEO chain folds to form a semi-cylinder with
pairs of such chains interlocking to form cylindrical tunnels within
which the Li+ ions reside. Inside the channel, lithium ions also can
couple with the O atoms on the ether oxygen chains, while the
anions are outside the channels and do not interact with lithium
ions. Lithium ions can migrate along cylindrical channels without
resorting to segment movement. In detail, the Li+ ion resides in a
site formed by ﬁve ether oxygens, three from one PEO chain and
two from the other. In order for a Li+ ion to migrate to another
stable ﬁve-coordinate site, it has to pass between two ether oxy-
gens, thus entering an intermediate site formed by four ether oxy-
gens deﬁning a rectangle. The Li+ ion then passes through another
bottleneck formed by two ether oxygens in order to enter the next
ﬁve-coordinate site. In the perfect crystal structure, all the ﬁve-
coordinate Li+ sites would be full. However, no crystalline solid is
perfect above 0 K, therefore, there must be some defects that lead
to vacant Li+ sites to which adjacent Li+ ions may migrate.
It has also been suggested that the low ionic conductivity and
short lifetime of SPE limit the application of ambient temperature
polymeric lithium metal batteries, perhaps as a result of the inher-
ent restricted segment movement of the polymer at room temper-
ature. For example, Wang et al. [67] developed an ambient
temperature bilayer solid polymer electrolyte and innovatively
investigated the relevant working mechanisms. In this strategy,
the polypropylene carbonate (PPC)/succinonitrile (SN) was con-
tacted with the cathode, while polyethylene oxide (PEO)/Li7La3Zr2-
O12 was employed in the vicinity of the anode. The molecular
dynamics simulations demonstrated the formation of dissolved
sheath-like structures [SN---Li+], which showed strong interactions
with the polymer (PPC--[SN---Li+]/PEO--[SN---Li+]). The combined
FTIR/NMR and DFT results also showed that the excellent Li-ion
conduction was mainly attributed to the generation of the
polymer--[SN---Li+] system, and thus SPE can operate at room tem-
perature by providing a rich Li-ion transport path through the
polymer segment.
In general, the conductivity mechanism of solid polymer elec-
trolytes needs to be investigated more deeply and systematically
based on more theoretical calculations and in-situ characterization
and testing.

2.1.2. Li-ion transport in inorganic solid electrolytes
Li+ transport dynamics are critical for the stability and durabil-
ity of various solid-state lithium-based batteries [68]. In SEs, since
the anions are bound to the SE structure, Li+ conduct through the
SE, which results in a maximum mobility number of 1 for lithium
ions. This contrasts with liquid electrolytes where both cations and
anions move and contribute to ionic conductivity [69]. Some inor-
ganic SE materials can achieve high ionic conductivity at room
temperature, and the representative materials are Li10GeP2S12(-
LGPS), Li7La3Zr2O12(LLZO) and Li1+XAlXTi2-X (PO4)3(LATP).
Targeting the transport of lithium ions in inorganic SEs, Mo et al.
[70] proposed the concept of concerted transport of lithium ions in
solid-state lithium-based batteries. That is, the concerted motion
of multiple ions stimulates the rapid transport of ions and reduces
the energy barrier of ion migration. In all multiple ion synchrotron
leap, the individual ions are at different energy positions, some at
high energy positions and some at low energy positions. When sev-
eral adjacent ions move together, the downward motion of the ions
at the high-energy position can partially offset the energy barrier
felt at the upward motion of the low-energy position. With the
joint motion of several ions, the concerted leap also will then exhi-
bit a lower energy barrier [70]. In addition, the size of the ion dif-
fusion channel is closely related to the ion transport performance.
For example, in the LISICON-like Li3.5M0.5M’0.5O4, with the increase
of lithium-ion diffusion channels, the activation energy of ion
migration
decreases.
Finally,
the
lithium-ion
conductivity
increases. Meanwhile, the anion lattice arrangement also can be
found to have a great inﬂuence on ion transport, and among the
many lattice arrangements, the body-centered cubic ionic skeleton
is the most desirable structure for obtaining high ionic conductiv-
ity. For example, fast ionic conductors Li10GeP2S12 and Li7P3S11
possess the structural characteristic of body-centered cubic ionic
skeleton, thus exhibiting very high ionic conductivity.
Lattice dynamics also have a large impact on ion transport. In
the solid crystal structure, the atoms are highly stacked and there
is the bonding between them. The transport of ions in the crystal
lattice is usually induced by thermal disturbance, that is, to
increase the vibration of ions at the equilibrium position and make
them move from the equilibrium position to other positions [71–
73]. Yang et al. [74] proposed that the low vibration frequency of
lithium ions was correlated with the enhanced mobility of lithium
ions in some SEs such as LISICON and Olivine families by combin-
ing inelastic neutron scattering measurements with density func-
tional theory based on lattice dynamics theory. It is proposed
that the vibrational frequency of lithium ions in crystals has a
strong correlation with ion transport and plays a key role in con-
trolling lithium ion conductivity and stability of lithium ion
conductors.
In summary, concerted transport, lattice arrangement and lat-
tice dynamics effectively affect the ionic conductivity and Li+ trans-
fer number of ISE. However, the key physicochemical issues
involved above can be very complex. It is suggested that ion con-
certed transport can have lower activation energy and is an emerg-
ing promising solution for enhancing ion transport. However, there
is a lack of research and evidence on the interactions between ions
and the factors inﬂuencing them, such as determining ion hopping
on spatial and temporal scales. Therefore, there is an urgent need
to further investigate the transport mechanisms of lithium ions
in ISEs, which will facilitate the design of new electrolytes to fur-
ther improve ionic conductivity.
2.1.3. Li-ion transport in composite electrolytes
Organic-inorganic hybrid composite electrolytes are formed
from functional polymers and inorganic ﬁllers proportional to the
polymer system [49,50]. Due to the synergistic effect of polymers
and inorganic ﬁllers, organic–inorganic composite electrolytes
can take advantage of their respective components to promote
transport of lithium ions. Inorganic ﬁllers can enhance the contact
with the polymer matrix and shorten the lithium ion diffusion
pathway due to their higher surface area, more interfaces between
organic and inorganic and more favorable lithium ion transmis-
sion. In addition, the addition of functional ﬁllers also can provide
a support matrix which can greatly improve the mechanical
strength and dimensional stability of the polymer allowing the
composite electrolyte to maintain its overall solid structure even
at high temperatures. The addition of inorganic ﬁllers also can sig-
niﬁcantly improve the ionic conductivity of the electrolyte, so that
some researchers have conducted extensive research on the theo-
retical mechanism of the enhancement of ionic conductivity by
introducing some inorganic ﬁllers in composite electrolytes.
Numerous studies have found that the functional groups of
inorganic ﬁllers play a critical role in promoting the diffusion of
lithium ions. The abundant functional groups of inorganic ﬁllers
(-F, –OH, -Cl, etc.) can adsorb the anions dissociated from lithium
salts through Lewis acid-base interaction, which can weaken the
interaction of Li ions with these polymers, and increase the num-
ber of free lithium ions by promoting the dissociation of lithium
salts, thereby greatly promoting the Li+ transportation at inorganic
ﬁller/polymer matrix interfaces [75]; Liu et al. [76] believed that
surface functional groups of inorganic ﬁllers also could change
the local structural environment of polymer electrolyte and hinder
the local recombination of polymer segments, thus reducing the
polymer crystallinity and increasing the concentration of free Li+.
It has also been found that the ionic conductivity of composite
electrolytes is associated with the space charge region. Li et al. [77]
used the PEO: Ga-LLZO composite as a conduction model system
which was applicable to other composite polymer electrolytes as
well. They believed that the enhancement of the ionic conductivity
of PEO: Ga-LLZO composite polymer electrolyte was closely related
to the space charge region formed at the interface between the PEO
matrix and the Ga-LLZO nanoparticles. They simulated the lithium-
ion transport in the composite polymer electrolyte by the Monte
Carlo simulation, demonstrating that the enhanced ionic conduc-
tivity can be ascribed to the ionic conduction in the space charge
regions and the percolation of the space charge regions. When
the space charge regions and the phase distribution meet the
demand of forming the percolation threshold, the percolation
effect takes effect, and continuous fast conduction pathways are
formed.
Under
these
circumstances,
the
ionic
conductivity
increased signiﬁcantly.
It is worth noting that the organic–inorganic interface is also an
important component of the complex electrolyte. The interface in
the complex also has a role in ion conduction as an essential path-
way for Li-ion fast transport conduction. High-quality organic–
inorganic interfaces in composite electrolytes can effectively
improve the ion conductivity of the whole system, even to the level
of mS cm1. However, conventional polymer solid electrolyte com-
posite ﬁllers are usually randomly dispersed in the solid composite
electrolyte, and the interface is often disordered and discontinu-
ous, making it difﬁcult to form a continuous ion transport path.
Therefore, such interfaces do not provide satisfactory ion conduc-
tivity [78]. In general, it is necessary to give them more research
in this area.
In conclusion, over the past years, researchers have extensively
studied the transport mechanism of Li+ and proposed different the-

carry out ion transport in polymer SEs, it relies on the ability of
polar groups to coordinate with the Li+, which in turn dissolves
the lithium salt, generating free-moving ions as well as chain seg-
ments to move. In inorganic SEs, concerted transport and lattice
dynamics theories have been proposed by some scholars. However,

2.2. Interface problems of SSLSBs
Unlike liquid batteries, the interface contact problem is one of
the biggest challenges for solid-state batteries. In SSLSBs, solid-
state electrolytes need to be in close contact with the electrodes
to enable the transport of lithium ions between the prepared elec-
trolyte and the electrodes. Polymer electrolytes, due to its ﬂexibil-
ity and adhesion, can form a uniform and close contact interface
with active substance of electrodes and electronic conductors, thus
reducing the interface impedance to a certain degree between elec-
trolytes and electrodes [49,79]. Non-ﬂowable inorganic solid cera-
mic or glass electrolytes are rigid and difﬁcult to make close
contact with electrodes, which may greatly affect Li deposition
and
dissolution
behavior.
Therefore,
the
interface

between SEs and battery electrodes including anode/solid elec-

electrolyte is particularly signiﬁcant in inorganic SSLSBs [80].
2.2.1. Anode /solid electrolyte interface
Lithium metal has a high speciﬁc capacity (3860 mA h/g) and a
low redox potential (-3.04 V vs. H+/H2) and is considered as an
ideal anode material for high speciﬁc energy lithium-based batter-
ies. In SSLSBs, due to the high reactivity and thermodynamic insta-
bility
of
lithium
metal,
interface
reaction
will
occur
after

tinuous loss of battery active materials. At the anode interface, the
reactivity between the ceramic electrolyte and the lithium metal
anode is high, which tends to produce an unstable intermediate
layer that can act as a barrier to a further reaction between elec-
trolytes and metal lithium. This reaction is more obvious for elec-
trolytes containing high-valent elements, such as the high-valence
metal cation in the lattice of Li10GeP2S12(LGPS) is easy to be
restored by lithium metal, which often leads to the occurrence of
interfacial side reactions, high interfacial resistance and serious
loss of active materials. The poor wettability between metal Li
and solid-state composite electrolyte (SCE) usually leads to poor
interfacial contact and thus high local current density, and lithium
dendrites are also more likely to be generated at such locations
with relatively high current density, which the theoretical view
has received general consensus from some researchers. Such
lithium dendrites can penetrate the SE and reduce the safety of
the battery, leading to ﬁre or explosion. In addition, this is often
inhomogeneous due to the solid–solid contact between metal
lithium and SE. The poor contact leads to increased interfacial
resistance, irregular lithium ﬂux distribution, and a signiﬁcant
reduction in the critical current density of the SCE [81–83].
In recent years, the effect of stack pressure on the lithium metal
anode in ASSBs are receiving more and more attention. Masias et al.
measured the Young’s modulus, the shear modulus, and the Pois-
son’s ratio of lithium metal at room temperature. Their ﬁndings
show that Li exhibits a yield strength of about 0.8 MPa. When yield
strength is exceeding this value, the metal start to creep [84]. This
has been conﬁrmed by LePage et al., who showed that at room tem-
perature, the yield strength of Li metal is creep-dominated. This
yield strength needs to be correlated with the stack pressure
applied on ASSBs during cycling to fully understand the mechani-
cal behavior of a lithium metal anode [85].
High stack pressures can induce uniform interfacial contact
between Li and the SSE, but they can also mechanically deform
and force Li through micropores within the SSE pellet to cause
short circuits. If the stack pressure is too low, interfacial contact
is not sufﬁcient, leading to interfacial void formation during the
Li stripping of the battery. The stack pressure can also vary with
continuous cycle due to changes in the volume of active materials,
meaning that the actual stack pressure inside the cell can be differ-
ent than that which is initially applied. Interphase formation and
morphology evolution at the Li/SSE interface also can affect the
stack pressure, but such phenomena are not yet well understood.
Lee et al. found that the evolution of stack pressure was highly
dependent on the chemical stability of the SSE in contact with
metal Li, and the interphase formation resulted in a decrease of
stack pressure during operation. The Li ﬁlament growth is shown
to exhibit distinct stack pressure signatures that depend on SSE
processing condition and density. This work will provide a new
understanding of the interfacial evolution of SSE and also can
demonstrate the value of electro-chemo-mechanical measure-
ments for advancing our understanding of SSLSBs [86].
In conclusion, various evidences show that the interface prob-
lem of anode/solid electrolyte is an important factor leading to
the growth of lithium dendrites. This is certainly a great hindrance
to the development and application of SSLSBs. The simple use of
SEs does not effectively inhibit the growth of lithium dendrites.
In order to improve the electrochemical performance of solid-
state lithium-based batteries, strategies such as solid electrolyte
interface optimization (such as adding additives to build mechan-
ically and chemically stable SEI in situ), modiﬁcation of the elec-
trolyte surface (such as surface coating strategies), and structural
design (such as obtaining a vertically heterogeneous electrolyte
by varying the planar laminar electrolyte in the vertical direction)
have often been used to improve the interface problem between
the anode and electrolyte. However, the interface optimization
methods and the composition design and structure designs of the
buffer layer still require a lot of effort to explore [87].
2.2.2. Internal interface of the solid electrolyte
The original idea of solid-state electrolyte is to use its high mod-
ulus strength to effectively inhibit lithium dendrite growth.
Although solid-state electrolyte avoids lithium dendrite growth
to some extent, however, lithium dendrite growth still occurs espe-
cially under the conditions of high current density. The mechanism
of lithium dendrite growth in solid-state electrolytes has been
widely explored and generalized by some researchers. Jeff Saka-
moto et al. proposed a model for the growth of lithium dendrites
at grain boundaries. There are abundant grain boundaries in inor-
ganic SEs. In solid-state batteries, some network-like lithium den-
drites

the

and
grow
along

causes more severe interfacial reactions, but also causes safety
accidents and even serious explosion problems due to short cir-
cuits [25,80]. The internal interface of the SE has a signiﬁcant
impact on the conductivity of the SE. Some unreasonable defects
and impurities at the grain boundaries can signiﬁcantly reduce
the ion transport rate, resulting in a decrease in the overall ionic
conductivity of the electrolyte [88]. Therefore, the ionic conductiv-
ity of SEs will be greatly enhanced by reducing the number of grain
boundaries, modifying and optimizing them rationally and increas-
ing the density in inorganic electrolytes, etc.
Inevitable defects and impurities in the electrolyte also provide
nucleation sites for lithium dendrite growth, which serve as ‘‘roots”
for dendrite growth and lead to the growth of lithium dendrites.

decrease in the overall ionic conductivity of the electrolyte [89].
Strategies to optimize the physical and chemical properties of
SCEs to achieve dense SCEs are rather important to avoid dendrite
formation. Lithium dendrites often grow together with inhomoge-
neous defects including voids, cracks, grain boundaries, impurity
precipitates, etc. Most of SCEs have undergone a tableting process
from SCE powders into thin ﬁlms, which inevitably introduces
defects. Single crystal SCE can fundamentally eliminate the effects
of grain boundaries and voids, while polycrystalline SCE avoids
dendrite formation by reducing the defect density. Removal of
impurities with poor ionic conductivity in SCE is another strategy
to avoid lithium dendrite growth. The widely studied garnet elec-
trolyte is easily contaminated by moisture to form a Li2CO3 surface
layer of lithium ion insulation, which leads to huge interfacial
resistance and non-uniform Li deposition, and the most commonly
used strategy is high temperature annealing in inert gas. Some SEs
with high mechanical strength usually are considered as a poten-
tial solution to lithium dendrite problem, which can fundamentally
change the Li deposition behavior [12]. According to the widely
cited Monroe and Newman model, lithium dendrite growth can
be successfully suppressed if the shear modulus of SE is 2 times lar-
ger than that of Li [80].
Introducing an interfacial buffer layer to keep the interface
stable is considered to be an effective way to avoid lithium den-
drite formation. For example, thionyl chloride (SOCl2) was used
as an electrolyte additive to lower the diffusion barrier and build
a stable interfacial protective layer to prevent lithium dendrite
growth [90]. In addition, the uniform porous structure also can
inhibit the formation of lithium dendrites. Xu et al. designed a
three-layer garnet matrix with a porous structure to stabilize Li.
The prepared frame has excellent wettability and lithium pene-
trates into the porous garnet framework, enabling seamless con-
tact. The 3D porous structure achieves a high contact area, which
reduces the local current density when surface current is applied,
and as a result effectively suppresses the growth of lithium den-
drites [91].
Recent studies also have shown that in addition to interfacial
wettability, electronic conductivity is also one of the signiﬁcant
factors affecting lithium dendrite growth, and the higher the elec-
tronic conductivity, the faster the growth rate of lithium dendrites.
Han et al.[92] illustrated the origin of the lithium dendrite forma-
tion by monitoring the dynamic evolution of Li concentration pro-
ﬁles in three popular but representative SEs (LiPON, LLZO and
amorphous Li3PS4) during lithium plating using time-resolved
operando neutron depth proﬁling. The high electronic conductivity
of LLZO and Li3PS4 was the main reason for lithium dendrite
formation in these SEs. Therefore, reducing the electronic conduc-
tivity, rather than further increasing the ionic conductivity of SEs,
is critical for enhancing the electrochemical performance of the
SSLSBs. Therefore, it is particularly necessary to construct an
interfacial layer with excellent wettability, low electronic conduc-
tivity and superior mechanical strength to inhibit the growth of
lithium dendrites, which can effectively suppress lithium dendrites
[93].
2.2.3. Cathode /solid electrolyte interface
During repeated charge/discharge processes, lithium ions are
continuously inserted/detached and an 80% volume change occurs
between sulfur and Li2S, resulting in a tendency for the active
materials to detach from the lithium ion and electron conductors,
causing a large interfacial resistance and rapid capacity decay.
The huge volume change may disconnect the lithium ion and elec-
tron transport channels, resulting in a limited lithium ion transport
rate at the SE/anode interface. The charge transport in solid-state
batteries is entirely dependent on the solid/solid interfaces
between the SEs and active materials. The inhomogeneous solid–
solid physical contact at the interface combined with the insulating
properties of sulfur and other factors lead to the instability of the
interfaces between electrode and electrolyte [25,27,28,88,94].
Therefore, solving the volume change during charging and dis-
charging processes, increasing the interfacial contact area and
improving the interfacial stability are rather keys to achieving
the excellent electrochemical performance of SSLSBs.
The interface design of the sulfur cathode side is also very
important for SSLSBs. Although sulﬁde SEs still have the drawbacks
of poor electrochemical stability and weak air stability to be over-
come, the sulﬁde electrolyte itself exhibits very high ionic conduc-
tivity and has a good interfacial contact with cell cathode due to its
soft texture, making the sulﬁde electrolyte has been extensively
studied. The cathode of SSLSBs based on sulﬁde electrolyte is gen-
erally prepared by isostatic pressure method. A certain proportion
of sulfur, conductive agent, and sulﬁde electrolyte are mixed well
by ball milling and then compounded by isostatic pressing to
obtain an all-solid-state battery with good ionic conductivity and
excellent interfacial compatibility [95,96]. Many studies have been
conducted to construct micro-nanostructures between the anode
and the interface from a nanosized perspective to improve the uti-
lization of active materials. For example, Nagao et al. [97] reduced
the size of Li2S particles by the high-energy ball milling, and then
compounded with other components to obtain the anode. The high
speciﬁc surface area of the nanostructure effectively improved the
contact between the electrode and the electrolyte, and the results
showed that the ball-milled active material was uniformly dis-
tributed in the cathode materials and could provide more active
sites, which effectively improves the Coulombic efﬁciency and
cycling stability of the battery. However, oxide electrolytes are
generally hard and do not easily improve the interfacial contact
by methods such as cold isostatic pressing, so the interfacial prob-
lems are more prominent in batteries using oxide SEs [98].
For SSLSBs using polymers as electrolytes, it is urgent to reduce
the solubility of the polymer electrolyte to LiPSs to avoid their
shuttle effects. In general, polymers tend to require higher operat-
ing temperatures to improve the energy conversion efﬁciency and
electrical energy of the battery. Melting of polymers into liquid
state occurs during battery use, which can lead to much improved
interfacial contact, but will inevitably dissolve the LiPSs and lead to
the shuttle effect [99]. Combining inorganic SEs with organic poly-
mers can improve the interfacial problems very well. The introduc-
tion of organic polymer makes the contact between electrolyte and
electrode tighter and more uniform, and the inorganic SE also has
higher ionic conductivity and more excellent mechanical strength.
In conclusion, interface problems are a major challenge for
SSLSBs. When inorganic solid ceramic or glass is used as elec-
trolytes for SSLSBs, the solid–solid contact makes the Li+ transport
at the interface between cathodes/SSEs and anodes/SSEs will be
greatly limited [93]. The physical properties of the electrolyte itself
(grain boundaries, density, impurities) also greatly affect the trans-
port of Li+. Therefore, more attention should be paid to three
aspects in the design of SSLSBs: Firstly, reserving enough space
for the volume changes of the cathode and ensuring tight contact
of the particles inside the cathode to reduce the solid phase inter-
face impedance. Secondly, improving interface compatibility by
modifying the interface between lithium metal and SE or designing
a reasonable buffer layer is a hot topic. Finally, optimizing the qual-
ity of the electrolyte itself, reducing the number of grain bound-
aries and impurities, and increasing its own density and strength
is also a major step to improving the interface problem.

3. Selection of solid electrolyte materials
3.1. Polymer electrolytes
Polymer electrolytes are generally based on various polymers,
and lithium salts are dissolved in the matrix to achieve high ionic
conduction of the electrolyte. Solid polymer electrolytes have been
recognized as promising candidates for obtaining safe and excel-
lent electrochemical performance lithium batteries due to the good
ionic conductivity, interfacial compatibility, low ﬂammability,
excellent
mechanical
properties,
and
outstanding
ﬂexibility
[8,11,30,49,61,93,100]. Not only the solid polymer electrolyte
potentially hinders the diffusion of LiPSs between cathode and
anode, but also the polymer with sufﬁciently high modulus can
prevent the formation of lithium dendrites. In the LSB system, elec-
trode materials with high sulfur surface loading are prone to large
volume changes during charging and discharging processes, and
the polymeric SE can also mitigate the volume changes of the
active material and thus prevent electrode structure fragmenta-
tion. Based on the above advantages, the safety and cycling perfor-
mance of the battery can be signiﬁcantly improved [49,61,101–
104]. In recent years, various polymer electrolytes have been
explored and applied in SSLSBs, such as polyethylene oxide(PEO),
polyacrylonitrile(PAN), polypropylene carbonate(PPC), polyimide
(PI) SEs, etc. [101,105]. Next, we will explain and illustrate them
in detail.
3.1.1. PEO
PEO is the main polymer host for an alkali metal salt introduced
as a solid polymer electrolyte and is currently the most widely
studied framework materials in polymer electrolytes, which is
rather promising application prospects [49,106,107]. PEO has a
molecular structure similar to that of polyether liquid electrolyte,
and the ﬂexible polyether segments can be complex with alkali
metal ions (Li+). Under the action of the electric ﬁeld, along with
the thermal movement of molecules, Li+ continuously undergoes
a process of ‘‘coordination-dissociation” with ether oxygen atoms,
and directional and rapid migration of Li+ can be realized through
local relaxation and PEO segment movement [49].The macro-
molecular structure of PEO also has good ﬂexibility and can form
good contact with cell electrodes. In addition, it has the advantages
of low cost, good mechanical stability, excellent electrochemical
stability and serious corrosion resistance [47,50]. However, the
room temperature(RT) ionic conductivity of PEO is low, only for
10-8 to 10-6 S cm1. In addition, the defects of PEO, such as high
crystallinity, poor thermal dimensional stability and mechanical
strength, and narrow operating temperature range, also hinder
its stand-alone application in SSLSBs [108,109].
It has been shown that reducing the crystalline region of PEO is
the key to improving ion transport, so the design principle of PEO
based polymer electrolytes is generally to increase the percentage
of amorphous phase by suppressing crystallization as a way to
improve the ionic conductivity of PEO based polymer electrolytes
at RT and to endow their good mechanical properties [110]. SPE
usually consists of polymer host as solid matrix with various
lithium salts. The addition of lithium salts can reduce the glass
transition temperature and crystallinity of the polymer matrix to
a certain extent, and greatly improve the mechanical strength of
the polymer and the stable temperature of the amorphous phase
(>150 C) [111]. Structure and concentration are the two main fac-
tors that affect the conductivity of the system. The application of
various lithium salts of different structures and concentrations
compounded with PEO based polymers to stabilize the catholyte
interface, thereby enhancing the performance of solid polymer bat-
teries is also a method of PEO modiﬁcation [112].
The dissociation constant of the lithium salt determines the
ability of the ion pair and ion cluster to bind to the polymer.
Increasing the dissociation degree of anionic lithium salts in the
electrolyte system can provide more carriers of lithium ions. The
dissociation degree of lithium salts is mainly affected by the struc-
ture of the applied polymers. The kinds and sizes of the anion
groups in the alkali metal salts determine the ionic conductivity
for applied polymer electrolytes. The larger the anion group, the
smaller the lattice energy, and the lithium salt can easily dissociate
and obtain more free ions, which can signiﬁcantly improve the
ionic conductivity of the whole polymer electrolyte system. The
common conductive lithium salts with large anion groups are
LiClO4 [113], LiPF6 [114], LiAsF6 [115], and LiBF4 [116], etc. The
obtained results can be conﬁrmed by Armand and co-workers as
early as 1990 [117]. They also made a study on lithium salts with
a large anion volume (eg. LiTFSI, LiTFSM and so on). It was pointed
out that the highly delocalized anion electron cloud in lithium salts
led to the increased polymer chain motility, and polymers contain-
ing these lithium salts have lower crystallinity and anion mobility
number as well as higher conductivity.
Appropriately increasing the concentration of lithium salt can
also increase the ion migration number. However, the change of
electrolyte salt concentration may bring inﬂuences in both positive
and negative directions, so the lithium salt concentration should
not be too large to avoid side effects such as increased polymer
Tg and the aggravation of salt ion complex. In 1993, Angell et al.
[118] designed and prepared a LiClO3-LiClO4/PEO polymer elec-
trolyte system and measured the conductivity with the increase
of lithium salt content as follows: a peak of conductivity appeared
at 10% of lithium salt content, and then gradually decreased and
reached the lowest value at 30%. And with the further increase of
lithium salt content, the system entered the ‘‘polymer in salt”
region, and the ionic conductivity increased with the addition of
lithium salt content and reached as high as 10-3 Scm1 at 25 C.
At this point, the nature of the ‘‘polymer in salt” system is mainly
dependent on the nature of the salt. The result suggests that under
certain conditions the long-range transport of ions can be achieved
without depending on the relaxation process of the polymer chain
segments.
In addition to the modiﬁcation of the structure and concentra-
tion of the lithium salt, the crystallinity of the polymer and the
enhancement of the ionic conductivity can also be reduced to some
extent by designing PEO based composite electrolytes. These poly-
mers mainly include blending, co-polymerization, and cross-
linking with other polymers and the addition of inorganic ﬁllers.
We will elaborate on more cases of composite electrolytes in Sec-
tion 3 of this chapter.

Although polymer electrolytes have been widely used in SSLSBs,
most of SPEs face some challenges such as low mechanical strength
at high temperature and low ionic conductivity at RT [147]. Sharp
lithium dendrites can easily grow and penetrate in the soft SPE,
eventually causing the battery to short circuit. During the develop-
ment of SEs, some materials combining high modulus and excel-
lent ionic conductivity may effectively suppress lithium dendrites
growth in batteries. A recent study also showed that SPEs with
shear modulus of the same order of magnitude as lithium metal
anodes exhibit unprecedented lithium dendrite growth resistance,
so it is particularly important to enhance the mechanical strength
of SPEs to suppress lithium dendrites [61,80]. By introducing non-
ionic conductive ceramic ﬁllers (such as Al2O3, SiO2, LLZO, LATP
and so on) into polymer electrolytes to enhance the mechanical
strength and ionic conductivity of the SPE, lithium dendrites can
be effectively inhibited. The introduction of additional polymers
in the pristine SPE by polymer blending, co-polymerization and
cross-linking can also be effective in inhibiting the inhibition of
dendrite growth. For example, Khurana et al. [148] reported a
cross-linked polyethylene-poly(ethylene oxide) SPE, which mainly
constrains the PEO chains by incorporating them into a cross-linker
reduces the crystallization of PEO in the copolymers. Thus the
excellent resistance to dendrite growth ability can be obtained.
In general, polymer electrolytes have generally received much
more attention and research in recent years because of their supe-
rior ﬂexibility, ease of mass production, and tighter electrode/elec-
trolyte interface bonding than solid electrolytes such as oxides and
sulﬁdes. However, polymer electrolytes generally have low ionic
conductivity, the stabilization potential window is not wide
enough, and both mechanical strength and thermal stability need
to be further improved. Therefore, it is not recommended to be
used alone in SSLSBs, and modiﬁcation by physical or chemical
has become a hot topic of current research.
3.2. Inorganic solid electrolyte
In recent years, all-inorganic solid-state batteries (AISSBs) also
have received extensive attention due to their signiﬁcant improve-
ments in safety and energy density over liquid-state batteries
[16,149,150]. Inorganic SEs (ISEs) have lithium ion mobility num-
bers closing to 1, ionic conductivity 2 to 4 orders of magnitude
higher than polymer SEs at ambient temperature, and a wide elec-
trochemical stability window [61,151]. Because ISE cannot dissolve
LiPSs, they can physically and effectively block the shuttle effect of
LiPSs [30,69]. In addition, ISEs with high mechanical strength can
also regulate the uniform deposition of lithium on the anode side
and inhibit the growth of lithium dendrites. Combined with the
good thermal stability, ﬂame retardancy and safety of ISE, these
advantages make it a promising application in LSBs including
SSLSBs.
Most ISE materials have a crystalline structure with a speciﬁc
framework formed by coordination polyhedron. We can classify
them into sulﬁde, oxide and nitride SEs, etc. based on the different
heteroatoms in their ligands [61]. Next, we will introduce them in
detail.
3.2.1. Sulﬁde
Among various candidate materials for SEs, sulﬁde-based inor-
ganic electrolytes have attracted much attention due to their high
ionic conductivity, mechanical properties, and good compatibility
with sulfur cathodes [18,93,152,153]. Compared with SPE and
oxide SEs, sulﬁde SEs have relatively low grain boundary resistance
resulting in high ionic conductivity (10-3-10-2 S cm1) at RT. At the
same time, sulﬁde electrolytes have relatively low elastic modulus
and relatively low hardness, so the processability also is very excel-
lent. In addition, sulﬁde electrolytes also exhibit excellent compat-
ibility and better interfacial contact, making them the most
promising SE type for SSLSBs [30,61,107,154]. Although great pro-
gress has been made, most sulﬁde electrolytes have high hygro-
scopic properties, making them less stable in air, degrading even
in dry air conditions, prone to hydrolysis to produce toxic H2S
gas, and the structure collapses during hydrolysis, resulting in a
dramatic drop in ionic conductivity and potential safety issues
[61,155]. In addition, narrow electrochemical windows (such as
1.71–2.31 V for Li3PS4 and 1.72–2.29 V for Li10GeP2S12) also hinder
their large-scale production and application [154]. Sulﬁde-based
inorganic electrolyte types mainly include: thio-LISICON type,
lithium argyrodite type, glass–ceramic type, etc. Next, we will give
a detailed introduction according to these classiﬁcations [156].

(2) Lithium argyrodite type
Argyrodite (Ag8GeS6) is a rare sulﬁde ore containing Ag and Ge
elements. Replacing Ag+ in the argyrodite with Li+, and at the same

transport, wide electrochemical window and they also can be syn-

3.2.2. Oxide
Besides sulﬁde SEs, oxide solid electrolytes (OSEs) are another
class of SIEs with fast ion diffusion channels, exhibiting high stabil-
ity and good compatibility, and generally possessing a relatively
wide electrochemical window [155]. Oxides are chemically more
stable in air than those of sulﬁdes. They are stable in dry air and
easy to synthesize and handle, but their ionic conductivity is not
as high as that of sulﬁdes. In addition, OSEs block lithium dendrites
which could lead to an increase in the overall energy density,
owing to the intrinsic properties of Li-metal [93,176]. The most
studied ceramic electrolytes are NASICON structured ceramic elec-
trolytes and garnet structured ceramic electrolytes, whose lattice
structure has facilitated channels suitable for rapid lithium ion
transport and a large number of defects such as ion vacancies
inside the materials, and both lattice cations and ion defects can
be involved in the ion conduction process [177].

achieves the complementary advantageous functions of each com-
ponent, which ultimately improves the comprehensive perfor-
mance of SEs, including ionic conductivity, mechanical strength,
interfacial stability, electrochemical window and migration num-
ber, etc. [61,93,101,201,202]. The application of various composite
electrolyte CPEs in LSBs can block the shuttle of LiPSs and is
expected to be used as the electrolyte for future high-energy–

Although active ﬁllers offer so many beneﬁts and have led to
signiﬁcant improvements in battery performance, however, larger

(2) Inert ﬁllers
The active ﬁller itself has ionic conductivity, which directly
affects the migration process of lithium ions in the polymer sys-
tem. While the inert ﬁller itself is not directly involved in the
conduction of lithium ions, it also can enhance the performance
of the composite electrolyte through interaction. Inert ﬁllers
mainly refer to typical ceramic oxides such as Al2O3, TiO2, and
SiO2 have been used in SPE based LSBs to achieve dendrite free
lithium anodes by regulating the lithium deposition of polar func-
tional groups, showing a strong ability to inhibit lithium dendrite
growth [28,61]. These inert ﬁllers cannot conduct ions by them-
selves, but when distributed in the polymer matrix, they can be
acted as cross-linking centers through physical and/or chemical
interactions, hindering the recrystallization of polymer chains
and thus reducing the crystallinity of the polymer. At the same
time, there is a strong Lewis acid-base interaction between the
surface chemical groups of the inert ﬁllers and the cation/anion
in the corresponding Li salts, which weakens the bond between
Li+ and the salt anion and promotes the dissociation of the Li salts
[203].
SiO2 is a typical widely used inert ﬁller with hydroxyl groups on
the surface, good dispersibility and large speciﬁc surface area. Lee
et al. [210] extracted a highly porous silica (RHSiO2) with a 3D net-
work nanostructure from rice husk as an inert ﬁller combined with
PEO polymer to form a low-cost, high-performance CPE. RHSiO2
particles have highly porous channels that allow PEO to create fast
3D lithium ion channels for improved ionic conductivity, which
plays a critical role in CPE. With the increase of RHSiO2 content,
the ionic conductivity was greatly improved. At RT, 10 wt% of
RHSiO2 CPE exhibited the highest ionic conductivity of 1.1  10-4
S cm1. In addition, the RHSiO2 CPEs of the SSLSBs exhibit stable
cycling ability (511 mA h g1 at the 300 th cycle) and an excellent
rate capability (348 mA h g1 at 1C).
However, since the improvement of ionic conductivity depends
on the surface interactions between the ﬁller and PEO, its beneﬁt is
limited by the maximum ﬁller loading. After this, the ionic conduc-
tivity decreases due to the aggregation of the ﬁller and the winding
of the Li+ pathways. Further modiﬁcations of the nanoﬁller, such as
surface functional groups or grafting onto PEO chains, can provide
satisfactory ionic conductivity. For example, Tao et al. [211] pre-
pared a composite electrolyte consisting of ionic liquid grafted
oxide nanoparticles and PEO polymers, in which the prepared
IL@ZrO2 exhibited high ionic conductivity at low temperatures,
reaching 4.95  10-4 S cm1, 2.32  10-4 S cm1 at 50 and 37 C,
respectively. At the same time, the assembled SSLSBs with this
composite electrolyte exhibited excellent electrochemical perfor-
mance, with discharge speciﬁc capacities of 986 mA h g1 and
600 mA h g1 at 50 and 37 C, respectively.
In conclusion, organic–inorganic composite electrolyte realizes
complementary advantages and functions of polymer and inor-
ganic ﬁller, which ultimately improves the comprehensive perfor-
mance of SE and has become the current research hotspot of SSE
for LSBs. However, this type of electrolyte still faces many chal-
lenges, including ionic conductivity, electrochemical window,
solid–solid interface issues, two-phase compatibility and other
key issues that need to be further explored.

hindering the shuttle effect of LiPSs. Combining these advantages,
the prepared PEO/LiTFSI/In2O3 SPE based SSLSBs exhibited out-
standing lithium anode stability and excellent cycling perfor-
mance. The mechanism of In2O3 to enhance the performance of
PEO based SSLSBs is shown in Fig. 9(A).
Li6.5La3Zr1.5Ta0.5O12 (LLZTO) is a typical composite electrolyte
active ﬁller and has been extensively studied. Shao et al. [147]
designed and fabricated a thermo-polymerized composite gel poly-
mer electrolyte for quasi-SSLSBs. The preparation process of homo-
geneous CSE was presented in Fig. 9(B). The prepared CPE has a
satisfying ionic conductivity and a wide electrochemical window
as high as above 4.5 V. In particular, the incorporation of garnet
LLZTO (0.3 lm) particle improves the ﬂame retardancy, mechani-
cal property and thermal stability. In addition, the application of
CPE in LSBs can hinder the shuttle of LiPSs to a certain extent,
and the quasi-solid LSBs assembled by CPE showed good electro-
chemical performance with only 0.22% decay per cycle at 0.1C
for 200 cycles.
In conclusion, compounding 0D ﬁllers with polymers not only
reduces the crystallinity of the polymer matrix, but also enables
the conduction of lithium ions in inorganic ceramic particles,
which greatly enhances the ionic conductivity performance of
CSE. However, the increase of ionic conductivity depends on the
surface interaction between the ﬁller and the polymer. Under con-
ditions beyond the maximum ﬁller loading, the ionic conductivity
decreases due to the aggregation of ﬁllers and the entanglement of
Li+ paths. It is also a challenge to avoid the aggregation of such ﬁl-
lers in the polymer so as to achieve uniform dispersion. Therefore,
0D ﬁllers require further modiﬁcations such as nanosizing, surface
functional groups or grafting onto polymer chain segments.
3.4.2. 1D materials
Replacing 0D ﬁllers with one-dimensional(1D) ﬁllers (nan-
otubes, nanorods and nanoﬁbers) is a reasonable choice to further
increase the lithium ion conductivity. 1D nanostructured materials
with large surface-to-volume ratios ensure large electrode–elec-
trolyte contact areas and provide sufﬁcient active sites to acceler-
ate reaction kinetics and also endows efﬁcient 1D electrons and
lithium ions transport pathways. In addition, the longer dimension
of the 1D material provides a more continuous percolation path-
way, so there is less chance of agglomeration. It is worth noting
that the addition of 1D material to the electrolyte can effectively
reduce the crystallinity of the polymer matrix and improve the
ionic conductivity. At the same time, the growth of lithium den-
drites was successfully inhibited and the penetration of lithium
dendrites was hindered [217].
Therefore, 1D materials incorporated into polymer matrix are
useful for improving ion conductivity and mechanical properties
[217]. For example, Lin et al. [218] designed and prepared elec-
trolytes for using natural halloysite nano-clay, which belongs to
aluminosilicates (Al2Si2O5(OH)4), as ﬁllers for PEO matrix. The hal-
loysite nanotube (HNT) is prepared as shown in Fig. 9(C). The
lithium salt was separated into lithium ions and anions by the
oppositely charged halloysite nanotube surfaces, where lithium
ions were adhered to the outer tube silica surface with negatively
charged and anions were absorbed on the inner tube aluminol sur-
face indicating positively charged, providing a well-organized
transport channel for lithium ions.
The integrated structure of electrolyte and electrode with tight
electrode/electrolyte interface contact, direct and fast electron
transport paths, and three-dimensional(3D) ion diffusion channels
contributes well to the performance improvement of SSLSBs. 1D
materials also play an essential role in the design of the integrated
structure of electrolyte and electrode. Yan et al. [219] introduced
an interconnected electrolyte-cathode bilayer structure as shown
in Fig. 9(D), which had an interconnected LLAZO channel from
the electrolyte into the cathode. The highly conductive one-
dimensional carbon nanoﬁbers can form a continuous ultrafast
conduction pathway for electrons, and the structural design of
the LLAZO nanoparticles continuously embedded in porous carbon
nanoﬁbers shortens the diffusion distance of Li+ and electrons,
greatly
reduces
the
charge
transfer
resistance
and
strongly
improves the kinetics of the redox reaction. In addition, the entire
structure can be mechanically robust while maintaining ﬂexibility
due to the excellent mechanical strength of carbon nanoﬁbers.
In summary, 1D ﬁllers have larger contact area with the poly-
mer matrix compared to 0D nanoparticle ﬁllers. Also, 1D ﬁllers
have high aspect ratios, which can create continuous ion transport
paths and thus improve the ionic conductivity of the electrolyte.
However, there are still many problems to be solved. Although
the application of 1D nanomaterials has improved the conductivity
of polymers to some extent, most conversions are still limited to
making SSLSBs work only at higher temperatures, which is not only
a safety hazard but also not conducive to the commercial develop-
ment of batteries. At the same time, the synthesis methods of 1D
materials (e.g., hydrothermal and calcination methods at high tem-
peratures and pressures) are not suitable for mass production and
commercialization. In terms of the long-term development of 1D
nanomaterials for solid-state lithium batteries, there is an urgent
need
to
explore
methods
that
are
convenient,
fast,
energy-efﬁcient, and suitable for large-scale production.
3.4.3. 2D materials
2D ﬁllers (montmorillonite (MMT), halloysite nanotube (HNT),
GO, boron nitride (BN), clay ﬂakes, MXene and so on), have a rich
surface and abundant functional groups, thus which can form more
active interfaces with polymers in CPEs. In addition, these 2D
materials can facilitate the rapid transport of lithium ions through
their ordered channels.
Exfoliated natural mineral ﬁllers can also be effective in
improving the ionic conductivity of polymer-based SEs. Minerals/-
clays are usually silicates with polar surfaces which can be further
exposed by layering. In addition, the mechanical strength, electro-
chemical stability and thermal stability of the prepared electrolyte
can be improved [110]. For example, Tang et al. [208] showed for
the ﬁrst time a PEO-based SPE reinforced with 2D ﬁllers using ver-
miculite ﬂakes VS, which was chemically close to inert ceramic ﬁl-
lers. So it had excellent chemical and thermal stability, and good
mechanical strength. In this composite system, a vertically aligned,
continuous
and
penetrating
polymer-ﬁller
interface
can
be
obtained, which facilitated Li+ transport as shown in Fig. 10(A).
Similarly, Zhai et al. [220] reported a thin laminar composite solid
electrolyte (LCSE), namely Vr/PEO-LCSE, was fabricated by ﬁltrat-
ing vermiculite nanosheets, followed by intercalation of PEO-
LiTFSI into the interlayer through a swelling and ﬁltration method.
The preparation process is shown in Fig. 10(B). The continuous and
regular interlayer channels improve the motility of PEO chains and
dissociation of LiTFSI with highly enhanced ionic conductivity. In
addition, the strength of the vermiculite nanosheets endows Vr/
PEO-LCSE with more excellent mechanical strength, and its com-
pressive strength reached as high as 131 MPa, which was 550%
higher than that of pure PEO.

3.4.4. 3D materials
The multi-purpose 3D frame ﬁller can effectively avoid agglom-

inhibiting the growth of lithium dendrites. Therefore, active ﬁllers

polymer modiﬁcation. Among the numerous 3D frameworks, inter-
connected ﬁber networks with high surface area/volume ratios are
one of the promising candidates for promoting ion transport and
electrochemical stability [222].

usually lower than 10-3 S cm1, which needs further improvement.

challenging due to the immaturity of the technology. In order to
achieve mass production, it is necessary to improve the prepara-

application requirements of lithium batteries. Therefore, the design
and preparation of CSEs and the functional hybridization of each
component have become an effective way to improve the perfor-
mance of SSEs. Despite the rapid development in the ﬁeld of SSEs,
there are still many challenges in the investigation of the basic prin-
ciples and practical applications. For example, the microscopic
interaction mechanism between inorganic ﬁllers and polymer
materials is not clear, and how to disperse inorganic particles
evenly in polymer matrix also needs to be further solved through
some novel methods. Therefore, the in-depth study of the conduc-
tion mechanism of lithium ions in CSEs, the synergistic interaction
among components and the interfacial properties will provide guid-
ance to further improve the performance of CSEs [227].
4. Conclusions and perspectives
The urgent demand for high energy density batteries and the
attention to battery safety make it an inevitable trend to use SE
instead of traditional liquid electrolyte in LSBs. The SSLSBs are
not only very attractive for large-scale energy storage systems,
but are also expected to ﬁnd applications in a variety of ﬁelds
including customizable and wearable batteries. The application of
wearable mobile phones, high-safety electric vehicles, drones, pho-
toelectric space stations and other aspects can make our life safer,
more convenient and more efﬁcient than before. Considering the
long-term development of LSBs including SSLSBs and the great
investment in recent years, SSLSB is the best choice for storage sys-
tem in the near future (Fig. 11).
At present, a lot of scientiﬁc researchers are focused on the
development of SEs with higher ionic conductivity. SSLSBs using
SSE instead of highly ﬂammable and enviably reactive organic-
based electrolyte has great advantages over the traditional liquid
LSBs. Firstly, the LiPSs are insoluble in SEs, and the SSEs are
expected to block the transfer of LiPSs by physically separating
the cathode and anode electrode, fundamentally and effectively
overcoming the shuttle problem of dissolved LiPSs. All of these
merits can provide an opportunity to achieve high energy density,
safety and reliability. In addition, the density and structure of the
SE allows more charged ions to gather and conduct a larger cur-
rent, increasing the battery capacity and exhibiting a high energy
density which is expected to exceed the battery performance of
current lithium-ion batteries. At the same time, SEs are non-
volatile and non-ﬂammable in nature, have a high melting temper-
ature, and can improve safety and durability by eliminating highly
ﬂammable liquid electrolytes. SEs are inert to lithium metal, and
their inherent rigidity and non-ﬂammability limit lithium dendrite
growth and reduce ﬁre risk.
Despite SEs having made great progress and developments in Li-
ion and LSBs, there are still some limitations that need to be paid
attention to the aspect. These limitations will bring some obstacles
to widespread commercial application. Firstly, ionic conductivity
remains a prerequisite for evaluating SEs. Existing SEs do not meet
sufﬁcient ionic conductivity and other standards, and ﬁnding and
preparing SPEs with high ionic conductivity at ambient tempera-
tures remains a signiﬁcant challenge up to now. In addition, non-
ﬂowinginorganicsolid ceramic or glass electrolytesare rigid and dif-
ﬁcult to close contact with cell electrodes for SSLSBs. Therefore, the
interface problem between SE and electrode is particularly signiﬁ-
cant in inorganic SSLSBs. At present, how to construct SSE with high
ionic conductivity, good mechanical properties and good compati-
bility with electrodes at RT is the main challenge faced by SSLSBs.
At present, composite electrolytes with multifunctional features
are the most promising direction. The research and development of
composite solid electrolytes starts with the identiﬁcation of the
detailed application area of the composite electrolyte in order to
determine its characteristics and performance requirements. Then,
suitable raw materials and production processes are selected
according to the requirements of various application areas. After
the complex research, developments and tests, they are continu-
ously optimized to ﬁnally meet the performance requirements.
Compared with the single inorganic solid electrolyte or organic poly-
mer electrolyte, this research process is tedious and complicated,
and the raw material ratios, additives and production processes of
the composite electrolyte all have important effects on the perfor-
mance of the composite electrolyte for the ﬁnal batteries. However,
so far, the design of guiding electrolytes is not clear enough, and
electrolyte design is hampered by the lack of effective guiding prin-
ciples. Therefore, the development of composite electrolytes for
speciﬁc performance requirements still is a huge challenge. In addi-
tion, some composite electrolytes also suffer from complex struc-
tures, complicated preparation processes and high costs, which are
not conducive to large-scale production. Overall, composite elec-
trolytes with comprehensive multi-functions have a longer way to
go to meet the actual needs of continuous development.

mainly are oxide, sulﬁde and nitride electrolytes, etc. Different
types of SE materials have their own advantages and disadvan-

Composite electrolyte has the advantages of both polymer and
inorganic electrolyte, so it is the most promising to meet various
properties at the same time, but there are still a lot of scientiﬁc
and technical problems to be solved. For example, the microscopic
interaction mechanism between inorganic ﬁllers and polymer
materials is not clear, and how to disperse inorganic particles
evenly in polymer matrix also needs to be further solved through
some novel methods.
Therefore, many scientiﬁc and technical challenges must be
overcome before SEs are commercially available. Current solutions
worth studying: (1) Designing high electronic/ionic conductivity
sulfur cathodes, such as developing and implementing more pre-
cise conductive coatings or electrode fabrication techniques,
designing and fabricating cathode materials with high electronic
and ionic conductivities; (2) Optimizing solid-state electrolytes
(such as precisely controlling the dispersion and arrangement of
‘‘active” ISE ﬁllers in polymer matrixes) and developing novel elec-
trolytes with excellent properties; (3) Improving electrode/elec-
trolyte interface stability and enhancing interfacial dynamics
between electrolyte and anode For example, replacing the in-situ
formed SEI with ex-situ coatings or using novel lithium anodes
such as lithium-based composite anodes or lithium alloys; (4)
Using more advanced test equipment and characterization tech-
niques to deeply analyze the conduction mechanism of lithium
ions in SSEs (such as, synchrotron X-ray techniques, solid state
nuclear magnetic resonance, neutron diffraction and in situ imag-
ing, etc.) and applying more theoretical calculations like DFT (den-
sity functional theory).
SSLSBs, as the future development direction of lithium batteries,
have been supported by policy plans of various countries and have
been extensively studied by scientiﬁc researchers. With the
advancement of novel technologies and new ﬁelds, the optimiza-
tion of solid-state electrolytes and the development of solid-state
electrolytes with new materials and structures are potential
approaches to solve the current problems of SSLSBs and accelerate
the feasibility of energy storage applications.
