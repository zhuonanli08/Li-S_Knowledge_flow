Enhanced Electrochemical Stability in All-Solid-State
Lithium–Sulfur Batteries with Lithium Argyrodite Electrolyte
Yin-Ju Yen, Hyunki Sul, and Arumugam Manthiram*
Lithium argyrodite electrolytes with halide dopings (Li6PS5X, X = Cl, Br,
I) are a group of sulﬁde solid electrolyte materials (SSEs) widely adopted in all-
solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) for their ease of processing and
low material costs. Speciﬁcally, the Cl-doped lithium argyrodite electrolytes,
with the highest ionic conductivities among the halide-doped lithium
argyrodite electrolytes, are extensively studied in the literature. However, their
narrow electrochemical stability window limits the performance of ASSLSBs
due to the inevitable electrolyte decomposition within the operating voltage
window. Here, the Br-doped lithium argyrodite electrolyte (Li5.5PS4.5Br1.5)
is utilized as a comparison to the widely-adopted Cl-doped lithium argyrodite
electrolyte (Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5) in ASSLSBs. Despite its slightly lower ionic
conductivity, Li5.5PS4.5Br1.5 signiﬁcantly enhances the overall cell performance
and cyclability of the LiIn ǁ SSE ǁ S-C-SSE cell due to a wider electrochemical
stability window. With a better electrochemical stability of Li5.5PS4.5Br1.5
within the working voltage range of ASSLSBs, the LiIn ǁ SSE ǁ S-C-SSE cell with
Li5.5PS4.5Br1.5 attains an excellent capacity retention of 90% over 100 cycles at
1.4–3.1 V and 68% over 250 cycles even at a wider voltage window of 1.1–3.1 V.
1. Introduction
All-solid-state batteries (ASSBs) are being considered as one
of the promising candidates for next-generation energy stor-
age due to their enhanced operational safety and high speciﬁc
energy.[1–3] With the adoption of environmentally friendly and
earth-abundant sulfur as active material, all-solid-state lithium-
sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) with high energy densities could
be achieved owing to the high theoretical capacity of sulfur
(1675 mA h g−1).[4,5] The replacement of conventional liquid elec-
trolytes with solid-state electrolytes can reduce the safety risks

batteries.[5,6,20–22]
Another bottleneck in solid-state electrolytes is the compati-
bility of the electrochemical stability within the cell working volt-

ionic conductivity, the narrow electrochemical stability window
of lithium argyrodite electrolytes fails to fully cover the redox volt-
age range of the sulfur conversion reaction, thus leading to a de-
composition of the electrolyte during the cycling process of the
ASSLSBs.[5] The electrochemical decomposition of the lithium
argyrodite electrolytes results in some reversible capacity in the
ASSLSB studies; however, the formation of undesirable oxidation

In this study, the Cl-doped lithium argyrodite electrolyte
Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 (hereafter referred to as LPSCl) and Br-doped
lithium argyrodite electrolyte Li5.5PS4.5Br1.5 (hereafter referred to
as LPSBr) are applied as the solid-state electrolyte in ASSLSBs.
The cyclability of the LiIn ǁ SSE ǁ S-C-SSE cells with LPSCl and
LPSBr are compared within two voltage windows, 1.4–3.1 V and
1.1–3.1 V. Interestingly, in both the voltage ranges, the overall
capacity and the cycle stability of the LiIn ǁ SSE ǁ S-C-SSE cell
with LPSBr are signiﬁcantly improved even with a slightly lower
ionic conductivity of LPSBr compared to LPSCl. Therefore, the
electrolyte decomposition behaviors of LPSCl and LPSBr are in-
vestigated to fully delineate the electrochemical stability of the
two electrolytes during the cycling process of the ASSLSBs. With
better reductive stability to lower voltages and better oxidative sta-
bility to higher voltages, the electrochemical stability window of
LPSBr is wider than that of LPSCl, contributing to less electrolyte
decomposition and better cycle life. Besides, in the voltage range
of 1.1–3.1 V, the ﬁrst discharge process is found to be the de-
termining step for the long-term cyclability of the LiIn ǁ SSE ǁ
S-C-SSE cell. With LPSBr in the ASSLSB, a high areal capacity of
2.0–2.4 mA h cm−2 is achieved. Furthermore, the capacity reten-
tion with LPSBr is signiﬁcantly improved to 90% after 100 cycles
compared to that of LPSCl (63%) in the range of 1.4–3.1 V, and it
remains at 68% even at a much wider voltage window of 1.1–3.1 V
after much-extended cycling over 250 cycles.
2. Results and Discussion
2.1. Materials Characterization
LPSCl has been widely studied and better understood in the lit-
erature for its outstanding ionic conductivity.[5,6,20–22] To iden-
tify alternative electrolytes with ionic conductivity comparable
to LPSCl, LPSBr was selected due to its similar properties to
LPSCl.[15,17] As shown in Figure S1a (Supporting Information),
with the same synthesis method, LPSCl and LPSBr show simi-
lar XRD patterns, conﬁrming the argyrodite structure,[27] while
the peak positions of LPSBr are slightly shifted to lower 2𝜃val-
ues due to a larger ionic size of Br−compared to that of Cl−.[17,28]
From Figure S1b,c (Supporting Information), the ionic conduc-
tivities of LPSCl and LPSBr at room temperature are 1.8 and 1.4
mS cm−1, respectively, consistent with the reported values in the
literature.[14]
2.2. Electrochemical Performance
According to previous literature, the lower cutoﬀvoltage has
been reported to impact the electrochemical performance more
than the upper cutoﬀvoltage.[5,29] To operate the ASSLSBs with
Cl-doped argyrodite electrolytes, a suﬃciently high potential of
0.8 V versus Li/In (1.4 V versus Li/Li+) for the lower cutoﬀvolt-
age has been suggested. Therefore, the voltage window of the
LiIn ǁ SSE ǁ S-C-SSE cells with LPSCl and LPSBr (hereafter re-
ferred to as S-LPSCl and S-LPSBr, respectively) was initially set at
1.4–3.1 V (versus Li/Li+) to compare the cyclability, as shown in
Figure 1a. After discharging to 1.4 V, the discharge capacity of
the S-LPSCl cell shows a ﬁrst discharge capacity of 850 mA h
g−1 with a peak capacity of 970 mA h g−1. Interestingly, with a
slightly lower ionic conductivity, the S-LPSBr cell displays an ini-
tial discharge capacity of 1025 mA h g−1 with a peak discharge
capacity of 1145 mA h g−1 that outperforms the S-LPSCl cell. The
gradually increasing capacities in both cells during the ﬁrst few
cycles to reach the peak capacities are a result of a gradual acti-
vation of the sulfur active material covered by the catholyte and
the electrolyte decomposition.[29] After 100 cycles at a C/20 rate,
the capacity retention of the S-LPSCl cell attains 63%, while the
S-LPSBr cell demonstrates a signiﬁcantly improved capacity re-
tention of 90%.
However, to increase the utilization of the active material and
thus to enhance the discharge capacity, reducing the lower cut-
oﬀvoltage is beneﬁcial. Therefore, the LiIn ǁ SSE ǁ S-C-SSE
cells with LPSCl and LPSBr were also compared in the voltage
window of 1.1–3.1 V (versus Li/Li+), as shown in Figure 1b.
When lowered to 1.1 V, the discharge capacity of the S-LPSCl
cell reaches a higher value of 1070 mA h g−1 with a peak ca-
pacity of 1184 mA h g−1. However, the decreased lower cutoﬀ
voltage leads to a faster capacity decay and an unstable Coulom-
bic eﬃciency throughout the cycling process. Therefore, the S-
LPSCl cell discharged to 1.1 V was stopped at 100 cycles due
to its poor cyclability (41% over 100 cycles). In contrast, the S-
LPSBr cell, while also displaying a higher discharge capacity of
1103 mA h g−1 with a peak discharge capacity of 1337 mA h g−1
due to the lowering of the cutoﬀvoltage to 1.1 V, exhibits a signif-
icantly improved capacity retention of 84% after 100 cycles and
68% after 250 cycles. Besides, the smaller capacity diﬀerence be-
tween the S-LPSBr cells discharged to 1.1 and 1.4 V, compared to
that of the S-LPSCl cells, suggests that the contribution of elec-
trolyte decomposition to the capacity may be less signiﬁcant with
LPSBr.
In order to understand the diﬀerences in the capacity retention
between the S-LPSCl and S-LPSBr cells, the cells were assessed
with electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) after the ﬁrst
discharge to 1.4 and 1.1 V, as shown in Figure 1c,d. Although a
decrease in the overall impedance may be expected on lowering
the cutoﬀvoltage to 1.1 V due to the formation of more ionically
conductive lithium sulﬁde (Li2S) compared to sulfur, the S-LPSCl
cell exhibits a signiﬁcant growth in impedance compared to that
with a higher cutoﬀvoltage of 1.4 V. In contrast, the S-LPSBr cell
shows a decrease in impedance when discharged to 1.1 V. The dif-
ferences in impedance trends between the S-LPSCl and S-LPSBr
cells when lowering the cutoﬀvoltage from 1.4 to 1.1 V suggest
that the lower cutoﬀvoltage of 1.1 V might lead to the formation
of some passivating side products in the LPSCl cell, while actu-
ally leading to more active material utilization in the S-LPSBr cell.
The diﬀerence in the cyclability within the two voltage windows
and the diﬀerences in the impedance change during the ﬁrst dis-
charge process indicate that in spite of the highest ionic conduc-
tivity among the halide-doped lithium argyrodite electrolytes, the
inferior reductive stability of LPSCl has a greater impact on the
electrochemical stability and cycling performance of the S-LPSCl
cell. Besides, the impedance growth in the S-LPSCl cell infers
that the side products formed due to the electrochemical decom-
position of LPSCl might not be favorable in the lithium–sulfur
cell system.[30,31] These results suggest the importance of a wide

2.3. Electrochemical Stability
LPSCl has been reported to undergo the sulfur reduction process
at around 2.0 V and phosphorus reduction process at ≈1.0 V.[6]
To explore the full reductive and oxidative stability window of the
electrolytes, the LiIn ǁ SSE ǁ SSE-C cells with LPSCl and LPSBr
were directly discharged to 0.6 V or charged to 3.6 V to evaluate
the electrochemical stability. LPSBr was expected to exhibit de-
composition behavior similar to LPSCl due to their similar mate-
rials characteristics. The SSE-C cathodes were prepared without
sulfur to isolate the behavior of the electrolyte materials, and indi-
vidual cells were assembled for each test to eliminate the interfer-
ence from the decomposed products,[20] as shown in Figure 2a.
The corresponding diﬀerential capacity plot (dQ dV−1 plot) is
shown in Figure 2b. From Figure 2a, during the entire direct dis-
charge or charge process, the LiIn ǁ SSE ǁ SSE-C cell with LPSCl
exhibits greater capacity compared to the cell with LPSBr, indi-
cating that LPSBr is less prone to decomposition and does not
generate much additional capacity during the normal cell cycling
process. In the dQ dV−1 plot (Figure 2b), LPSCl undergoes an
initial minor reduction starting at 1.2 V, followed by a major re-
duction starting at 1.0 V. As a comparison, LPSBr only exhibits a
major reduction beginning at 1.0 V. Regarding the oxidation sta-
bility, LPSCl begins decomposing at 2.6 V, while LPSBr decompo-
sition starts at a higher voltage of 2.9 V. In addition, when cycled
between 1.1 and 3.1 V, the reductive and oxidative peak intensities
for LPSBr in the dQ dV−1 plot are both signiﬁcantly weaker than
those for LPSCl. These results indicate that LPSBr has superior
reduction and oxidation stability compared to LPSCl.
Besides, within the voltage window of 1.1–3.1 V that the LiIn ǁ
SSE ǁ S-C-SSE cell was tested, both LPSCl and LPSBr undergo
oxidative decomposition, while only LPSCl exhibits signiﬁcant
reductive decomposition. This explains the greater diﬀerence in
the ﬁrst discharge capacity of the S-LPSCl cells when discharged
to 1.4 V versus 1.1 V. The more pronounced electrolyte decom-
position in LPSCl within 1.1–3.1 V further results in the deteri-
orated ﬂuctuations in the Coulombic eﬃciency in the S-LPSCl
cell compared to the S-LPSBr cell. Therefore, to further look into
the decomposition behavior of LPSCl and LPSBr, the LiIn ǁ SSE
ǁ SSE-C cell was tested within the two same voltage windows
as the LiIn ǁ SSE ǁ S-C-SSE cell to evaluate the capacity contri-
bution from the SSE-C cathodes, as shown in Figure 2c,d, and
Figures S2–S4 (Supporting Information). The calculated capacity
was based on the total mass of the electrolyte in the SSE-C cath-
odes. From Figure S2, within the voltage range of 1.4–3.1 V, the
ﬁrst discharge capacity from LPSBr is nearly zero, while LPSCl

g−1 compared to LPSBr (38 mA h g−1), and the discharge/charge

strates a lower impedance than the S-LPSBr cell due to a higher
ionic conductivity provided by LPSCl. However, after 10 cycles,
the S-LPSBr cell exhibits a lower impedance despite the inher-
ently lower ionic conductivity of LPSBr, inferring the formation
of side products from LPSCl decomposition to passivate the S-
LPSCl cell.[37] As a hint observed from the polarization change
in the S-LPSCl and S-LPSBr cells in the ﬁrst and second cy-
cles in addition to the impedance change, the electrolyte decom-
position during the ﬁrst and second cycles might be important
to investigate the long-term cyclability diﬀerence between LP-
SCl and LPSBr. Therefore, operando EIS analysis was performed
with the LiIn ǁ SSE ǁ S-C-SSE cell to look into the impedance
change during cycling in the ﬁrst and second cycles, as shown
in Figures 4 and S9 (Supporting Information).[38] The operando
EIS spectra were deconvoluted with an equivalent circuit sum-
marized in Figure S10 (Supporting Information), which consist
of high-frequency resistance (RHF), middle-frequency resistance
(RMF) close to the frequency of 1 kHz, low-frequency resistance
(RLF) close to the frequency of 1 Hz, and a Warburg impedance
followed in the lower frequency region due to the non-ideal dif-
fusion capacitive behavior.[39] These resistances correspond to
charge-transfer resistance at the grain boundary, anode, and cath-
ode, respectively.[37,39]
To focus on the impedance change at the cathode during
operando EIS measurement, the RLF values obtained from the
equivalent-circuit ﬁtting are summarized in Figure 4e. For a bet-
ter visualization of the RLF change, the data points at 1 Hz are
highlighted in each spectrum. From the ﬁrst discharge shown in
Figure 4a,b,e, the initial RLF of the S-LPSBr cell is higher than that
of the S-LPSCl cell, consistent with the static EIS measurements.
As the discharge proceeds to undergo the solid-solid conversion
from sulfur to Li2S, the RLF of the S-LPSBr cell keeps decreas-
ing due to the formation of relatively conductive Li2S.[40] How-
ever, the RLF of the S-LPSCl cell decreases at the beginning of
the discharge plateau and increases during the rest of the conver-
sion. The resistance increase in the S-LPSCl cell during the ﬁrst
discharge indicates that the electrolyte decomposition produces
unfavorable side products and thus passivates the cell, while the
better reduction stability of LPSBr mitigates the decomposition.
During the ﬁrst charge, the RLF in both cells grows as Li2S con-
verts back to insulating sulfur, and the overall RLF of the S-LPSBr
cell remains lower than that of the S-LPSCl cell. The drastic re-
sistance growth in the S-LPSCl cell at the end of the ﬁrst charge
compared to that in the S-LPSBr cell might be related to the elec-
trolyte decomposition due to the poorer oxidative stability of LP-
SCl. As the second discharge and charge continue, the trends
observed in the ﬁrst cycle persist, as shown in Figure 4e and
Figure S9 (Supporting Information). Besides, the RLF in the S-
LPSBr cell during the second cycle remains consistently lower
than that in the S-LPSCl cell, indicating better sulfur conversion
kinetics. These results suggest that the solid–solid conversion be-
tween sulfur and Li2S might become sluggish in the S-LPSCl cell
after the ﬁrst discharge, while the conversion remains smooth in
the S-LPSBr cell due to mitigated electrolyte decomposition. In
addition, diﬀerences in the decomposition behavior with LPSCl
and LPSBr in the ﬁrst cycle appear to have a great impact on the
long-term cyclability of the LiIn ǁ SSE ǁ S-C-SSE cell.
2.4. Electrolyte Decomposition
To further investigate the electrolyte decomposition between LP-
SCl and LPSBr during the ﬁrst cycle, X-ray photoelectron spec-
troscopy (XPS) P 2p data were collected on the cathodes retrieved

ﬁrst charge, respectively, as summarized in Figure 5. For the pris-
tine SSE-C cathodes, the PS4
3−peaks associated with the argy-
rodite structure of LPSCl and LPSBr are mainly detected. These
PS4
3−peaks are thus labeled as P–S–Cl and P–S–Br in the repre-
sentations of pristine LPSCl and LPSBr, with the 2p3/2 and 2p1/2
splittings at 132.0 and 132.9 eV, respectively.[26,32] After the ﬁrst
discharge process, the peak intensity of the P2Sx species at 133.0
(2p3/2) and 133.9 (2p1/2) eV increases signiﬁcantly for the LPSCl
cathode, while only a small amount of the P2Sx species is detected
in the LPSBr cathode. The weaker intensity of the P2Sx species in
the LPSBr cathode conﬁrms a reduced decomposition of LPSBr
in the ﬁrst discharge due to better reductive stability, which aligns
with the previous galvanostatic test results of the LiIn ǁ SSE ǁ SSE-
C cells. During the ﬁrst charge, the intensity of the P2Sx species
in the LPSBr cathode remains low; however, the amount of the
P2Sx species increases with a growing peak intensity in the LPSCl
cathode due to the lower oxidative stability limit.[4,6] This varia-
tion in the peak intensity of the P2Sx species between LPSCl and
LPSBr cathodes further provides evidence for the narrower elec-
trochemical stability window of LPSCl, which leads to a greater
amount of electrolyte decomposition and thus degradation of the
cycling performance. Besides, the ﬁrst discharge process might
be the critical step to aﬀect the long-term cyclability of cells with
LPSCl and LPSBr.

LPSCl cathode. The peak broadening at the higher chemical shift

3. Conclusion
In this study, we systematically compared the electrochemical
stability of argyrodite solid electrolytes to assess their impact on
the long-term solid-state cell performance. LPSCl, as the most
renowned lithium argyrodite electrolyte material with the high-
est ionic conductivity among the halide-doped argyrodite elec-
trolytes, has a narrow electrochemical stability window that leads
to electrolyte decomposition within the working voltage window
of ASSLSBs. The limited electrochemical stability of LPSCl re-
sults in the formation of LiCl and P2Sx species with poor ionic
conduction, thus passivating the cell with fast capacity decay in
the long-term cycling test of the S-LPSCl cell. As a similar alter-
native among the halide-doped lithium argyrodite electrolytes,
LPSBr is found to demonstrate a broader reductive and oxida-
tive stability window compared to LPSCl, with much-suppressed
electrolyte decomposition. Despite a slightly lower ionic conduc-
tivity of LPSBr compared to LPSCl, the long-term cyclability of
the S-LPSBr cell is well-improved, even within a wider working
voltage window of 1.1–3.1 V. The cyclability diﬀerence between
the S-LPSCl and S-LPSBr cells highlights the importance that
the electrochemical stability of the solid electrolyte within the
cell operating voltage window plays a more critical role in deter-
mining long-term cyclability than ionic conductivity in ASSLSBs.
Furthermore, despite a less amount of electrolyte decomposi-
tion from LPSBr, the formation of 𝛽-Li3PS4 as a decomposi-
tion product contributes to mitigating polarization after the ﬁrst
cycle, ultimately enhancing the cycle life of the S-LPSBr cell.
Therefore, with LPSBr as the separator and the electrolyte in the
catholyte, the S-LPSBr cell achieves an excellent areal capacity of
2.0–2.4 mA h cm−2, with a great capacity retention of 90% after
100 cycles within 1.4–3.1 V, and a much-improved capacity reten-
tion of 68% after 250 cycles within 1.1–3.1 V.
4. Experimental Section
Preparation of Lithium Argyrodite Electrolytes and Catholyte Materials:
The lithium argyrodite electrolyte materials were synthesized with a high-
energy planetary ball-milling process (Fritsch Pulverisette 6). Lithium
sulﬁde (Li2S, Sigma–Aldrich), phosphorus pentasulﬁde (P2S5, Sigma–
Aldrich), and lithium chloride (LiCl, Sigma–Aldrich) or lithium bromide
(LiBr, Thermo Scientiﬁc) were combined in a stoichiometric ratio and
mixed in a zirconia ball-milling jar with a ball-to-powder ratio of 20:1. The
planetary ball-milling procedure was set at 500 rpm for 20 h, and the pre-
cursor powder was annealed at 500 °C for 5 h with a ramping and cooling
rate of 5 °C min−1 to obtain the LPSCl or LPSBr argyrodite electrolyte. The
catholyte material in the LiIn ǁ SSE ǁ S-C-SSE cell was prepared with sul-
fur/Ketjen Black, vapor-grown carbon nanoﬁber (VGCF, Sigma–Aldrich),
and lithium argyrodite electrolyte with a planetary ball-milling process at
200 rpm for 10 h. The weight ratio of the catholyte was set at 30:20:50
for sulfur, carbon, and electrolyte. Sulfur (S, Thermo Scientiﬁc) and Ketjen
Black (KB, EC-600JD) were mixed in a weight ratio of 80:20 and ground
with a mortar and a pestle. The mixture was then melt-diﬀused in an au-
toclave at 155 °C for 12 h to obtain the S/KB composite. For the catholyte
material in the LiIn ǁ SSE ǁ SSE-C cell, the SSE-C catholyte was prepared
with the planetary ball mill by mixing the lithium argyrodite electrolyte and
KB at 300 rpm for 2 h at a weight ratio of 70:30.
Cell Assembly:
LiIn ǁ SSE ǁ S-C-SSE cells and LiIn ǁ SSE ǁ SSE-C cells
were assembled in an air-tight commercial cell setup (Qingdao Victor
Instrument Company) with a diameter of 12 mm. The argyrodite elec-
trolyte (120.0 mg) was ﬁrst densiﬁed at 240 MPa as the separator, and
the catholyte (6.7 mg) was densiﬁed at 385 MPa with the separator in the
polyether ether ketone (PEEK) mold. The indium (In, Thermo Scientiﬁc)
and lithium-metal foils were placed on the separator as the anode, and
the whole cell was densiﬁed at 70 MPa as the stack pressure. The overall
sulfur loading of the LiIn ǁ SSE ǁ S-C-SSE cell is 1.8 mg cm−2. For assem-
bling the LiIn ǁ SSE ǁ SSE-C cell and In ǁ SSE ǁ SSE-C cell, the weight of the
catholyte was calculated based on the same amount of electrolyte in the
S-C-SSE catholyte.
Materials Characterization:
The annealed LPSCl and LPSBr argyrodite
electrolytes were characterized with X-ray diﬀraction (XRD, Rigaku Mini-
ﬂex 600 diﬀractometer) to investigate the crystalline structures. The solid-
state 7Li and 31P magic angle spinning nuclear magnetic resonance (MAS
NMR, Bruker AVANCE II 400 spectrometer) spectra were conducted at
9.4 T with a 4 mm HX MAS probe. The cycled cathode powder was col-
lected from LiIn ǁ SSE ǁ SSE-C cells and loaded into 4 mm zirconia ro-
tors inside an argon-ﬁlled glovebox to spin at 12500 Hz. X-ray photoelec-
tron spectroscopy (XPS, VersaProbe4 spectrometer) was conducted with
monochromatic Al K𝛼radiation.
Electrochemical Analysis:
The ionic conductivities of the LPSCl and
LPSBr argyrodite electrolytes were measured on a Biologic VMP-3 system
and calculated based on the electrolyte thickness and the electrode area.
The electrolyte powder was densiﬁed at 355 MPa in a PEEK mold with a
diameter of 0.5 inches and stainless steel as the electrode plungers. The
galvanostatic cycling test of the LiIn ǁ SSE ǁ S-C-SSE cells was conducted
on an Arbin cycler inside an argon-ﬁlled glovebox. The operating voltage
windows were set at 1.1–3.1 V and 1.4–3.1 V (versus Li/Li+) and the cy-
cling rate was set at C/20 (1C = 1675 mA g−1) for the LiIn ǁ SSE ǁ S-C-SSE
cells. For the investigation of the electrochemical stability window, the LiIn
ǁ SSE ǁ SSE-C cells were assembled to run a direct discharge to 0.6 V or
a direct charge to 3.6 V (versus Li/Li+). The voltage window for the fol-
lowing electrochemical analyses was set at 1.1–3.1 V (versus Li/Li+) un-
less otherwise stated. For the study of the electrolyte decomposition, the
LiIn ǁ SSE ǁ SSE-C cells were cycled at a C/20 rate for 10 cycles. The static
electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS) of the LiIn ǁ SSE ǁ S-C-SSE cells
were attained with an AC amplitude of 30 mV and a frequency range of
7 MHz–10 mHz on a Biologic VMP-3 potentiostat. The cyclic voltamme-
tries (CV) of the LiIn ǁ SSE ǁ S-C-SSE cells and LiIn ǁ SSE ǁ SSE-C cells were
carried out on the Biologic VMP-3 system with a scanning rate of 0.05 mV
s−1 for 3 cycles. The operando EIS analysis of the LiIn ǁ SSE ǁ S-C-SSE
cells was conducted at a C/20 rate with a single sine mode, a frequency
range of 0.1 MHz–10 mHz, 7 points per decade, and 1 measurement per
frequency.
