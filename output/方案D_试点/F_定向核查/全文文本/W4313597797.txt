High-performance all-solid-state Li2S batteries
using an interfacial redox mediator†
Chun Yuen Kwok,

All-solid-state Li–S batteries based on lithium sulfide cathodes have generated much excitement as pos-
sible next-generation energy storage candidates because they can offer a high theoretical energy
density exceeding that of lithium-ion batteries, and the possibility of ‘‘anode-less’’ cell designs. However,
solid-state Li2S cathode kinetics are hindered by both limited ionic and electronic transport and a high
activation barrier on charge. Here we report a Li2S/LiVS2 core–shell cathode architecture design,
whereby the shell serves as both a charge-carrier transport vehicle and electron transfer mediator for
Li2S during oxidation. With an argyrodite-type solid electrolyte, the solid-state Li2S cell exhibits very
good rate capability up to 3 mA cm2 at room temperature, and nearly 80% capacity retention up to
1000 cycles at a moderate current density of 1 mA cm2. High active material loading (4 and 6 mg cm2) is
demonstrated in this configuration with stable capacity retention. High areal capacity up to 5.3 mA h cm2
with a very high active material loading of 10 mg cm2 is also reported, albeit with limited cycle life.
Broader context
All-solid-state Li–S batteries are appealing due to their high theoretical energy density at low cost, while employing solid inorganic Li-ion conducting
electrolytes eliminate the notorious polysulfide shuttle problem. Although the initial conversion of sulfur to lithium sulfide creates mechanical stress owing to
volume expansion, this can be mitigated by starting with a Li2S cathode which also allows for anode-less cell designs. However, Li2S suffers from a high
activation energy barrier on oxidation. Here, we report a strategy that reduces such sluggish kinetics by wrapping a Li2S core with a metallic LixVS2 shell that
serves as an interfacial solid redox mediator to facilitate Li2S 2 S conversion. The improved cathode kinetics enables charging at relatively high current
densities up to 3 mA cm2. Furthermore, cells operating at room temperature at 1 mA cm2 and active material loading of 2.0 mg cm2 retain 80% initial
capacity over 1000 cycles. The cells also show very long-cycle life at moderate cathode loadings of 4 and 6 mg cm2 with close to 100% coulombic efficiency.
Higher loading cells exhibit areal capacities of 45 mA h cm2 at 60 1C, albeit with reduced cycle life. This work demonstrates an approach to resolve inherent
kinetic barriers in all-solid-state batteries by microstructural design.
Introduction
The advance in electromobility has triggered a quest for novel
battery systems that go beyond lithium-ion batteries. The Li–S
battery is one candidate that could fulfill this demand because
it offers a higher theoretical energy density at a relatively low
cost. The practicality and cycling stability of this promising
technology in liquid electrolytes, however, is hampered by a
multitude of factors such as the need for high electrolyte/sulfur
ratios, the polysulfide shuttle, and lithium dendrite growth.1,2

to the polysulfide transport mechanism remain unresolved.9
Substitution of the organic electrolyte for a solid-state
lithium-ion conductor may be the ultimate solution to this
decades-old problem.10–14 However, the fabrication of all-solid-
state Li–S batteries necessitates the construction of efficient
charge transfer pathways within the cathode due to the poor
electronic (e) and ionic (Li+) conductivities of the sulfur and
Li2S redox-end members.15–18 Forming sulfur composites with
high surface area carbon11,15 or substituting sulfur with other
elements such as Se19 and Al20 can improve the cathode’s
electronic conductivity. However – unlike a conventional Li–S
cell where the flowable liquid electrolyte can easily infiltrate the
pores of the S/C composite – in a solid state configuration there

Another challenge inherent to a solid-state sulfur cathode is
the volume expansion of S 2 Li2S during cell cycling (80%)
that places a large stress on a rigid SSE material. This can lead to
cracking and detachment of active material from the electronic/
ionic conduit.26 In this respect, Li2S may pose an advantage
because the cathode architecture can be more easily tailored
to accommodate contraction on charge. Furthermore, a fully
lithiated cathode has the potential to be coupled with anode-less
designs to further boost energy density at the cell level.27–29
However, solid-state Li2S cathodes have been shown to suffer
from a high activation energy barrier on initial charge (43 V vs.
Li+/Li), in addition to higher cell polarization and lower material
utilization in comparison with their fully oxidized counterparts.
This can be ascribed to poor cathode kinetics.11,15,30 One
approach to the problem is encapsulation of the active material
using a mixed-conductor to enhance the charge-transfer effi-
ciency. Cui et al. recently showed that coating a thin nanolayer of
TiS2 on the surface of Li2S lowers the initial activation energy
barrier to 2.5 V.31 These cells used a LiTFSI/PEO electrolyte,
necessitating operation at 80 1C and exhibited an areal capacity
of 0.6 mA h cm2 in long-term cycling at a Li2S loading of
1 mg cm2. Another approach is the use of a redox mediator
to faciliate Li2S oxidation. Conventional redox mediators are
soluble electrolyte additives that can easily diffuse and shuttle
back and forth between the current collector and the isolated
active materials.32 While the diffusivity of a molecular mediator
is negligible in a true all-solid-state configuration (i.e., using an
inorganic, not polymer electrolyte),33 there are limited reports of
interfacial redox mediators.34,35
Here, we report on an encapsulated Li2S cathode design, in
which the outer 30 nm-thick LiVS2 shell serves as a charge-
carrier (Li+ and e) transport vehicle for the Li2S core. We show
that VS2 (once delithiated at the outer surface) serves as a solid-
solid interfacial mediator for Li2S 2 S conversion. By adopting
such a core–shell morphology, intimate contact between the
quasi-metallic, ion-conductive LiVS2 shell and insulating Li2S
core is realized (Fig. 1B). This helps permeate the cathode
composite with electronic and ionic pathways after it is blended
with a superionic argyrodite electrolyte. The bifunctional behavior
offered by LiVS2 improves the cathode kinetics in solid-state sulfur
electrochemistry and enables Li2S batteries that can be charged at
reasonably high current densities (up to 3 mA cm2). Using this
approach, an all-solid-state Li2S cell operating at a moderate
current density of 1 mA cm2 and active material loading of 2.0
mgLi2S+LiVS2 cm2 can be cycled for over 1000 cycles while retaining
nearly 80% of its initial capacity at room temperature. The cells
also show exceptionally long-cycle life at moderate loading of
4 and 6 mgLi2S+LiVS2 cm2. High loading cells (10mg cm2) exhibit
areal capacities of 45 mA h cm2 at 60 1C, albeit with limited
cycle life. These studies provide insight for the tailored design of
all-solid-state Li–S cathode architectures.

Electrochemistry at low-material loadings
All-solid-state Li–S cells were cycled between 1.5 V and 3.1 V vs.
Li+/Li at room temperature, utilizing In/Li alloy as the anode
and Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 as the separator. Cathodes with an areal
mass loading of 2 mg cm2 (based on LVS) demonstrate good
cycling stability and charging capability. The LVS cathode
operating at a low current density of 0.15 mA cm2 exhibits
an initial capacity of 1.2 mA h cm2 on charge and an areal
capacity of 1.25 mA h cm2 (Fig. 3A) in subsequent cycles with a
low polarization of 0.4 V (measured at the half-way point on the
charge–discharge profile). The Coulombic efficiency is close to
100%, corresponding to a material utilization of 83%. This
electrochemical behavior is quite different from Li2S cathodes
operating in liquid and polymer electrolytes that generally
suffer from a high initial activation barrier, resulting in high
cell polarization and low initial capacity.29,31,37 Notably, Li–S
cells fabricated with carbon black (Vulcan XC-72) and Li2S as a
physical composite – operating under identical conditions –
only exhibit B30% Li2S utilization (0.45 mA h cm2), accom-
panied by a two-fold higher polarization of 0.8 V (Fig. 3B). In
comparison, Li2S utilization in the LVS cathode is fairly high,
namely 77% (Li2S Utilization Calculation, ESI†).
The rate capability was evaluated by cycling the cell at
diﬀerent current densities (Fig. 3C). At a high current density
of 3 mA cm2, the cell still delivers 50% material utilization
(0.75 mA h cm2). Such a modest drop in capacity (30%) with a
15-fold increase in current density (0.2 mA cm2 vs. 3 mA cm2)
can be attributed to the intimate contact between LiVS2 and
Li2S that shortens the Li+/e transport pathway. Employing Li2S
as the starting active material should lower the chance of
cathode deterioration from mechanical stress due to volume
expansion, because Li2S shrinks upon conversion to sulfur on
charge. This suggestion is supported by the cell capacity
recovery to B1.1 mA h cm2 when the current density reverts
to 0.2 mA cm2 after cycling the cell at 3 mA cm2 for 14 cycles
(Fig. 3D), further highlighting the ability of the LVS cathode to
withstand high-rate cycling. At a moderate current density of
1 mA cm2, excellent long-term cycling performance of the
LVS cathode is attained at a high initial areal capacity of
0.95 mA h cm2 (Fig. 3E). The cell experiences some polariza-
tion during deep cycling (Fig. S5, ESI†), but a very low decay rate
of 0.02% per cycle is achieved nonetheless (Fig. 3E). This is the
lowest reported in all-solid-state Li–S batteries to the best of our
knowledge.

key property of such a mediator, aside from exhibiting excellent
electrochemical redox reversibility, is that its redox potential
should be close to that of the active material34 (Li2S) – a
property exhibited by VS2. Importantly, TiS2 – another common
sulfide-based host material for Li2S cathodes – is not able to
engage in redox mediation synergy with Li2S because of its
lower potential relative to that of Li2S.51 Instead, it may aid Li2S
oxidation by lowering the cathode charge-transfer resistance.31
As these processes – co-oxidation of Li2S and LiVS2 and
chemical oxidation of Li2S by the newly formed VS2 – continue,
there will be an accumulation of sulfur that sits between the
VS2 and Li2S interface that eventually restricts VS2 from acces-
sing the inner core Li2S materials. This could account for the
larger cell polarization observed at the latter stage of charge
(Z2.5 V) because of poor mass-transport within the cathode. In
the subsequent discharge process, the cell polarization consistently
remains at B70 mV. Furthermore, a two-plateau-like feature is not
evident in Fig. 6B, in accord with our previous report showing that
VS2 and S engage in a simultaneous conversion/lithiation process
on discharge.25

Conclusions
Solid state batteries based on Li2S present advantages over
those based on sulfur because the lithiated cathode is at
maximum volume, and it also has the potential to be coupled
with anode-less designs to further boost energy density. Our
nanostructured cathode architecture for an all-inorganic-solid
state Li2S battery accommodates volume contraction on charge,
and reduces the sluggish kinetics involved in conversion of Li2S
to sulfur by adopting multifunctional metallic LiVS2 as a host
material. The hLi2S core – LiVS2 shelli design enables LixVS2 to
