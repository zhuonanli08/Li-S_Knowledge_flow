Unraveling Asymmetric Macroscopic Reaction Dynamics in
Solid-State Li–S Batteries During Charge–Discharge Cycles:
Visualizing Ionic Transport Limitations with Operando
X-Ray Computed Tomography
Yuta Kimura,* Mai Tanaka, Shiori Kawasaki, Shoma Yanagihara, Jan Huebner,
Nozomu Ishiguro, Takashi Nakamura, Oki Sekizawa, Kiyofumi Nitta,
Yoshiharu Uchimoto, Koji Amezawa, and Saneyuki Ohno*
By eliminating liquid electrolytes, solid-state Li–S batteries (SSLSBs) deliver
emerging high-energy storage solutions with intrinsic safety advantages.
However, restricted access to in-battery reaction dynamics has obscured the
mechanisms behind critical limitations in rate capability and cyclability. Here,
operando X-ray computed tomography is employed to capture the evolving
through-thickness sulfur-lithiation distribution within composite SSLSB cath-
odes during cycling, directly linking macroscopic ion transport to cell perfor-
mance. The observations not only demonstrate that macroscopic ion transport
severely restricts the rate capability of SSLSBs, but also disclose a previously
unreported charge–discharge asymmetry in reaction heterogeneity that triggers
irreversible Li2S formation. Diﬀerential-evolution ﬁtting of the reaction proﬁles
quantiﬁed a deterioration in the eﬀective ionic conductivity during charging,
which drives the observed asymmetry and likely arises from electrochemical
and/or microstructural degradation of the electrolyte. This study establishes a
methodology for high-resolution visualization of reaction dynamics within com-
posite cathodes, providing quantitative insights into the macroscopic transport
bottleneck based on experimental observations, which are essential for guiding
rational electrode design toward enhanced rate capability and cyclability.

To address these inherent challenges arising from insulat-
ing nature of active materials, the cathode of SSLSBs neces-
sitates uniform nanoscale compositing of sulfur, SE, and con-
ductive additives, which ensures extensive triple-phase bound-
aries within the cathode and shortens the diﬀusion length in
the sulfur active material.[5] However, such nano-structuring si-
multaneously render ionic and electronic conduction within the
cathode highly ineﬃcient. The resulting complex and tortuous
transport pathways can reduce the eﬀective ionic conductivity in
composite cathodes by two to three orders of magnitude com-
pared to the intrinsic conductivities of the SEs.[20,21] Further-
more, the resulting high interfacial area density can acceler-
ate electrolyte degradation,[22] exacerbating the inherently slow
macroscopic ion transport within the composite. In some cases,
this electrolyte degradation has been intentionally exploited to
form redox-active interphases, enabling stable cycling with capac-
ities exceeding 500 mAh gcathode
−1.[22,23] While the electrochem-
ical measurements suggest the macroscopic ion transport is a
critical performance bottleneck of the SSLSB, these measure-
ments cannot unambiguously distinguish whether the observed
performance limitations stem from macroscopic ionic conduc-
tion within the composite or from microscopic diﬀusion within
the sulfur particles themselves. This distinction has become in-
creasingly important for mechanistic understanding in SSLSBs,
and also across various cathode chemistries including transition
metal-based systems, particularly in the design of high-loading
electrodes or cells operated at high current densities, where the
demands on mass transport are especially severe.[24–26]
Recently, Bradbury et al. have provided the ﬁrst direct evidence
that the macroscopic mass transport limitation poses indeed a se-
rious challenge in SSLSBs.[27] Using operando neutron imaging,
they directly visualized the reaction front advancing from the SE
separator to the current collector side within the electrode, clearly
demonstrating that the electrochemical reactions in the cathode
were governed by limited macroscopic ion transport. Further-
more, their tomographic analysis revealed irreversible lithium ac-
cumulation near the current collector side, suggesting the impact
of macroscopic transport limitations on capacity loss. However,
these observations were restricted to exceptionally thick (≈200
μm) cathodes due to the limited spatial resolution of neutron
imaging and thus cannot be generalized to more practical cell
conﬁgurations with better cyclability or high-rate performance.
Consequently, despite growing recognition that macroscopic ion
transport is a central limitation in SSLSBs,[21] it remains unclear
how signiﬁcantly it impacts cells with improved reversibility or
under various (dis)charging rates.
To provide deeper insights into these critical challenges, here
we conducted operando observations of macroscopic reaction
heterogeneities within composite SSLSB cathodes using syn-
chrotron X-ray computed tomography (XCT). While XCT has
been widely employed to observe morphological changes within
batteries,[28–31] its application for chemical analysis has been
limited,[32] particularly in the context of macroscopic chemical
change within electrodes. This study demonstrates for the ﬁrst
time that XCT, when coupled with high-ﬂux synchrotron beams
and optimized cell design, can eﬀectively evaluate spatial state-of-
charge distribution within well-cyclable cathodes under various
(dis)charging rates with micron-to-submicron spatial resolution.
This approach not only provided direct evidence that macroscopic
ion transport severely restricts (dis)charge capacities at high rates
but also uncovered that reaction heterogeneities within the elec-
trode exhibit asymmetric patterns between charging and dis-
charging, demonstrating that macroscopic ionic transport deteri-
orates during charging. Furthermore, the high-resolution quan-
titative visualization capability of XCT enables us to extract key
transport parameters, eﬀective ionic conductivity and interfacial
charge-transfer kinetics, by ﬁtting the experimentally obtained
state-of-charge proﬁles using a diﬀerential evolution algorithm.
This quantitative analysis revealed a threefold decrease in ef-
fective ionic conductivity within the composite cathode during
charging, oﬀering new mechanistic insights into irreversible Li2S
accumulation within the electrode under realistic cell conﬁgura-
tion and cycling parameters.
2. Results and Discussion
To visualize reaction heterogeneities within composite SSLSB
cathodes using XCT, a cylindrical sample holder with a PEEK
housing was developed, as shown in Figure 1a. Within this cus-
tom holder, a cylindrical SSLSB cell (Figure 1b) was fabricated
by uniaxial pressing (220 MPa[33]) of S/C/Li6PS5Cl (LPSC) com-
posite powder and LPSC powder, followed by the incorporation of
In/Li foil. The nominal sulfur loading in the cathode was set at 5.1
mg cm−2, with volume fractions of sulfur, LPSC, and conductive
additive ﬁxed at ≈0.33:0.53:0.15 (2:3:1 by mass), a ratio previously
reported to exhibit favorable performance.[20] To maintain an in-
ert environment during measurements, the cell was sandwiched
between two 𝜑1.5 mm stainless steel punches with O-ring seals,
which not only prevented exposure to air and moisture but also
served as current collectors. Additionally, a constant constraint
pressure of ≈60 MPa was applied to the cell during cycling by ap-
plying torque to the holder bolt. This integrated cell-holder design
has been conﬁrmed to enable reversible cycling, demonstrating
stable electrochemical performance (Figure S1, Supporting In-
formation). Prior to XCT measurements, the cell underwent two
discharge–charge cycles at 0.1C. It should be noted that the com-
posite cathode layer is placed at the bottom of the cell, as depicted
in Figure 1b, to mitigate the inﬂuence of the volume change of
the anode side on the reconstruction of the 3D image.
Monochromatic XCT at 18 keV eﬀectively distinguished in-
dividual cell components (Figure 1c), with brighter regions in-
dicating higher X-ray absorption. Figure 1d illustrates a cross-
sectional view near the interface between the LPSC separator
layer and the composite cathode layer. The composite cathode
layer exhibits a uniform, dense morphology without visible voids.
In contrast, inhomogeneous densiﬁcation is observed in the sep-
arator layer, as shown in Figure 1e, where electrolytes in the cen-
tral area appear more densely packed than those in the peripheral
regions. This inhomogeneity is commonly encountered in uni-
axially compacted powders. However, since ion transport within
the separator layer is expected to be signiﬁcantly faster accord-
ing to previous studies,[21] the impact of inhomogeneously den-
siﬁed separator on the cathode composite behavior is anticipated
to be negligible. Conversely, the non-uniform thickness and the
observed bending of the composite cathode layer (Figure 1d)
require careful consideration, as a simple cross-sectional slice

of discharge, indicating that the discharge capacity was limited
by these reaction heterogeneities in the cathode. Indeed, as illus-
trated in Figure 4c, the discharge capacity signiﬁcantly decreased
with increasing discharge rate.
To assess the impact of the reaction heterogeneity on discharge
capacity, the discharge capacity of the cell at each rate was com-
pared with those estimated from the integrated value of the Δ𝜃
proﬁle after discharge (Figure 4d). The discharge capacity esti-

monotonically decreased at higher rates. As shown in Figure 4d,

normalized capacities obtained from both X-ray absorbance and
electrochemical measurements were in good agreement, indi-
cating that the discharge capacity degradation is closely linked
to reaction heterogeneity arising from limited macroscopic ionic
transport.

scopic ion transport within the composite cathodes. To quan-
titatively demonstrate the impact of macroscopic ion transport
limitations during charging, we back-calculated the eﬀective
ionic conductivity (𝜎eﬀ,ion) within the composite cathode during

lating (dis)charge reactions in composite cathodes and searched
for the 𝜎eﬀ,ion value that best reproduced both the experimen-
tally obtained Δ𝜃proﬁles and the (dis)charge capacity. (Figure

area per unit volume (a) and exchange current density (j0) —
aj0— and the total resistances external to the composite cathode
(Rtotal), such as the LPSC separator resistance and charge trans-
fer resistance in InLi. In particular, aj0 is also a critical parameter
determining the interfacial charge-transfer kinetics. Therefore,
we simultaneously evaluated changes in this parameter during
(dis)charge.

through DC polarization measurements.[39] The estimated 𝜎eﬀ,ion
for charge was 1.8 × 10−2 mS cm−1, dropping to approximately
one-third of its discharge counterpart. The simultaneously esti-
mated aj0 and Rtotal from the charging data were 0.12 mA cm−3
and 9.9×103 Ω, respectively, showing a decrease in aj0 and an in-
crease in Rtotal relative to the discharge values (0.89 mA cm−3 and
4.6×103 Ω). Thus, our parameter estimation indicates that both
𝜎eﬀ,ion and aj0 decreased during charging, suggesting deteriora-
tion of both macroscopic ionic transport and interfacial reaction
kinetics. Among them, the principal driver of the exacerbated re-
action heterogeneity during charging is the degradation of 𝜎eﬀ,ion.
Indeed, the decrease in aj0 alone cannot replicate this asymmetric
reaction pattern during (dis)charge cycles (Figure S5, Supporting
Information). Thus, our analysis indicates that the diminished
𝜎eﬀ,ion during charging critically impacts the irreversible Li2S
formation.
A primary candidate responsible for the decrease in 𝜎eﬀ,ion dur-
ing charging is the degradation of SE at high potentials. Previous
studies have demonstrated that electrochemical decomposition
of LPSC occurs above its narrow oxidative potential window limit
(2.01 V vs. Li+/Li).[38] We infer that the resultant low ionic conduc-
tivity phases, including various Li-thiophosphates glasses con-
taining PS4
3−, P2S7
4−, and P2S6
4−units, restricted macroscopic
ionic transport during charging, as illustrated in Figure 5c. On
the other hand, these degradation products are known to un-
dergo reversible reduction at low potentials, thereby recovering
the 𝜎eﬀ,ion during discharge.[21] Such reversible redox activity of
the degradation products and the associated cyclic changes in
𝜎eﬀ,ion likely causes the formation of asymmetric reaction het-
erogeneity. Additionally, the volume contraction of sulfur during
charge may cause not only a reduction in triple phase boundaries
but also partial disruption of ionic conduction pathways, poten-
tially contributing to the decreased 𝜎eﬀ,ion during charging. Thus,
our research suggests that the sluggish macroscopic ion trans-
port is not only a standalone issue but is also linked to the electro-
chemical and microstructural stability of the SE, causing critical
problems for SSLSB performance. In other words, the change in
the 𝜎eﬀ,ion is a consequence of multi-fold factor, making it to be a
universal descriptor for further development.
3. Conclusion
This study demonstrates the utility of XCT for observing macro-
scopic reaction heterogeneities within composite battery elec-
trodes. Through the development of an experimental setup en-
abling operando synchrotron XCT measurements, we have pio-
neered visualizing reaction heterogeneities within realistic com-
posite SSLSB cathodes that exhibited stable cyclability and fa-
vorable rate capability. Our observation provided direct evidence
that macroscopic ion transport within composite cathodes is a
critical limiting factor for the rate capability of SSLSBs. More
importantly, through XCT visualization, we revealed that this
slow macroscopic transport issue is exacerbated during charg-
ing, resulting in asymmetric reaction heterogeneities during
(dis)charge cycles, and consequently promoting the irreversible
Li2S formation. The asymmetric macroscopic ion transport lim-
itation appears to be closely related to the electrochemical redox
of the SE and/or chemo-mechanical eﬀects, thus necessitating
integrated electrode designs that simultaneously ensure eﬃcient
ionic transport as well as electrochemical and microstructural sta-
bility throughout the electrode.
