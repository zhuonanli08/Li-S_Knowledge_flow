A B S T R A C T
The energy storage and automotive sectors are channeling substantial investments into advanced all-solid-state 
battery systems, driven by the urgent need to overcome the pervasive safety concerns and energy density con­
straints of conventional liquid-electrolyte lithium-ion counterpart. Among emerging alternatives, all-solid-state 
lithium-sulfur batteries have emerged as a transformative candidate, leveraging sulfur-rich cathodes, lithium 
metal anodes with ultrahigh capacity, and inherently non-flammable solid electrolytes. However, significant 
scientific and engineering hurdles persist, including sluggish sulfur redox kinetics, lithium dendrite formation, 
interfacial instability between components, and manufacturing scalability issues. Translating fundamental 
breakthroughs in material science into commercially viable solutions requires coordinated efforts bridging atom- 
scale mechanistic studies and industry-oriented process optimization. This review systematically analyzes the 
intrinsic electrochemical challenges of all-solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries while evaluating cutting-edge 
operando characterization methodologies and innovative electrode–electrolyte architectures. We critically 
assess the rationale for transitioning from conventional systems to all-solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries, eluci­
date the electrochemical mechanisms governing their operation, and propose multidisciplinary strategies 
spanning materials engineering to architectural innovation. Additionally, we synthesize current research tra­
jectories and investment patterns across academic and industrial sectors, highlighting synergistic collaborations 
accelerating technological maturation. Building upon these insights, this work aims to chart a roadmap for 
transitioning all-solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries from laboratory prototypes to industrial-scale applications, 
ultimately enabling their integration into next-generation energy storage.
1. Introduction
The escalating global climate crisis underscores the urgent need to 
reduce carbon dioxide emissions by transitioning from internal com­
bustion engines to electric vehicles. This paradigm shift has intensified 
the demand for cutting-edge battery technologies capable of powering 
automotive and aerospace applications [1–3]. Consequently, there is 
growing concern about the energy density, safety, and supply chain 
vulnerabilities associated with conventional batteries, underscoring the 
need for innovative battery solutions that significantly enhance energy 
density while maintaining cost-effectiveness [4,5]. Among various 
emerging technologies, lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries, which utilize 
sulfur as the cathode material and lithium metal as the anode material, 
have emerged as a leading contender in the post-lithium-ion battery 
landscape [6–9]. Li-S batteries are notable for their high theoretical 
energy capacities, with sulfur achieving 1675 mAh g−1 and lithium 
reaching 3860 mAh g−1. This enables Li-S batteries to achieve energy 
densities of up to 600 Wh kg−1 (Fig. 1) [10,11]. Moreover, due to the 
abundant supply and low cost of elemental sulfur, Li-S batteries are 
becoming increasingly attractive in the post-lithium-ion battery era, 
with estimated minimum costs potentially as low as $36 kWh−1 [12].
Recent advancements in Li-S battery technology have concentrated 
on several critical domains, including the engineering of cathode ar­
chitectures, the formulation of electrolyte additives, and the mitigation 
of inherent challenges associated with these systems [12–15]. A primary 
challenge is the intrinsically low ionic and electronic conductivity of 
sulfur. Furthermore, the dissolution and shuttling behavior of lithium 
polysulfides (LiPSs), as well as the volumetric expansion and contraction 
of sulfur during cycling, exacerbate the complexity of Li-S battery 
operation. Compounding these issues is the formation of lithium metal 
dendrites, which presents significant safety and performance concerns.

In traditional liquid and polymer electrolyte Li-S batteries, the electro­
chemical reduction of sulfur follows the reaction S + 2Li + 2e−→ Li2S, 
characterized by a multiphase transformation involving solid, liquid, 
and re-solidified states (Fig. 2a) [18]. This is evident in the discharge 
voltage profile, which typically features two distinct voltage plateaus. 
During this process, intermediate LiPSs (Li2Sx, where 6 ≤x < 8) are 
generated, which dissolve in the electrolyte before being further reduced 
to solid-state Li2S. By contrast, Li-S batteries employing inorganic solid- 
state electrolytes undergo a single-phase solid-state transformation, 
wherein sulfur is directly reduced to Li2S without the dissolution of in­
termediate LiPSs, resulting in a discharge curve with only a single 
voltage plateau (Fig. 2b) [19,20]. Solid-state electrolytes offer multiple 
advantages, such as mitigating the issues associated with liquid elec­
trolytes, including the polysulfide shuttle effect and the growth of 
lithium dendrites [21]. Additionally, they enhance safety by eliminating 
the risk of combustion associated with flammable organic solvents used 
in liquid electrolytes [22,23]. These breakthroughs in electrolyte engi­
neering and cathode design, coupled with the transition to all-solid-state 
configurations, represent significant strides toward overcoming the 
challenges of Li-S battery technology and enhancing its potential for 
commercialization.
To address the commercial demand for ultra-high energy density in 
all-solid-state Li-S batteries, researchers have investigated various 
electrode composition parameters to calculate mass energy density. 
Initially, after examining different solid electrolyte parameters to 
compute mass energy density, they assumed 100 % sulfur utilization. We 
employed a range of solid electrolytes, including polymer (polyethylene 
oxide, PEO), oxide (Li7La3Zr2O12, LLZO), sulfide (Li6PS5Cl, LPSCl), and 
halide (Li3InCl6) [24,25]. Among these, PEO-based batteries exhibited 
the highest energy density, followed by those based on LPSCl, Li3InCl6, 
and LLZO. This order is inversely correlated with the density of each 
solid electrolyte: PEO (1.2 g cm−3), LPSCl (1.64 g cm−3), Li3InCl6 (2.59 g 
cm−3), and LLZO (5.1 g cm−3). Under consistent solid electrolyte con­
ditions, increasing sulfur loading leads to enhanced mass energy density. 
Moreover, altering the battery’s N/P ratio reveals that higher ratios 
decrease mass energy density, with optimal density achieved when the 
N/P ratio approaches 1. Similarly, increasing sulfur content-either by 
raising sulfur content at the same loading or increasing loading at the 
same content-boosts mass energy density. Additionally, at a fixed sulfur 
loading and content, higher utilization rates result in greater mass en­
ergy density.
To quantitatively evaluate the structural advantages of all-solid-state 
configurations, we conducted systematic calculations comparing the 
gravimetric energy densities of liquid and all-solid-state Li-S batteries 
using pouch cell architectures, with critical performance parameters 
including sulfur content and cathode loading. Detailed computational 
methodologies and analytical frameworks are comprehensively docu­
mented in Supplementary Table S1. Our findings demonstrate the 
consistent superiority of all-solid-state configurations across all tested 
scenarios. Particularly noteworthy are the achieved energy densities of 
743 Wh kg−1 at 80 wt% sulfur content with 6 mg cm−2 cathode loading, 
escalating to 1100 Wh kg−1 when increasing the loading to 18 mg cm−2. 
These values not only substantially exceed those of liquid-phase coun­
terparts under equivalent conditions but also surpass by over 120 % the 
500 Wh kg−1 benchmarks established by leading global initiatives. The 
exceptional energy density realization stems fundamentally from the 
implementation of bipolar stacking architectures in all-solid-state con­
figurations – a structural optimization prohibited in conventional liquid 
systems due to inherent electrochemical instability. This paradigm- 
shifting design enables unprecedented active material utilization while 
maintaining robust interfacial stability.
The scientific community has witnessed unprecedented global in­
vestment in the development of all-solid-state Li-S battery systems, with 
synergistic collaborations emerging between academic research con­
sortia and industrial partners [26,27]. Despite these concerted efforts, 
formidable technical barriers persist along the path to commercial 
viability, necessitating a rigorous and authoritative synthesis of cutting- 
edge advancements across both fundamental research and applied 
technological domains. This comprehensive review systematically 
evaluates the current technological landscape while critically examining 
persistent challenges, emergent opportunities, and transformative in­
novations within the field. Given the multifaceted challenges inherent in 
this domain −spanning materials synthesis, interfacial phenomena, and 
scalable manufacturing −
interdisciplinary collaboration becomes 
imperative. This requires the concerted efforts of researchers across 
materials science, electrochemistry, chemical engineering, and 
condensed matter physics to address fundamental scientific bottlenecks. 
Moreover, strategic industry-academia partnerships are crucial for 
translating laboratory breakthroughs into industrially-relevant techno­
logical solutions with commercial potential. This analysis specifically 
investigates four critical dimensions: 1) Engineering high-performance 
solid-state electrolytes with optimal ionic conductivity and electro­
chemical stability; 2) Mechanistic understanding of charge-transfer 
processes in all-solid-state Li-S architectures; 3) Advanced structural 
engineering of cathode/anode configurations and electrolyte interfaces; 
4) Resolution of interfacial electro-chemo-mechanical phenomena 
under operational stress conditions. Complementing these scientific 
perspectives, the review integrates industrial insights regarding prac­
tical challenges in solid electrolyte manufacturing and battery proto­
typing, thereby bridging the critical gap between fundamental research 
and commercial implementation. Through this dual academic-industrial 
lens, we aim to establish a roadmap for accelerating the transition from

2. The reaction system of all-solid-state Li-S batteries
The electrochemical performance of all-solid-state Li-S batteries is 
profoundly affected by their discharge products; however, the precise 
composition and characteristics of these products have long been a 
subject of uncertainty (Fig. 3). Initially, it was thought that Li2S was the 
only discharge product. Recent studies, however, have identified a 
complex mixture of unreacted S8, Li2S2, and Li2S as the actual discharge 
products [28]. Further investigations have not only reaffirmed the 
presence of Li2S2 but also shed light on its critical role in influencing 
electrochemical behavior. For example, it has been demonstrated that 
encouraging the formation of Li2S2-dominated discharge products can 
significantly enhance the initial discharge capacity, electrochemical 
reversibility, and cycling stability of these batteries. The observed 
improvement in performance due to Li2S2-rich discharge products can 
be attributed to two primary factors [29]: (1) Li2S2 exhibits a lower 
energy barrier for conversion back to S than Li2S, primarily because of 
its narrower electronic band gap, which enhances electronic conduc­
tivity and thus accelerates charge transfer kinetics within the sulfur 
composite electrode. (2) Li2S2 undergoes less volumetric expan­
sion—approximately 60 % compared to around 80 % for Li2S—thus 
reducing mechanical stress and enhancing the structural integrity of the

Although the introduction of discharge products dominated by Li2S2 
may sacrifice the initial discharge capacity, if this capacity is not fully 
reversible, pursuing a higher initial discharge capacity (such as 
approaching the theoretical capacity of sulfur) could be futile. There­
fore, a deep understanding of the decomposition process of Li2S2/Li2S is 
crucial, as previous studies have shown that their formation hinders the 
electrochemical reversibility of all-solid-state Li-S batteries [30,31]. In 
this regard, in situ transmission electron microscopy (TEM) has been 
used to study the electrochemical decomposition process of Li2S [32], 
revealing that Li+ diffusion is the key factor limiting the reversibility of 
Li2S, and this diffusion limitation can be alleviated by increasing the 
temperature. It is also important to note that sulfides like Li2S2 exist in a 
metastable phase and spontaneously undergo asymmetric decomposi­
tion reactions, converting into Li2S and S. The impact of asymmetric/ 
symmetric decomposition reactions on the electrochemical behavior of 
all-solid-state Li-S batteries remains unclear and requires further 
investigation.
The extent of sulfur utilization hinges on the type of discharge 
product formed, specifically whether it is Li2S or Li2S2. Thus, it is 
essential to deeply understand the activation energy and bandgap of 
solid-state sulfur species under various conditions. This knowledge is 
crucial for developing strategies to facilitate the conversion from sulfur 
to Li2S, thereby enhancing sulfur utilization and subsequently increasing 
the energy density. Adjusting the bandgap of sulfur through doping or 
substitution could offer a viable approach to lower the energy barrier for 
sulfur conversion.
Investigating the charging mechanism of all-solid-state Li-S batteries 
is crucial for understanding their potential failure modes. Despite its 
importance, this area remains significantly underexplored. Ideally, 
during discharge, sulfur would fully convert to Li2S, and during 
charging, Li2S would revert entirely to sulfur, resulting in a theoretically 
perfect conversion efficiency of 100 % during the first cycle [33]. 
However, under practical conditions, the conversion efficiency often 
drops below 90 % [34,35]. This suboptimal performance is attributed to 
the formation of irreversible Li2S and LiPSs after discharge. Further­
more, the charging products are not purely sulfur; they may contain 
high-order LiPSs as well as partially reacted or unreacted low-order 
LiPSs. It remains uncertain whether the sulfur ring can reconstitute it­
self in subsequent cycles, potentially leading to the formation of linear or 
chain-like sulfur structures. Notably, chain-like sulfur could exhibit 
metallic properties [36], suggesting that a deeper understanding of the 
formation processes of charging products might allow for the deliberate 
induction of chain-like sulfur structures to enhance conductivity [37]. 
Additionally, the extent of sulfur recrystallization during the charging 
process in all-solid-state Li-S batteries presents another vital research 
avenue, offering profound insights into the reversibility of the conver­
sion process. In liquid-phase Li-S batteries, the degree of sulfur recrys­
tallization post-charging is highly dependent on the composite cathode’s 
fabrication method [38]. Synthesizing the key research directions con­
cerning the charging products underscores the imperative of further 
exploration in this domain.
During the charging process, various products are formed, some of 
which may undergo volumetric contraction. This phenomenon can lead 
to the loss of contact between the Li+ and electron-conductive compo­
nents, which are crucial for facilitating sulfur redox reactions. Conse­
quently, this detachment can impair the overall performance of the 
battery. Thus, spatial characterization of the distribution and arrange­
ment (e.g., crystalline versus amorphous) of these charging products can 
provide valuable insights into the failure mechanisms of all-solid-state 
Li-S batteries [39]. A comprehensive understanding of these charging 
products is essential; without it, future research efforts may be 
misguided. For example, if the charging products formed after the first 
cycle include sulfur species other than cyclic sulfur, then strategies 
focused on enhancing active material utilization through catalyzing 
sulfur ring opening may be ineffective.
Over the past decade, substantial research efforts have been devoted 
to investigating LiPSs species in liquid Li-S batteries [40–42], with 
enhanced understanding of their existence states and mechanistic roles 
significantly improving battery performance. In stark contrast, despite 
the critical functions of LiPSs in liquid systems, there remains a notable 
paucity of research addressing potential polysulfide formation in all- 
solid-state Li-S batteries. This research gap can be attributed to multi­
ple factors: the chemical similarity between sulfur species complicates 
characterization, while the absence of standardized LiPSs reference 
samples further impedes detection efforts. However, we posit that the 
fundamental cause of this oversight lies in the academic community’s 
widespread presumption regarding the improbability of polysulfide ex­
istence in solid-state systems. Notably, Xiao et al. elucidated sulfur 
cathode reaction mechanisms across three distinct electrolyte systems: 
conventional liquid electrolyte [LiPF6/EC/EMC], concentrated liquid 
electrolyte [LiTFSA/G4 mixed with HFE], and solid-state electrolyte 
(Li3PS4) [43]. Through soft X-ray absorption spectroscopy analysis, they 
investigated sulfur cathode reaction pathways during initial discharge in 
both liquid and solid-state configurations. In conventional electrolytes, 
sulfur cathodes were reduced to long-chain LiPSs (S6
2-) that subsequently 
dissolved in the electrolyte. The concentrated electrolyte system 
demonstrated initial reduction to medium-chain LiPSs (S4
2-), followed by 
sequential conversion to short-chain LiPSs (S2
2-) and Li2S, ultimately 
regenerating long-chain LiPSs (S6
2-). For solid-state electrolytes, sulfur 
cathodes initially formed long-chain LiPSs (S6
2-), followed by progressive 
reduction through intermediate (S4
2-) and short-chain LiPSs (S2
2-) to Li2S. 
These divergent reaction pathways directly govern the electrochemical 
characteristics of respective systems, manifesting in distinct discharge 
voltage profiles. We maintain that systematic investigation into poten­
tial polysulfide existence and its correlation with battery performance 
remains crucial for advancing all-solid-state Li-S technology. Specif­
ically, enhanced understanding of polysulfide evolution mechanisms 
could illuminate solid sulfur morphological transformations during deep 
galvanostatic cycling. Such fundamental understanding would inform 
novel electrode design principles aimed at stabilizing solid–solid in­
terfaces within sulfur composite electrodes, ultimately enabling break­
through improvements in cycling longevity.
3. Characterization of all-solid-state Li-S batteries
To achieve a thorough understanding of intricate electrochemical 
response systems, battery researchers utilize a range of material char­
acterization tools that provide cross-scale resolution (Fig. 4). However, 
the inherent solid-state nature of these systems poses significant chal­
lenges for post-cycling material analysis. Once meticulously con­
structed, the electrode materials become intricately entangled, forming 
a composite that is difficult to separate. This aggregation not only 
complicates the isolation of individual components but also leads to 
cross-contamination, thereby blurring the distinct properties of the 
materials being analyzed. Moreover, the inherent fragility of these 
batteries makes them susceptible to new cracks during disassembly, 
complicating the distinction between cracks that occurred during 
cycling and those that arise from subsequent sample preparation 
processes.
Sulfur experiences a set of phase transitions during the process of 
charge and discharge, yet the complex atomic-scale details of these 
transitions and the resulting structural changes have not been directly 
observed. Consequently, critical aspects such as the distribution, ag­
gregation, and deposition of sulfur species, along with their interfacial 
reactions at the nanoscale, remain unclear. TEM has the potential to aid 
in this regard by providing atomic-scale visualization of sulfur species 
during battery operation. For instance, a recent study on liquid Li-S 
batteries developed an in-situ electrochemical TEM technique to visu­
alize transformations of LiPSs on electrode surfaces at the atomic scale, 
revealing a novel lithium polysulfide interfacial reaction pathway [44].

the native states of highly beam-sensitive materials such as sulfur and its 
related species. One method utilizing cryo-EM involves freezing various 
sulfur species at different electrochemical states and probing their 
chemical properties using electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS). This 
approach may offer novel understandings of the atomic structure, spatial 
distribution, and microstructural evolution of solid-state sulfur during 
cycling [45]. Furthermore, it can elucidate the transformation mecha­
nisms of solid-state sulfur by identifying intermediate sulfur species 
formed during discharge and charge processes. Low-dose imaging could 
offer high-resolution structural information and distinguish different 
phases from the surface to the bulk.
Neutron imaging has emerged as a promising technique for investi­
gating the reaction mechanisms and transport restrictions in all-solid- 
state Li-S batteries [46,47]. Recent research utilized in-situ neutron 
radiography and neutron tomography to visualize lithium dynamics 
within solid-state thick sulfur composite electrodes [48]. The research 
revealed that under these conditions, slow lithium-ion transport is the 
rate-limiting factor. These insights are instrumental in developing sulfur 
composite electrodes that can achieve high loadings and high-rate per­
formance. It is anticipated that future studies will increasingly employ 
neutron imaging to elucidate the formation, growth, and evolution of 
solid-state sulfur, as well as to understand how phase heterogeneities 
impact the overall battery behavior.
Understanding the redox systems under non-equilibrium conditions 
is crucial, as LiPSs such as Li2S6 and Li2S4 exhibit metastability and 
disproportionation reactions during cycling. Employing non-destructive 
in situ and real-time characterization techniques is essential, particu­
larly because LiPSs are sensitive to air and may alter their chemical 
states during sample preparation. X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) 
and Raman spectroscopy are regarded as powerful devices for eluci­
dating the mechanisms of solid-state sulfur reactions. The uniqueness of 
XAS lies in its ability to probe the local electronic structure of specific 
elements, providing key insights into local chemistry, oxidation states, 
and atomic coordination [49]. This capability allows for the clear dif­
ferentiation of LiPSs with similar chemical properties. Raman spec­
troscopy, on the other hand, is highly suitable for exploring the redox 
mechanisms of solid-state sulfur due to sulfur molecules’ distinctive 
vibrational and rotational modes, which can be used to identify specific 
sulfur species [50]. Nevertheless, designing a dedicated real-time bat­
tery that can maintain high pressure and ensure proper sealing from the 
external environment without compromising characterization signals 
remains a significant challenge.
Advanced characterization techniques are useful in enhancing our 
insight into the solid-state sulfur conversion mechanisms. However, a 
deeper comprehension of the dynamic procedures in all-solid-state Li-S 
batteries is still required. For instance, the galvanostatic intermittent 
titration technique (GITT) can quantify lithium-ion diffusivity within 
solid-state sulfur cathodes [51]. Additionally, molecular dynamics 
simulations can reveal the interfacial steadiness and kinetics between 
sulfur and solid electrolyte particles within the composite electrode 
[52–54].
4. The problems of all-solid-state Li-S batteries
4.1. Sluggish sulfur redox kinetics
All-solid-state Li-S batteries encounter challenges analogous to those 
in liquid electrolyte Li-S systems, primarily due to the sluggish sulfur 
redox reactions [55]. These challenges arise from the inherently poor 
lithium-ion and electronic conductivity of both elemental sulfur and 
Li2S. Advanced in situ imaging techniques have been employed to 
elucidate these slow kinetic processes. For instance, operando neutron 
radiography and in situ neutron tomography revealed the distribution of 
lithium within the composite sulfur cathode during cycling [56]
(Fig. 5a). This groundbreaking study successfully implemented 
advanced operando neutron radiography and in-situ neutron tomogra­
phy to unveil critical limitations in lithium-ion transport within solid- 
state sulfur cathode composites. The real-time 2D neutron imaging 
captured a distinct spatial progression of the electrochemical reaction 
front during initial discharge phases, revealing a systematic advance­
ment from the separator-facing side toward the current collector inter­
face. More crucially, three-dimensional tomographic reconstruction 
demonstrated significant lithium retention near the current collector 
region post-recharge, indicating incomplete active material utilization. 
These empirical observations were further corroborated by optimized 
porous electrode theory modeling specifically adapted for solid-state 
systems. The simulations conclusively identified sluggish effective 
lithium-ion diffusivity within the composite matrix as the principal rate- 
limiting mechanism, which not only governs the overall reaction

During the discharge process of Li-S batteries utilizing liquid elec­
trolytes, two distinct voltage plateaus emerge, signifying the 
involvement of a multiphase sulfur redox mechanism. Conversely, the 
discharge curve of Li-S batteries with inorganic solid-state electrolytes 
displays only a single voltage plateau, indicating a fundamentally 
different electrochemical behavior. This divergence implies that Li-S 
batteries with inorganic solid-state electrolytes follow an alternative 
reaction pathway. However, the details of this mechanism remain 
partially understood. Recently, Cao et al. proposed a discharge mecha­
nism involving the formation of metastable Li2S2 during the sulfur-to- 
Li2S conversion process. A notable aspect of this model is the absence of 
LiPSs such as Li2S6, Li2S4, and Li2S2, a conclusion supported by in-situ 
Raman spectroscopy and ex-situ synchrotron X-ray absorption spec­

with sulfide-based electrolytes (Li3PS4), sulfur undergoes a stepwise

similar behavior to those utilizing liquid electrolytes. The formation of

towards the lithium anode, and the resultant degradation of lithium 
metal have been widely investigated. Techniques such as in situ optical 
microscopy (OM), atomic force microscopy (AFM), scanning electron 
microscopy (SEM), and operando ultraviolet–visible spectroscopy 
(UV–vis) have been employed to study these phenomena [59,60].

4.2. Deterioration of solid-state electrolytes
Solid-state Li-S batteries are prone to irreversible chemo-mechanical 
failures primarily due to the degradation of solid electrolytes. During the 
Li-S conversion process, the volumetric expansion and contraction of 
Li2S generate substantial internal stress, adversely affecting surrounding 
solid electrolytes, conductive agents, and current collectors. This, in 
turn, leads to the loosening of particle contacts within the composite 
cathode, forming voids and cracks. Unlike liquid or polymer electro­
lytes, inorganic solid-state electrolytes in Li-S batteries lack a fluid me­
dium, which exacerbates morphological changes and accelerates 
capacity degradation [61–63]. Furthermore, the oxidative and reductive 
degradation of sulfide-based solid electrolytes impairs the battery’s 
electrochemical performance [64]. The decomposition of these sulfide 
electrolytes and the redox reactions of sulfur occur within overlapping 
voltage ranges, as shown in Fig. 6, leading to the generation and 
dissolution of polysulfide phosphates (−P-S, −P-), which may contribute 
to reversible reactions during charge–discharge cycles [65]. In some 
instances, these reversible reactions within the solid electrolyte can 
temporarily augment the overall capacity associated with sulfur’s redox 
processes [66,67]. However, prolonged cycling involving redox re­
actions of solid electrolytes progressively degrades their ionic conduc­
tivity, thereby reducing the cycling efficiency [68]. Common techniques 
for analyzing electrolyte decomposition include X-ray diffraction (XRD), 
X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and Raman spectroscopy, all of 
which help to characterize degradation following cycling. Dynamic 
electrochemical mass spectrometry (DEMS) offers real-time detection of 
gases evolved during battery operation, providing valuable insights into 
degradation mechanisms. [69].

4.3. Lithium dendrite generation
The spread of lithium dendrites remains a persistent difficulty in the 
field of all-solid-state batteries. The spread of lithium dendrites in solid- 
state electrolytes has been captured by different microscopic imaging 
tools, which leads to short-circuit failures [70–72]. By employing 
operando nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy. Liang et al. inves­
tigated the cumulation of dead lithium in sulfide-based solid-state 
electrolytes [73]. In lithium metal batteries utilizing polymer-based 
electrolytes, the penetration of lithium dendrites through the polymer 
electrolyte is attributed to the low mechanical strength of the polymer 
[74]. Although inorganic solid-state electrolyte-based lithium metal 
batteries have high yield strength, they are still prone to dendrite 
penetration. Several principles have been proposed to explain this sit­
uation: one suggests that lithium propagates along defects on the sur­
faces of solid electrolyte particles [75]; because solid-state electrolytes 
have low electronic conductivity, lithium ions reduce to metal; cracks 
formed and propagated during lithium metal deposition provide path­
ways for dendrite propagation [76]. By using in situ X-ray computed 
tomography (XCT), Ning et al. showed that lithium plating first triggers 
cracks, through which lithium dendrites subsequently grow (Fig. 7a) 
[77]. Moreover, they found that the initiation mechanism of lithium 
deposition involves the metal infiltrating sub-surface pores through 
microcracks connecting these pores to the surface. As the pores fill up,

Notably, lower stack pressures help to inhibit the spread, significantly

The interface formed between solid-state electrolytes and lithium 
metal can lead to chemical and mechanical failures. This situation is 
especially obvious if ceramic electrolytes with metallic or semi-metallic 
factors such as titanium, germanium, tin, and silicon, come into contact 
with lithium metal. Under these circumstances, the interface exhibits 
mixed ionic-electronic conductor (MIEC) features and expands as time 
passes. Interface expansion triggers cracks in the materials, resulting in 
increased interfacial impedance [79]. Tippens et al. conducted a 
groundbreaking investigation into the mechanical degradation evolu­
tion of Li1+xAlxGe2-x(PO4)3 (LAGP) solid-state electrolytes during elec­

tomography to track interfacial phase dynamics. Their analysis revealed 
that volumetric expansion at the electrode–electrolyte interface directly 
instigated fracture propagation within LAGP matrices. Crucially, the 
study demonstrated that escalating impedance during cycling predom­
inantly stemmed from crack network development rather than intrinsic 
resistivity of the interfacial phase itself, challenging conventional as­
sumptions about performance degradation mechanisms. The research 
further delineated the fracture nucleation process, identifying prefer­
ential crack initiation near lithium metal/LAGP interfacial peripheries. 
This phenomenon, consistent with finite element simulation outcomes, 
underscores the deterministic role of interfacial mechanical stress dis­
tribution in governing damage inception.
In the delamination process of all-solid-state lithium metal batteries, 
gaps develop between the solid electrolyte and the lithium metal layer. 
These voids occur when the rate of lithium removal exceeds its replen­
ishment rate. With continued cycling, these gaps accumulate, leading to 
heightened local current density. This increase in local current density 
promotes dendrite formation during electrodeposition, ultimately 
resulting in short circuits and battery failure [80,81]. Lewis et al. using 
XCT, identified the formation of voids during lithium delamination in 
sulfide electrolyte symmetric cells (Fig. 7b, c). In the absence of short 
circuits, this loss of contact is considered the primary cause of failure in 
solid-state electrolyte batteries with MIEC interfaces [82]. This study 
pioneers the application of operando synchrotron radiation X-ray 
microtomography to dynamically probe the interfacial evolution be­
tween lithium metal and solid-state electrolytes during battery cycling. 
The tripartite interplay governing battery behavior was mechanistically 
unraveled: (a) void nucleation directly observed during lithium strip­
ping, (b) redox-active interfacial phase proliferation during charging, 
and (c) bulk volumetric fluctuations stemming from partial molar vol­

revealed through symmetrical cell configurations quantified the 
lithium/Li10SnP2S12 electrolyte interfacial degradation, demonstrating

servations by establishing an intrinsic correlation between the redox 
activity of interfacial phases and electrode dimensional changes, while 
emphasizing how localized volumetric incompatibilities catastrophi­
cally compromise macroscopic cell functionality.
4.4. Operation pressure
The operation of all-solid-state batteries is becoming increasingly 
intricate, especially when considering the impacts of external stacking 
stress (Fig. 8). In the case of composite cathodes, significant external 
pressure is necessary during cycling [83]. Throughout charge and 
discharge cycles, active materials undergo repeated expansion and 
contraction, often leading to detachment of electrode particles, creating 
cracks and voids. To ensure close contact and minimize interfacial 
resistance, applying external stacking pressure becomes essential. 
However, excessive pressure on lithium metal anodes can result in un­
intended penetration of lithium metal into the solid electrolyte layer, 
causing short circuits [84]. Conversely, insufficient stacking pressure 
may fail to adequately fill voids after lithium stripping, resulting in 
numerous voids at the interface between the solid electrolyte and 
lithium metal, thereby increasing interfacial resistance [85,86]. There­
fore, applying appropriate stress to establish a robust solid electrolyte/ 
lithium metal interface is critical. However, for battery scalability, 
employing excessive stacking pressure may not be feasible. Thus, 
minimizing stacking pressure while considering technical and cost- 
effectiveness factors is essential [87]. Monitoring operational pressure 
is extensively researched to understand volume changes, associated in­
ternal stress fluctuations, and battery pressure during operation [88]. 
Pressure sensors are commonly employed within battery casings to 
measure these variations.
All-solid-state Li-S batteries offer considerable promise, yet they 
confront significant material challenges involving the design of cath­
odes, anodes, and solid-state electrolyte layers, as well as considerations 
for packaging and operational conditions [89,90]. Achieving battery 
energy densities exceeding 500 Wh kg−1 necessitates targeting an areal 
energy density above 6 mAh cm−2. This requires a high sulfur loading in 
the cathode, exceeding 4 mg/cm2, assuming a 90 % sulfur utilization 
rate. Similar to other all-solid-state batteries, minimizing the thickness 
of the solid-state electrolyte to approximately 30 μm is ideal. Addi­
tionally, optimizing the thickness of the lithium metal anode, aiming for 
an N/P ratio close to 1 (preferably between 1.05 and 1.1), further en­
hances energy density. To meet these goals, optimizing various critical 
parameters is crucial. Key objectives include achieving high capacity 
and maximizing sulfur utilization in high-loading cathodes to ensure 
long cycle life. However, restricted sulfur utilization remains a funda­
mental challenge for all-solid-state Li-S batteries due to the inherently 
low lithium-ion and electronic conductivity of sulfur and Li2S (Table 1). 
In contrast to layered oxide cathode materials, which exhibit relatively 
high ionic and electronic conductivity without requiring carbon addi­
tives in the cathode composite, sulfur cathodes necessitate significant 
carbon content. This introduces processing difficulties and affects elec­
trochemical efficiency. The high surface area of carbon complicates 
cathode layer fabrication using solution processing methods, as carbon 
tends to absorb large amounts of solvent. Moreover, substantial carbon 
additives can degrade the solid-state electrolyte. Therefore, achieving 
high sulfur utilization without compromising manufacturing feasibility 
and cycle life is critical. Another challenge is the significant volume 
expansion and contraction of sulfur particles, which can increase by up 
to 80 % during conversion from elemental sulfur to Li2S. This volumetric 
variation, much greater than in other cathode materials, complicates 
maintaining intimate contact, particularly in all-solid-state configura­
tions where the absence of a liquid phase can lead to complete discon­
nection of ionic pathways and poor interfacial contact. Additionally, the 
formation of lithium dendrites poses a significant challenge to feasi­
bility. Various approaches have been explored to mitigate lithium 
plating and stripping issues in all-solid-state Li-S batteries, including 
protective coatings on lithium metal and enhancing the mechanical 
strength of solid-state electrolytes through doping or incorporating 
insulating compounds, yet an optimal solution remains elusive. Precise 
calibration of cycling temperatures and stack pressures is crucial, 
ensuring adequate pressure during stripping to minimize void

5. The fundamentals of Li-S batteries
5.1. Types of solid electrolyte
Solid-state electrolytes present considerable advantages over 
traditional liquid electrolytes, primarily by mitigating the risk of fire 
[91,92]. These electrolytes can be broadly categorized into polymer 
electrolytes, inorganic electrolytes, and their composites (Table 2). 
Polymer electrolytes are noted for their flexibility, adhesion, and 
excellent processability, facilitating superior solid–solid contact without 
requiring compression during operation. However, challenges such as 
the dissolution and shuttle effect of LiPSs within certain polymer 
matrices, as well as low ionic conductivity especially at high tempera­
tures, persist. Inorganic electrolytes, encompassing sulfides, oxides, and 
halides, typically exhibit higher ionic conductivity. Overcoming the low 
ionic conductivity of sulfur and lithium sulfide is crucial for high- 
performance solid-state electrolytes. To address sulfur’s low electronic 
conductivity, significant amounts of carbon are commonly incorporated 
into the cathode composite materials of all-solid-state Li-S batteries. 
Low-density solid-state electrolytes are preferable for establishing 
continuous ion conduction pathways, although ensuring adequate 
physical contact between particles remains a challenge, necessitating 
high external pressure during manufacturing and battery cycling. In 
some cases, high-temperature sintering of oxide electrolytes becomes 
necessary. One of the primary obstacles hindering widespread adoption 
of solid-state electrolytes is their chemical and electrochemical insta­
bility. While most solid-state electrolytes are compatible with sulfur 
cathodes and maintain electrochemical stability within the operating 
voltage range of sulfur (1.5–2.8 V), certain solid-state reduction re­
actions may still occur. Looking forward, the replacement of liquid 
electrolytes with solid-state alternatives enables the bipolar stacking of 
battery cells, reducing packaging costs and further enhancing energy 
density. This trend augurs well for advancing solid-state electrolyte 
technologies.
(1) Polymers
Research into polymer electrolytes has attracted considerable inter­
est due to their remarkable flexibility and compatibility with electrode 
interfaces. Among these polymers, PEO stands out as the most exten­
sively studied [93,94]. PEO offers a wide electrochemical window of 
0–4.5 V (vs. Li/Li+), making it highly suitable for accommodating both 
lithium metal and sulfur cathodes. Its soft nature facilitates excellent 
solid–solid contact with electrode components, eliminating the need for 
stacking stress during battery cycling. However, PEO suffers from sig­
nificant drawbacks, notably its extremely low room temperature ionic 
conductivity, approximately 0.01 mS cm−1, limiting its application to 
temperatures above 60 ◦C [95,96]. Researchers have addressed this 
challenge by incorporating heteropolymers or creating composite

(2) Composites
Composite electrolytes represent an innovative approach that com­
bines various solid-state electrolytes [101]. Typically composed of 
polymer electrolytes infused with inorganic fillers, these composite 
materials can be categorized into active fillers [102–105], which facil­
itate lithium ion conduction, and inert fillers such as indium oxide, 
aluminum oxide, fullerenes, boron nitride, ceramic nanosheets, and 
metal–organic frameworks [106–109]. Within composite polymer 
electrolytes, lithium ions migrate along polymer chains, across filler- 
polymer interfaces, or through the fillers themselves [110–112]. Com­
posite electrolytes incorporating inert fillers predominantly utilize the 
first two pathways, while those incorporating active fillers leverage the 
inherent lithium-ion conductivity of the active components. Incorpo­
rating fillers into polymer electrolytes offers substantial benefits while 
retaining the advantages of traditional polymer electrolytes, such as 
excellent processability. Firstly, composite electrolytes exhibit enhanced 
ionic conductivity and broader electrochemical stability compared to 
pure polymer electrolytes. Fillers promote ion conduction and mitigate 
undesirable side reactions, thereby enhancing overall efficiency and 
safety. Secondly, these composites significantly bolster mechanical 
strength. Fillers reinforce the polymer matrix, enhancing resistance to 
mechanical stress while preserving the desired flexibility of polymer 
electrolytes.
(3) Sulfide electrolytes
Sulfide electrolytes have gained considerable attention due to their 
remarkable ionic conductivity, which can reach 10 mS cm−1 at room 
temperature [113]. These electrolytes can be categorized into three 
classes based on their crystal structure: glass (e.g., Li2S-P2S5), glass- 
ceramics (e.g., Li3PS4, Li7P3S11), and crystalline solids (e.g., argyrodite- 
type Li6PS5X (LPSX, X = Cl, Br, I, and their combinations), LGPS-type 
Li10GeP2S12) [114–116]. Early research predominantly focused on 
glassy and glass–ceramic electrolytes. By mechanically synthesizing Li2S 
and P2S5, along with various sulfides, oxides, and halides, followed by 
thermal treatment, these studies achieved ionic conductivities of around 
0.1 mS cm−1, which was relatively low [117]. Substantial advancements 
occurred in the late 2000 s and 2010 s with the development of new 
structures and techniques, such as liquid phase synthesis. Argyrodite- 
structured sulfides became popular due to their high ionic conductiv­
ity, cost-effectiveness, and excellent electrochemical stability [118]. 
Among these, LGPS-type sulfides have received significant attention for 
their ultra-high ionic conductivity of up to 25 mS cm−1 [119]. However, 
they are susceptible to reduction instability upon contact with lithium 
metal. Sulfide electrolytes containing metals or metalloids such as Sn, Si, 
and Ge tend to form conductive solid electrolyte interphases at the 
lithium-electrolyte interface, which continue to develop over time. The 
flexibility of sulfide electrolytes enables them to easily establish effec­
tive inter-particle interfaces through cold pressing, thereby simplifying 
the manufacturing process. Nevertheless, they have notable drawbacks: 
sensitivity to moisture and polar solvents, resulting in structural 
degradation and significant reduction in ionic conductivity when 
exposed to humid environments, as well as the release of toxic H2S gas. 
Their narrow electrochemical stability window, typically ranging from 
1.5 to 2.5 V, limits their compatibility with lithium metal, high-voltage 
layered oxides, and sulfur cathodes [120,121]. However, certain sulfide 
electrolytes have demonstrated effective operation within an extended 
electrochemical window of 0 to 4.3 V under specific conditions, showing 
good electrochemical compatibility with high-nickel LiNixCoyMn1-x-yO2 
(NCM) cathodes and lithium metal anodes [122]. This makes sulfide 
electrolytes particularly attractive for use, given their electrochemical 
stability window overlaps with the operating voltage of sulfur cathodes 
(1.5–2.8 V).

stability. Notable oxide crystal structures include NASICON (e.g.

approximately 0.1 mS cm−1 [126]. The inherent mechanical rigidity of 
oxides results in insufficient solid–solid contact and significant grain 
boundary resistance. Consequently, high-temperature sintering or the 
incorporation of liquid/polymer components is necessary to achieve 
effective interfaces in oxide electrolytes. Sulfur sublimates at tempera­

(5) Halide-based electrolytes
Halide-based electrolytes, characterized by the chemical formula 
LiaMXb (with X  = F, Cl, Br, and I) and incorporating various metal el­
ements [131], exhibit remarkable properties. These electrolytes 
demonstrate high ionic conductivity at room temperature (approxi­
mately 1 mS cm−1) and exceptional oxidation stability. The develop­
ment of halide electrolytes traces back to the early 1970 s, initially 
showing low room temperature ionic conductivity. However, the dis­
covery of highly conductive Li3YX6 (with X = Cl, Br) in 2018 marked a 
significant leap forward in all-solid-state battery technology [132]. This 
breakthrough paved the way for a series of halide solid electrolytes, 
including Li3ErCl6, Li3ScCl6, and Li3HoBr6 [133,134]. A primary eco­
nomic concern regarding halide electrolytes is their dependence on rare 
earth metals, posing financial challenges. Consequently, current 
research focuses on developing cost-effective alternatives, such as 
Li2+xZr1−xFexCl6 [135]. Halide electrolytes are typically synthesized via 
ball milling followed by annealing or aqueous synthesis. They offer 
numerous advantages, including high ionic conductivity, flexibility, 
stability at high voltages, and compatibility with cathode materials and 
high-nickel NCM without the need for surface protection coatings [136]. 
However, halide electrolytes have limitations, such as insufficient 
reduction stability and an electrochemical stability window of 0.5–3 V 
[137]. Moreover, the use of halide-based electrolytes necessitates at 
least one additional solid electrolyte to effectively interface with lithium 
metal anodes, thereby limiting their application [138]. Another issue is 
their sensitivity to humidity, though this can be mitigated through 
thermal regeneration [139]. It is crucial to maintain sufficient stack 
pressure during cycling to ensure solid–solid contact. Finally, the soft 
and easily processable nature of halide electrolytes enhances their 
practical applicability.
5.2. Optimization strategy for solid electrolytes
To enhance electrochemical efficiency, improving the ionic con­
ductivity of solid electrolytes is fundamental. This conductivity directly 
impacts sulfur utilization in cathode composites and the transport of 
lithium ions across the solid electrolyte layer. One highly effective 
method to enhance ionic conductivity involves doping strategies, where 
heteroatoms are substituted into the lattice of the inorganic solid elec­
trolyte [140–142]. Introducing foreign ions with varying charges or 
sizes creates vacancies and expands ion transport pathways, facilitating 
the conduction of lithium ions. Incorporating doping agents during 
synthesis helps restrain the formation of low-conductivity polyhedral 
units and promotes the formation of highly conductive phases, thereby 
significantly boosting the ionic conductivity of sulfide electrolytes 
[143]. Xie reported that the doping of Cl- has been shown to significantly 
enhance the conductivity of Li7P2S8I [142]. The ionic conductivity of 
Li7P2S8I increases from 1.53 mS cm−1 to 3.08 mS cm−1 upon Cl- doping, 
with the improvement attributed to the introduction of a disordered S2-/ 
Cl-/I- structure. Additionally, by adjusting the pressing pressure and 
temperature, the Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 material exhibits a remarkable con­
ductivity of 6.67 mS cm−1. When this material is utilized in batteries, the 
assembled Li2S-LiI/Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5/Li-In battery delivers an initial 
discharge capacity of 1051 mAh g−1 at room temperature, 1385.0 mAh 
g−1 at 60 ◦C, and 713.0 mAh g−1 at 0 ◦C, all tested at a current density of 
0.13 mAh cm−2. Moreover, the material exhibits excellent compatibility 
with lithium metal and effectively suppresses dendrite growth. The 
thermally compressed Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5 particles maintain stable lithium 
deposition/stripping for 280 h under a current density of 0.1 mA cm−2. 
When employed in a 3Li2S-LiI/ Li7P2S8I0.5Cl0.5/Li battery, this system 
shows an initial discharge capacity of 474 mAh g−1 at 0.13 mA cm−2, 
with a capacity retention of 83 % after 30 cycles, demonstrating both 
high efficiency and stability over time.
Adjusting the phosphorus-to-sulfur ratio in lithium phosphorus sul­
fur electrolytes further enhances ionic conductivity, thereby improving 
rate performance. Nagata et al. conducted a study on the composite 
solid-state electrolyte (100 −x)(Li1.6PS2)⋅x (LiI), aiming to combine the 
high P/S ratio of solid electrolytes with LiI to provide dual functionality 
of enhanced sulfur activation and high ionic conductivity [144]. The 
composite electrolyte exhibited an ionic conductivity exceeding 0.5 mS 
cm−1 when the molar ratio of LiI (x) was greater than or equal to 35. In 
terms of cathode performance, a cathode material composed of 
65Li1.6PS2⋅35LiI demonstrated an impressive high-rate capacity of over 
1260 mAh g−1 (sulfur) at a current density of 6.4 mA cm−2 (0.8C) under 
25 ◦C conditions. Furthermore, at 45 ◦C, the battery maintained a ca­
pacity of more than 1000 mAh g−1 even at a high current density of 25.5 
mA cm−2 (3.2C). These findings underscore the promising potential of 
the composite solid-state electrolyte in delivering excellent battery 
performance, especially at elevated temperatures.
The use of low-density solid electrolytes ensures ample lithium ion 
pathways in high-sulfur-content cathodes (Fig. 9a). Wang et al. proposed 
a liquid-phase synthesized Li3PS4-2LiBH4 glass–ceramic solid-state 
electrolyte, which exhibits low density (1.491 g cm−3), a small pri­
mary particle size of approximately 500 nm, and a volumetric ionic 
conductivity of 6 mS cm−1 at 25 ◦C [145]. This electrolyte is used in the 
fabrication of all-solid-state Li-S batteries. In Swagelok cell configura­
tion, the battery was tested with a Li-In anode and a 60 wt% sulfur 
cathode, under an average stack pressure of around 55 MPa. The 
resulting all-solid-state battery not only demonstrated long cycling life 
with 77.5 % capacity retention after 800 cycles at 837.5 mA g−1, but also 
retained high discharge capacity of 620 mAh g−1 at 1675 mA g−1 under 
60 ◦C (Fig. 9b, c). Further evidence suggests that the use of low-density 
solid-state electrolytes can enhance the volume ratio of the electrolyte at 
the cathode, reducing the ineffective bulk sulfur (Fig. 9d, e). This 
improvement contributes to the uniformity of the sulfur-based cathode’s 
composition and provides ample ionic conduction pathways, leading to 
enhanced battery performance.
Furthermore, the in situ synthesis of sulfide electrolytes on the active 
material surface enables close-contact electrode assembly [20]. The 
determination of oxidation onset voltages (OOVs) has been reported by 
Hakari for several solid electrolytes, which are compatible with Li2S as a 
high-capacity cathode material. Experimental results indicate that solid 
electrolytes with low OOVs tend to decrease the capacity of corre­
sponding all-solid-state lithium batteries and increase the interfacial 
resistance. To achieve high capacity, it is essential for the OOVs of the 
solid electrolytes to exceed that of Li2S by at least 0.2 V, a requirement 
that is closely linked to the ionic conductivity of the solid electrolyte. In 
order to obtain a high-capacity all-solid-state lithium batteries (500 Wh 
kg−1) with a high content of Li2S, the combination of Li2S cathodes and 
pseudo-binary Li-oxide salts with high OOVs and ionic conductivity is 
proposed as an ideal solution. (Fig. 9f, g) [146].
In polymer-based or composite electrolyte Li-S batteries, extensive 
research has been conducted to address the challenges posed by poly­
sulfide shuttling and lithium dendrite formation. The introduction of 
functional ionic salt additives has proven highly effective in precipi­
tating LiPSs at the cathode, thereby preventing their migration. Addi­
tionally, these additives promote the formation of a favorable solid 
electrolyte interphase (SEI) on the lithium metal anode, effectively 
suppressing dendrite formation [147]. Furthermore, novel anion 
frameworks have been developed to improve chemical compatibility 
with LiPSs during battery cycling. These innovative anion structures can 
readily decompose, contributing to the formation of ion-conductive SEI 
components [148].

Despite its promising theoretical specific capacity, the electrical 
conductivity and ionic conductivity of S are suboptimal, approximately 
5 × 10-30 S cm−1 and 10-13 S cm−1 [154]. Efforts are being made by 
researchers to enhance the electrochemical efficiency of cathode active 
materials in response to this limitation. Composite cathode designs are 
being pursued to tackle this issue, often integrating high concentrations 
of conductive carbon materials and solid-state electrolytes. While this 
approach improves overall conductivity, studies frequently note a 
decrease in sulfur content. Unfortunately, this reduction significantly 
reduces the energy density of all-solid-state Li-S batteries. Over the past 
decade, considerable efforts have been devoted by scholars to enhance 
the performance of sulfur materials in these batteries.
(2) Metal sulfide
Sulfide-based materials, in contrast to metal sulfides, exhibit lower 
conductivity and slower lithium-ion diffusion rates, which render them 
suitable as cathode active materials for all-solid-state Li-S batteries 
[155]. However, due to their higher molecular weight, batteries 
composed entirely of metal sulfides have a lower energy density 
compared to those utilizing elemental sulfur. Researchers are striving to 
strike a balance between energy density and electrochemical efficiency 
by integrating metal sulfides with elemental sulfur [156,157]. Hosseini 
et al. investigated three different copper sulfide-sulfur-carbon (CuS/S/ 
C) composites as cathodes [158]. Their study revealed that the redox 
properties of the composite cathodes were significantly influenced by 
the CuS/C ratio. A CuS/S ratio of 2–1 exhibited an optimal balance, 
delivering a capacity of 1200 mAh g−1 at 20 mA g−1 and 1100 mAh g−1 
at 40 mA g−1. Xu et al. explored intercalation-type metal sulfides as 
promising cathode active materials [159]. They developed a hybrid 
cathode combining conversion-type sulfur with intercalation-type VS, 
resulting in high-efficiency sulfide-based all-solid-state Li-S batteries. 
These batteries demonstrated approximately 85 % sulfur utilization and 
a reversible capacity of 1444 mAh g−1 with an active material loading of 
1.7 mg cm−2. Moreover, at an active material loading of 15.5 mg cm−2, 
the batteries achieved a consistent areal capacity of 7.8 mAh cm−2. Kim 
et al. conducted a comparative analysis of the electrochemical responses 
of SnS materials in solid-state and liquid-state batteries [160]. In solid- 
state batteries, the SnS material retained a capacity of 629 mAh g−1 after 
100 cycles, with only an 8.2 % capacity loss in the first cycle. 
Conversely, in liquid-state batteries, the irreversible capacity loss in the 
first cycle reached 44.6 %.
(3) Organic sulfur
Organic sulfur compounds consist of sulfur atoms bonded covalently 
within an organic structure, predominantly comprising sulfur chains 
and organic components. This structural configuration ensures a ho­
mogeneous distribution of sulfur, preventing aggregation and maxi­
mizing its utilization. Furthermore, the organic matrix serves to mitigate 
volume expansion during charge and discharge cycles [161,162]. Jiang 
et al. developed a high-density composite S-carbon (S/C) cathode using 
a macroporous carbon (MaPC) conductive framework to enhance the 
performance of SPAN@MaPC [163]. Batteries employing approximately 
1 mg cm−2 of sulfur achieved a reversible capacity of 1396.2 mAh g−1 at 
a rate of 0.1C and maintained a capacity of 715.5 mAh g−1 after 200 
cycles. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis revealed peaks 
corresponding to C-Li bonds in the discharge products, indicating the 
presence of irreversible C-Li bonding that contributed to a supercapacity 
exceeding theoretical limits. Huang et al. prepared nano-spherical sul­
fide polyacrylonitrile (NS-SPAN) with a uniform diameter of around 
150 nm. While Li4(BH4)3I as solid-state electrolyte effectively inhibits 
the growth of lithium dendrites and enhances the cycling performance. 
Then, NS-SPAN as the cathode is matched with Li4(BH4)3I solid-state 
electrolyte to assemble the all-solid-state Li-S battery. At a current 
density of 0.2 mAcm−2 at 100 ◦C, the voltage of the Li–Li cell is able to 
maintain stable after 250 h cycling. After 150 cycles at 0.1 C, the Li-S 
battery still has a discharge capacity of 878.5 mAh g−1, with the 
coulombic efficiency of 99 %.
Studies on organic sulfur cathode materials have advanced signifi­
cantly, surpassing sulfurized polyacrylonitrile materials [164]. Wang 
et al. used poly-SCN as a S-rich cathode. The results showed that the 
material has an S loading of > 55 wt% and good stability above 200 ◦C. 
The full cell assembled with LPSCl electrolyte, Li anode, and poly-SCN 
cathode had a specific capacity of ≈800 mAh g−1 at 100 ◦C. [165]. 
Further research utilizing X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) 
confirmed the role of solid-state electrolytes in partially lithiating SAC 
cathodes during the ball-milling process. PTTCA emerged as the pio­
neering organic disulfide cathode [166]. Integration with carbon 
nanotubes and sulfide electrolytes enabled the battery to achieve a 
reversible capacity of 410 mAh g−1, an energy density of 767 Wh kg−1, 
and a capacity retention rate of 83 % after 100 cycles.

5.3.2. Modification of sulfur cathode
The design of the cathode plays a crucial role in enhancing the ef­
ficiency of all-solid-state Li-S batteries. Sulfur and lithium sulfide are 
naturally insulators, both electrically and ionically. Therefore, a signif­
icant quantity of conductive agents, primarily carbon, must be inte­
grated with solid electrolytes and active materials. Ensuring the 
homogeneous blending of these components within the composite 
cathode is essential for maximizing sulfur utilization in redox reactions.
Researchers are currently working towards establishing a highly 
integrated three-phase system comprising sulfur active materials, car­
bon, and solid electrolytes. Their efforts are primarily focused on two 
approaches: reducing the particle sizes of sulfur active materials and 
solid electrolytes, or inducing an amorphous state in sulfur during 
preparation [172,173]. Central to their strategy is the integration of 
sulfur into carbon structures. This approach not only reduces sulfur 
particle sizes and crystallinity but also significantly enhances electronic 
conductivity. A novel technique introduced recently involves the use of 
lithium halides as dopants, replacing conventional solid electrolytes and 
carbon to enhance the ionic conductivity of lithium sulfide. Addition­
ally, MoS2 is incorporated into this framework as an additive to boost 
electronic conductivity [174].
Recent research has investigated the integration of various redox 
mediators into the cathode to amplify sulfur’s redox reactions and 
alleviate polysulfide diffusion. Alongside advancements in material 
properties, diverse composite methodologies have been explored to 
attain superior battery efficiency and increase output. An innovative 
technology has been developed to preserve the disrupted interfaces in 
all-solid-state Li-S batteries during cycling. This breakthrough in­
corporates iodine with sulfur to reduce its melting point, thereby reju­
venating interface integrity. Elevating the temperature to 100 ◦C 
enhances overall efficiency and extends battery lifespan significantly 
[175].
(1) Sulfur-impregnated carbon
Carbon-based materials demonstrate unparalleled advantages in 
advanced battery architectures, primarily rooted in carbon’s intrinsic 
electronic conductivity and robust structural reinforcement capabilities. 
These attributes render carbon an optimal matrix for sulfur composites, 
where synergistic design principles yield transformative performance 
enhancements. By integrating sulfur with multidimensional carbon 
frameworks, the composite simultaneously addresses the poor conduc­
tivity of sulfur cathodes and structural instability during cycling, 
thereby amplifying energy density while prolonging operational lifespan 
through optimized charge transfer kinetics. This technological paradigm 
proves particularly pivotal for all-solid-state Li-S batteries, aligning with 
global demands for high-energy–density storage solutions that harmo­
nize cost-efficiency with environmental sustainability. Contemporary 
material innovation leverages carbon’s dimensional versatility to engi­
neer hierarchical electrode configurations [176–180].
Recent advancements in Li-S battery technology demonstrate 
remarkable progress through innovative nanocomposite engineering. 
Yamamoto’s team pioneered three-dimensional nanoporous graphene 
(NPG)-based composite cathode incorporating Li3PS4 solid electrolyte 
through analogous mechanochemical processing (Fig. 10a) [177]. This 
dual engineering approach establishes robust triphasic interfaces, 
enabling all-solid-state Li-S batteries to deliver 716 mAh g−1 capacity at 
50 % sulfur loading, significantly outperforming conventional S/C 
composites prone to phase segregation. Further innovation manifests in 
a PAN-derived porous carbon fiber (PPCF) core–shell fibers fabricated 
through sequential electrospinning, carbonization, and activation 
(Fig. 10b) [176]. Their hierarchical porosity facilitates sulfur infiltration 
while microporous cores constrain polysulfide shuttling, synergistically 
yielding 1166 mAh g−1 capacity in all-solid-state Li-S batteries through 
optimized electrolyte contact (Fig. 10c). Complementing these struc­
tural strategies, Liu and colleagues developed LLTO/C nanofibers 
establishing dual-conductive interfaces that enhance charge transfer 
kinetics [181]. Though quantitative metrics remain unspecified, this 
interfacial optimization purportedly increases active sulfur utilization 
while reducing operational instabilities during redox cycling. Collec­
tively, these architectural innovations underscore the critical role of 
nanoscale material engineering in overcoming intrinsic limitations of 
sulfur-based battery systems.
Recent advancements in electrode fabrication techniques demon­
strate significant progress in optimizing sulfur-based cathodes for all- 
solid-state Li-S batteries. While conventional methods like high-energy 
ball milling, sulfur melting, and annealing remain prevalent, vapor 
deposition has emerged as a competitive alternative. Notably, Xu et al. 
[182] achieved uniform sulfur particle fixation within expanded 
graphite (EG) through sulfur vapor deposition, where robust S-C bonds 
effectively mitigated volume fluctuations and sulfur loss, substantially 
enhancing cycling stability. Parallel innovations in composite cathode 
design are exemplified by Hao et al. [183], who employed a dual-phase 
approach: initially depositing sulfur in porous Ketjen Black (KB) via 
vapor deposition, followed by ball-milling with LiX (X = F, Cl, Br, I). 
Their results revealed LiI-incorporated all-solid-state batteries delivered 
superior electrochemical performance, attaining a reversible capacity of 
627.1 mAh g−1 at 1C. A groundbreaking departure from traditional 
methodologies is Motoshi et al.’s [184] thermal melt-kneading process, 
which utilizes thermomechanical stress and shear forces in vibrating 
mixers (FLX-60) to fabricate sulfur-conductive agent composites.

(2) Redox mediator
Redox mediators play a crucial role in sulfur conversion reactions by 
reducing the activation energy required. These mediators encompass a 
variety of compounds, including metal sulfides and organic molecules. 
Metal sulfides, such as those identified for use in sulfide-based Li-S 
batteries [187,188], are particularly noteworthy. These compounds not 
only exhibit compatibility with sulfur and sulfide electrolytes but also 
augment the batteries’ reversible capacity. In contrast to sulfur and Li2S,

facilitating the management of sulfur’s significant volume changes 
during cycling. For example, VS2, when employed as a mediator, boasts 
a redox potential slightly higher than that of the active material Li2S, 
thereby enabling its active participation in sulfur redox reactions 
(Fig. 11a). Conversely, the use of another commonly researched metal 
sulfide, TiS2, as a mediator proves impractical due to its lower redox 
potential compared to Li2S [189].

In electrodes, adhesives play a crucial role as an important bridge 
between active components and conductive agents, serving as an 
intermediary to maintain the structural stability of the electrode mate­
rial. For sulfur cathodes, adhesives with high adhesion are essential to 
compensate for the damage caused by the electronic isolation and the 
significant density differences between active S and Li2S. This helps 
address issues like capacity decay and cycle instability. However, con­
ventional adhesives often fall short, as they lack electrochemical activity 
and polarity, which leads to a reduction in the overall energy density of 
the battery. Therefore, there is a need to modify adhesives through 
micro-additives or develop new types of adhesives [196,197]. Bio-based 
adhesives emerge as an excellent choice for sulfur cathodes due to their 
stronger adhesion and ability to adsorb LiPSs [198]. Their strong 
bonding capabilities make it possible to manufacture high-sulfur-loaded 
electrodes, while their polarity helps reduce the need for additional 
adsorbents. Moreover, bio-based adhesives are environmentally 
friendly, abundant, and renewable, offering great potential for industrial 
applications.
In energy storage systems, the importance of conductive agents 
cannot be overstated, as they play a crucial role in controlling electron 
transfer at the interface and determining the kinetics of sulfur-related 
reactions [199]. A critical factor in sulfur cathodes is the expansion of 
the contact area between sulfur materials and conductive substances, 
which is essential for enhancing the overall performance of the system. 
However, in practical applications, this contact area is often hindered by 
the deposition of Li2S. This leads to the obstruction of further LiPSs 
reduction–oxidation processes and results in an uneven interface. 
Therefore, designing an effective conductive agent is of paramount 
importance. Not only must the conductive agent function as an elec­
tronic conductor, but it should also significantly impact the catalytic 
activity of the electrocatalytic centers through its varied hybridization 
states [200].
The current collector plays a crucial role in the stability of the 
electrode/electrolyte interface in all-solid-state Li-S batteries. As the 
current collector directly supports the entire cathode material, its 
properties significantly influence the overall stability of this interface. In 
all-solid-state Li-S batteries, metallic current collectors, such as 
aluminum and copper foils, can face corrosion issues. This corrosion 
leads to damage of the electrode structure and depletion of active ma­
terials, compromising battery performance. To address these challenges, 
various strategies have been explored, including the use of porous ma­
terials to increase the contact area between the active sulfur and the 
conductive substrate. This approach can enhance the electrochemical 
performance of the battery by providing a larger surface area for in­
teractions [201]. Common current collector materials, such as carbon- 
coated aluminum and copper foils, are often employed, but research 
has also focused on the advantages of free-standing carbon-based ma­
terials. These materials offer superior conductivity, mechanical 
strength, and electrochemical stability, making them ideal candidates 
for all-solid-state Li-S batteries current collectors. Further advancements 
have been made in functionalizing carbon-based current collectors. 
Modifications using organic functional groups and metallic compounds 
enhance the chemical adsorption and catalytic properties of the mate­
rials [202]. For instance, functionalized free-standing carbon materials, 
such as nitrogen-doped carbon foams modified with reduced graphene 
oxide or magnesium oxide, and cobalt disulfide/carbon nanoleaf arrays, 
have shown promising results [203,204]. Additionally, the concept of 
structure-free current collector configurations has gained attention. This 
design enhances the sulfur loading capacity and extends the cycling life 
of LSBs, presenting a promising direction for future battery development 
[205].
In the transition from laboratory experiments to real-world appli­
cations, one of the key challenges is minimizing the use of electro­
chemically inert materials or enhancing their functionality, while 
simultaneously ensuring the stability of the battery structure and elec­
trochemical performance. The role of highly adhesive, polar binders 
becomes crucial in this context. These binders not only bridge the 
electrode components but also effectively adsorb LiPSs, thereby 
providing more active sites for sulfur-related conversion reactions at the 
electrode/electrolyte interface. Additionally, high-quality conductive 
additives, which possess electrocatalytic centers, can reduce the accu­
mulation of LiPSs at the electrode/electrolyte interface, thus improving 
efficiency and performance. The advantage of independent support 
materials lies in their integrated features—large specific surface areas 
coupled with conductive, adsorptive, and catalytic functions. These self-
supporting materials exhibit significant advantages under high sulfur 
loading and low electrolyte conditions, and they hold even greater po­
tential as manufacturing costs decrease.
(4) Fabrication optimization
The ball milling technique is essential in processing composite 
cathodes. Compared to using a mortar, employing a planetary ball mill 
results in more uniform particle distribution. The multi-step ball milling 
process, incorporating varying speeds, significantly increases the con­
tact area between sulfur and solid electrolytes [206]. Iwao et al. [184]
utilized a large-scale hot melt kneading method for sulfur/carbon 
composites. In this method, powders are fed into a twin-screw kneader 
under controlled heat and shear, allowing selective melting of sulfur. 
This process embeds carbon into the sulfur matrix, thereby improving 
electrochemical efficiency.
A common strategy to achieve tightly bonded solid–solid interfaces 
involves solution processing. One approach within this method is the in 
situ synthesis of solid electrolytes during the liquid-phase process. Here, 
solid electrolytes are synthesized from precursors, forming a thin 
coating on the surfaces of active materials and carbon additives 
(Fig. 13a) [207]. Another method pre-synthesizes solid electrolytes 
using a dissolution-precipitation method, wherein the electrolyte is 
dissolved and then precipitated prior to application. A third technique 
dissolves both the solid electrolyte and sulfur in a solution, which per­
meates into a porous carbon structure [208]. Research conducted by 
Kim et al. underscores the critical role of solvent selection in the solution 
processing of sulfur cathodes (Fig. 13b) [209]. They observed that wet 
milling cathode composite materials with weakly polar solvents triggers 
a chemical interaction between elemental sulfur and sulfide solid elec­
trolytes, forming polysulfide intermediates at the interface and thereby 
establishing excellent solid–solid contact.

Lithium metal is widely regarded as the most promising anode ma­
terial for lithium batteries due to its low density (0.59 g cm−3), highly 
negative electrochemical potential (−3.04 V vs. standard hydrogen 
electrode), and exceptionally high theoretical specific capacity [210].

choice for anode applications in lithium batteries. However, its high 
reactivity and the absence of dedicated structural hosts severely limit its 
practical use. Traditionally, the electrochemical stability of electrolytes 
is assessed through the electrochemical stability window. When the 
chemical potential of the lithium anode surpasses the lowest unoccupied 
molecular orbital (LUMO) or the conduction band of solid-state elec­
trolytes, the system becomes thermodynamically unstable, triggering 
spontaneous reactions with the lithium metal. Most electrolytes exhibit 
a stability window between 1.7 to 2.3 V [211]. The interaction between 
lithium and materials containing weak P-S and M−S bonds (where M 
represents Si, Sn, Ge, or Al) leads to the formation of Li2S, Li3P, Li-M 
alloys (M being Si, Sn, Ge, or Al), and LiX compounds (X being Cl, Br, 
or I). These reactions significantly increase polarization and interfacial 
impedance [212]. The lack of a structured host for lithium metal permits 
random deposition on the electrode surface, providing little resistance to 
the volumetric changes of the lithium anode [213]. Surface defects in 
lithium or solid-state electrolytes, such as voids, pores, cracks, and 
protrusions, result in uneven current distribution. Localized lithium 
deposition on these protrusions leads to the “tip effect”, where lithium 
ions accumulate more rapidly due to intensified local electric field 
strength [214]. This phenomenon exacerbates electric field asymmetry, 
accelerating dendritic growth. Solid-state electrolytes with a high 
Young’s modulus are expected to mitigate the growth and penetration of 
lithium dendrites. However, dendrites still form at interfacial defects 
between lithium and the solid-state electrolyte, propagating into pores 
and grain boundaries even at low current densities, ultimately fracturing 
the electrolyte in all-solid-state Li-S batteries [215]. The mechanical and 
electrochemical properties of lithium metal are closely tied to dendrite 
formation, with factors such as the material’s “creep” behavior influ­
encing the deposition morphology of lithium [216].
In the cycling process, lithium-based alloys significantly enhance 
interfacial adhesion and facilitate uniform lithium deposition and 
stripping, serving as an effective protective layer [217]. Despite their 
substantial specific capacity, the application of lithium-based alloys as 
anode materials in all-solid-state Li-S batteries presents certain chal­
lenges. Due to their higher potential (approximately 0.622 V versus Li/ 
Li + ) and superior compatibility with solid electrolytes, lithium-indium 
alloys have been widely employed as anode materials in all-solid-state 
Li-S batteries featuring sulfide-based solid electrolytes [218]. Park and 
colleagues observed that the indium layer effectively suppresses 
dendrite formation and promotes a stable interface with sulfide elec­
trolytes, thereby enabling extended cycling without compromising 
battery integrity [219]. However, Luo et al. reported the emergence of 
lithium-indium dendrites in sulfide electrolytes under higher current 
densities and areal capacities (Fig. 14a) [220]. The formation of these 
dendrites leads to volumetric expansion and diminished interfacial in­
teractions, eventually encapsulating electrolyte particles within the 
lithium-indium dendrites. This encapsulation induces short circuits and 
battery failure after prolonged cycling. Moreover, the high molar mass 
of lithium-indium alloys substantially increases the weight of the anode, 
limiting their feasibility in high-energy–density all-solid-state Li-S bat­
teries (Fig. 14b, c). In contrast, lithium-aluminum alloys exhibit a 
moderate potential range (0.3–0.4 V versus Li/Li + ) and a lower molar 
mass, offering potential advantages in mitigating lithium dendrite for­
mation [221]. This characteristic extends the lifespan of the lithium/ 
solid electrolyte interface, fostering the development of batteries with 
enhanced energy density. Notably, the volumetric expansion of lithium- 
aluminum alloys is significantly lower compared to the substantial 
volumetric changes observed in the lithiation and delithiation processes 
of lithium-silicon alloys (320 %) and lithium-tin alloys (265 %) [222].

5.5. Binder
Polymer binders play a critical role in composite electrodes by 
facilitating strong interparticle adhesion, preserving structural integrity, 
and enhancing the overall durability of the electrode, while also regu­
lating the characteristics of the slurry [237]. Despite their pivotal 
function in Li-S batteries, binders remain a relatively underexplored 
area of research. The prevalent use of carbon as a conductive additive 
presents specific challenges for binder selection. Due to carbon’s high 
surface area, it tends to absorb significant amounts of solvent, making it 
difficult to achieve a homogeneous slurry with optimal viscosity.
To address this challenge, Yuan et al. investigated the application of 
silicone rubber as a thickening agent and binder, integrating it into a 
slurry with n-hexane solvent to regulate viscosity and dispersibility 
[238]. Traditionally, water and N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) are used 
as solvents in liquid battery slurry formulations. However, for all-solid- 
state batteries that utilize chemically unstable solid electrolytes, these 
solvents degrade the ionic conductivity of the electrolyte, necessitating 
the use of low-polarity solvents. The choice of binder is further con­
strained by the fact that most high-adhesion binders are composed of 
polar functional groups, which can significantly influence the mechan­
ical stability of cathode composites. Another obstacle in sulfur cathodes 
arises from the non-polar nature of carbon and sulfur surfaces, making it 
challenging to identify binders with strong adhesion to the electrode. Li 
et al. tackled this issue by plasticizing a polar binder, PEO, incorporating 
it with LGPS powder, and thermocompressing the mixture into a film 
(Fig. 16a, b) [239]. The search for suitable solvent-binder combinations 
is complicated by the chemical instability of sulfide solid electrolytes, 
leading to limited research on wet electrode processing methods. 
However, dry electrode manufacturing techniques, which employ pol­
ytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) as a binder, have garnered increasing 
attention in both academia and industry (Fig. 16c) [240,241]. These 
techniques offer comparable advantages to wet processing in sulfide- 
based all-solid-state batteries by circumventing the challenges 
associated with solvent selection. Additionally, they provide economic 
and environmental benefits. When a powder mixture containing elec­
trode materials and PTFE binder is subjected to shear forces during 
processing, the electrode particles are fibrillated and interconnected by 
the PTFE binder. While PTFE is the dominant binder in most dry elec­
trode applications, recent studies have explored the use of ethyl­
ene–vinyl acetate (EVA) as an alternative [242]. Despite ongoing 
research into various binders for all-solid-state sulfur cathodes, further 
advancements are necessary to optimize both the manufacturing pro­
cesses and the electrochemical performance of these systems. Given the 
significant volumetric expansion and contraction of sulfur during 
charge–discharge cycles, the development of innovative binders with 
high elasticity and strong adhesion will be crucial in improving the 
overall efficiency of all-solid-state Li-S batteries.
6. Practical applications and advance
The industrial-scale production of all-solid-state Li-S batteries pre­
sents formidable challenges, primarily due to the high cost of raw ma­
terials and the intricate manufacturing processes required to produce 
solid electrolytes, cathode composites, and lithium metal anodes. To 
overcome these obstacles, the development of more cost-efficient and 
practical manufacturing techniques is essential. Despite the complexities 
involved, these batteries offer significant advantages, including excep­
tionally high energy density and enhanced safety features. The appeal of 
this technology is especially strong for applications in sectors such as 
defense and aerospace. Hence, continued technological advancements 
are critical for the successful commercialization and widespread adop­
tion of all-solid-state Li-S batteries.
6.1. Solid-state electrolyte production

the industry, the development of solid-state electrolyte manufacturing 
technologies has become a focal point of research. While the production 
of polymer and composite electrolytes follows relatively straightforward 
and advanced methods akin to other commercial polymer engineering 
processes, the fabrication of ceramic electrolytes remains a significant 
challenge, requiring substantial technological advancements.

6.2. All solid-state Li-S cell design and fabrication
The development of scalable technologies for solid-state batteries is 
progressing along a path similar to that of conventional liquid lithium- 
ion batteries. Solid-state batteries can feature two primary cell struc­
tures: a bi-layer cell, where the electrolyte is sandwiched between the 
cathode and anode, or a tri-layer cell with distinct layers for the cathode, 
electrolyte, and anode. The manufacturing process encompasses several 
stages, including electrode and electrolyte layer casting, drying, calen­
daring, laser cutting, stacking, pressing, and packaging (Fig. 18). The 
planar pouch cell design is widely adopted in solid-state battery pro­
duction and accommodates various solid electrolytes [249]. To produce 
polymer electrolyte layers, a range of wet casting techniques are uti­
lized, such as drop casting, doctor blading, spin coating, and spray 
coating. For inorganic solid electrolyte layers, thin-film deposition 
methods like physical vapor deposition (PVD), chemical vapor deposi­
tion (CVD), and ALD are commonly used, particularly for nanoscale 
layers [250–252]. Bulk solid electrolytes and composite electrode layers 
are manufactured using methods like tape casting, solution infiltration, 
hot or cold pressing, extrusion, and 3D printing. Tape casting, in 
particular, is widely employed for producing thin ceramic layers, where 
active material powders are blended with conductive additives, solid 
electrolytes, and binders to create a uniform slurry. This slurry is then 
applied to current collector foils and processed via a roll-to-roll method. 
Precise control over the layer thickness is achieved by adjusting the 
slurry’s viscosity and the height of the doctor blade [253]. Following the 
casting, solvent evaporation and calendaring are employed to densify 
the layers, with additional sintering required for oxide-based thin films 
[254]. The next step involves cutting the fabricated electrode and solid 
electrolyte layers into the desired geometries. Lithium metal anodes are 
typically produced using foil extrusion, melt infiltration, electro­
chemical deposition, or vapor deposition techniques [255]. However, 
producing ultra-thin lithium foils remains challenging due to their high 
adhesiveness and reactivity. The assembly of solid-state batteries re­
quires stacking the cathode, solid electrolyte, and anode layers 
sequentially, followed by additional pressing or heating to ensure 
optimal interfacial contact [256]. The use of all-solid electrolytes en­
ables a bipolar cell structure, where the anode of one cell serves as the 
bipolar cathode for the adjacent cell [257]. This configuration reduces 
both space and packaging demands by minimizing the need for welding 
joints [258]. Finally, the process concludes with welding and connecting 
the current collectors to lead tabs, with each unit cell stacked into 
modules, enclosed in a casing, and sealed [259].

companies to develop large-scale solid-state battery technology. A lot of

technology. For instance, Toyota has partnered with Panasonic, Volks­

7. Conclusion
Over the past decade, all-solid-state Li-S batteries have achieved 
significant advancements, emerging as a promising candidate for next- 
generation energy storage technologies (Table S1). Their notable ad­
vantages include the use of earth-abundant sulfur cathodes and high- 
capacity lithium metal anodes, which offer the potential for exception­
ally high energy densities. The incorporation of non-flammable solid 
electrolytes adds an extra layer of safety, while the cost-effectiveness of 
sulfur—surpassing that of nickel and cobalt-based metal oxides—­
renders these batteries more economical compared to other all-solid- 
state variants. The lightweight nature and high capacity of sulfur 
further enhance the appeal of all-solid-state Li-S batteries in terms of 
gravimetric energy density, making them particularly suitable for 
airborne mobility and energy storage applications. However, their per­
formance in volumetric energy density remains less impressive. The low 
operating voltage associated with sulfur may limit power density but 
could simultaneously enhance safety. Although these batteries may not 
be optimal for all energy storage applications, it is crucial to identify 
niches where their low cost, lightweight nature, high energy density, 
and safety profile can be fully exploited. As the battery market continues 
to expand, particularly in sectors such as electric vehicles, maritime 
transport, and aviation electrification, the importance of such targeted 
applications is increasingly evident. According to benchmarks set by the 
U.S. Advanced Battery Consortium (USABC) and the Advanced Research 
Projects Agency-Energy (ARPA-E), the energy density and cost targets 
for electric vehicle batteries should exceed 700 Wh kg−1 and $100 
kWh−1, respectively, while energy storage systems and aviation goals 
should aim for 1000 Wh kg−1 and $0.3 kWh−1 (Fig. 19). Achieving these 
ambitious targets presents a formidable challenge, highlighting the po­
tential of all-solid-state Li-S batteries. In the short term, the primary 
objective is to attain an energy density of 500 Wh kg−1, which 
necessitates an areal energy density above 6 mAh cm−2, a cathode sulfur 
loading exceeding 4 mg cm−2, and a sulfur utilization rate of 90 %. 
Additionally, the thickness of the solid electrolyte layer must be kept 
below 30 μm, and the N/P ratio should remain under 1.1.
The cutting-edge development of all-solid-state Li-S batteries pre­
dominantly revolves around the use of sulfide-based solid electrolytes. 
These electrolytes not only demonstrate exceptionally high ionic con­
ductivity at ambient conditions but also exhibit outstanding compati­
bility with sulfur and lithium sulfide active materials. Recent research 
has achieved substantial progress in improving the energy density of all- 
solid-state Li-S batteries through novel material design and advanced 
processing techniques. Some studies report an initial areal capacity 
exceeding 5–6 mAh cm−2 and sulfur loadings reaching 4–5 mg cm−2, 
while maintaining 90 % capacity retention over 10 cycles. Despite these 
advancements, several challenges persist in transitioning these tech­
nologies to practical applications. Only a handful of studies have 
demonstrated long-term stable electrochemical performance over hun­
dreds of cycles. Many investigations report insufficient battery perfor­
mance during prolonged cycling. Although some research shows good 
capacity retention, this often comes at the expense of reduced sulfur 
loading, leading to high retention rates over extended cycles but lower 
overall areal capacity. Moreover, numerous studies conduct cycling tests 
at elevated temperatures, typically around 60 ◦C, to enhance lithium-ion 
transport within the cathode and anode. Issues related to increasing 
current density (or C-rate) remain a critical bottleneck in most research. 
Additionally, there is limited exploration of using Li-In alloy as a sub­
stitute for pure lithium, despite its potential to significantly improve 
energy density, making it a promising area for future research. Beyond 
sulfide solid electrolytes, the investigation of alternative materials re­
mains scarce, and significant breakthroughs are yet to be achieved in 
this domain.
To comprehensively address these challenges, this review offers a 
systematic overview of cutting-edge advancements made in laboratories 
to overcome these obstacles. The primary hurdles include: (1) insuffi­
cient sulfur redox reactions, (2) severe polysulfide shuttle effects, (3) 
persistent side reactions in solid electrolytes, (4) the growth of lithium 
metal dendrites, and (5) complexities in large-scale production. Efforts 
have thus focused on enhancing sulfur utilization and reversibility, 
while achieving efficient lithium stripping and plating at high areal 
capacities and under low stack pressures. Innovative cathode designs 
have been proposed, including structural and chemical modifications of 
carbon, the incorporation of redox mediators at the sulfur-carbon-solid 
electrolyte three-phase interface, and advancements in cathode
