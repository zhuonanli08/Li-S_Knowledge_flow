Sulfurized polyacrylonitrile cathodes with
electrochemical and structural tuning for high
capacity all-solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries†

All-solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) possess higher safety, longer lifespan, and elevated energy
density compared to the traditional liquid lithium–sulfur batteries (LLSBs). However, the ion-electron
insulating nature along with the large volume changes of sulfur and Li2S signiﬁcantly hinder the full
exploitation of their high theoretical speciﬁc capacities, especially in ASSLSBs. In this work, dense
composite sulfur carbon (S/C) cathodes featuring sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) supported by
a macroporous carbon (MaPC) conductive matrix (SPAN@MaPC) are prepared to solve the above issues.
ASSLSBs consisting of the SPAN@MaPC-Li6PS5Cl-vapor grown carbon ﬁber (VGCF) composite cathode
are evaluated. With 1 mg cm2 pure sulfur loading in the cathode, the assembled ASSLSBs achieved
a high reversible capacity of 1396.2 mA h g1 under an applied current of 0.1C at room temperature and
still retained 715.5 mA h g1 after 200 cycles. The reason it has achieved such performance is that the
sulfur in SPAN, which is uniformly dispersed at the atomic level and covalently bonded to the
polyacrylonitrile
(PAN)
carbon
skeleton,
has
higher
electrochemical
reactivity.
Further,
the
electrochemical behavior of SPAN@MaPC in the ﬁrst cycle is revealed. Also, it is evidenced that the
irreversible C–Li bonding contributes partially to the super-theoretical capacity.
1.
Introduction
Conventional lithium-ion batteries can't satisfy the increasing
energy density and safety requirements of future energy storage
devices. It is hard to make a signicant improvement with the
electrochemical performance getting closer to the limits of the
chemical nature for materials such as LiCoO2 and graphite.1,2
Alternatively, lithium–sulfur batteries are considered to be one
of the most promising candidates for next-generation lithium
batteries because of the high theoretical specic capacity of
1675 mA h g1 and abundant mineral reserves of the sulfur
element.3–5 However, the volume expansion of the sulfur elec-
trode, the dissolution of polysuldes in the liquid electrolyte,
and the safety problems caused by lithium dendrites have been
impeding the further development of LLSBs.6,7
The high safety, long lifespan, and elevated energy density of
ASSLSBs have become possible by using solid-state electrolytes
instead of liquid electrolytes. Among the various solid electro-
lytes, sulde solid electrolytes, such as Li10GeP2S12,8 Li7P3S11,9
Li6PS5Cl (LPSC),10 etc., have been successfully employed in Li–S
batteries due to their high ionic conductivity (>10  103 S
cm1 at 25 C) and relatively better contact with the sulfur
cathode. Although the shuttle eﬀect (the polysulde interme-
diate produced at the cathode dissolves into the electrolyte,
diﬀuses to the anode and reacts with the metal lithium) and the
limitations of conventional liquid electrolytes do not exist in
ASSLSBs, the practical application of ASSLSBs is still hampered
by the ion-electron insulating nature of sulfur (5  1030 S cm1
at 25 C) and the reduction product Li2S (3.6  107 S cm1 at
25 C) as well as the contact failure arising from the volume
evolution of the cathode.11–13
To improve the electrochemical reaction kinetics of the
sulfur cathode, many researchers have proposed various strat-
egies, including the structural design of the S/C composite
cathode,14 conning sulfur in a porous conducting matrix with
high specic surface area,15 nanosizing active materials,16
coating the sulfur particles with highly ionic conductive pha-
ses,17 and elemental doping into sulfur.18,19 Especially, the SPAN
copolymer has attracted great attention since Wang's group rst
reported it in 2002.20 The short-chain Sx (x ¼ 2–3) molecules or
sulfur atoms that are covalently bonded to the organic carbon

Unlike the conventional sulfur cathode, SPAN is believed to
convert S directly to Li2S in a carbonate-based liquid electrolyte
without the involvement of polysulde Li2Sn thus avoiding the
shuttle eﬀect.22 Nevertheless, in addition to the issue of rapid
degradation of lithium-metal anodes with cycling,23 the new
problem of irreversible parasitic reaction between SPAN and
liquid electrolyte solvents to form Li2SO4 seriously damages the
reversible capacity and cycle life of the battery.24 The application
of SPAN in ASSLSBs can obviate the above problems and achieve
excellent electrochemical performance. For instance, Xie et al.
doped 5 mol% Se in SPAN to prepare the Se0.05S0.95PAN cathode
that provides a reversible capacity of 840 mA h g1 at a current
density of 167.5 mA g1 (0.1C) at room temperature, with a pure
sulfur loading of 1 mg cm2.25 When the Te0.05S0.95PAN sample
is further coated with the Li7P3S11 electrolyte, the Te0.05S0.95-
PAN@Li7P3S11 cathode maintains 890.1 mA h g1 aer 200
cycles with a capacity retention of 74.2% (based on the revers-
ible capacity in the second cycle).26 It can be seen that many
studies are more devoted to improving the electrochemical
performance of ASSLBs. Another issue worth noting is that,
though the structure–property relationship and electrochemical
mechanism of SPAN material are under intense investigation in
LLSBs,27–33 there is still no authoritative result, especially for
ASSLSBs.
Herein, we introduce S into a conductive matrix to obtain
a high-performance SPAN@MaPC composite material. The
evenly dispersed Sx (x ¼ 2–3) or S atoms in the cathode can
enhance the electrochemical reactivity. Meanwhile, the stable
conductive framework can accelerate the charge transport and
transfer as well as mitigating the volume change of the cathode
during cycling. Comparing the electrochemical performance of
SPAN@MaPC in all-solid-state and liquid batteries both with
about 1 mg cm2 of pure sulfur loading, the ASSLSB provides
a higher reversible specic capacity up to 1396.2 mA h g1 than
1377.3 mA h g1 of the liquid one at room temperature. This
ASSLSB retains a specic capacity of 715.5 mA h g1 aer 200
cycles, while that of the LLSB decays to 395.7 mA h g1 aer 180
cycles. Furthermore, when the pure sulfur loading is reduced to
0.6 mg cm2, the ASSLSB can even achieve the highest initial
discharge capacity of 1923.5 mA h g1, with 857.8 mA h g1
retained aer 200 cycles at 0.1C. In addition, we also investi-
gated the electrochemical mechanisms, demonstrated the
evolution of the SAPN@MaPC cathode in the rst cycle, and
conrmed that the irreversible binding of partial C–Li contrib-
utes to the superior theoretical capacity.
2.
Experimental
2.1.
Preparation of SPAN@MaPC and S@MaPC
SPAN@MaPC was prepared following the reported process.24 As
exhibited by Fig. 1a, rstly, macroporous carbon (MaPC) was
synthesized via a nano-casting route. Cassava starch (30 g),
Super-P (0.9 g, Timical), and colloidal SiO2 (70 g, Sigma-Aldrich)
were mixed in deionized water (50 mL). The mixture was
vigorously stirred to form a high-viscosity slurry at 80 C, and
then dried in an oven at 140 C followed by carbonization at
1100 C for 3 h under argon ow. The as-obtained carbon–SiO2
mixture was treated with hydrouoric acid (10 wt%) to remove
the SiO2 to obtain the MaPC. Secondly, polyacrylonitrile (PAN,
Aladdin) and MaPC were then dispersed in N-methyl-2-pyrroli-
done (NMP, Aladdin) with a mass ratio of 2 : 1 to ll the pores of
MaPC with PAN (PAN@MaPC). Following the complete drying
of the above homogeneous dispersion at 80
C, the bulk
PAN@MaPC mixture was nely ground. Finally, the obtained
PAN@MaPC and sulfur source were mixed by ball milling and
then vulcanized at 350 C for 6 hours in an argon atmosphere to
yield the SPAN@MaPC samples. The sulfur source was prepared
by co-heating pure sulfur and p-benzoquinone dioxime (PBD,
Aladdin) at 180 C for 5 hours. PBD was used as a cross-linking
agent to promote the vulcanization of PAN.24 As a control, we
also prepared another S/C composite (S@MaPC) by direct co-
heating treatment of pure sulfur and MaPC at 155 C for 2 h.
2.2.
Synthesis of the Li6PS5Cl solid electrolyte
Li6PS5Cl (LPSC) was synthesized through high-energy ball
milling with a planetary mill and the subsequent solid-phase
calcination method. At rst, raw materials of Li2S (99.9%,
Aladdin), P2S5 (99.9%, Aladdin), and LiCl (99.9%, Aladdin) were
put into a mortar as per the stoichiometric ratio, and ground by
hand to obtain homogeneously mixed powders. The green
precursor powders were then obtained by ball milling the above
mixture in a zirconia ball milling tank at a speed of 200 rpm for
6 hours while zirconia beads with a diameter of 15 mm were
used. Next, the precursors were sintered in a sealed quartz
crucible at 500 C for 8 hours. Finally, the sintered powders were
screened with a 400-mesh sieve, and then the undersize mate-
rials were collected to obtain usable LPSC powders with a rela-
tively uniform particle size distribution. The above operations
were carried out in a glove box lled with Ar gas.

2.4.
Battery assembly
All-solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries were assembled accord-
ing to the structure presented in Fig. 1b to investigate the
electrochemical performance of the composite cathodes. In
particular, the SPAN@MaCP composite cathode (SPAN@MaCP-
ca) consisting of SPAN@MaCP (pure sulfur content of 39.6 wt%
determined by a carbon/sulfur analyzer), conductive agent
vapor-grown carbon ber (VGCF; Showa Denko), and LPSC with
a weight ratio of 3 : 2:5 was obtained by ball milling at 260 rpm
for 5 hours, while the S@MaPC composite cathode (S@MaPC-
ca) consisting of S@MaPC (pure sulfur content of 75.0 wt%
determined by a carbon/sulfur analyzer), conductive agent
carbon nanotubes (CNTs; Aladdin) and LPSC with a weight ratio
of 2 : 3 : 5 was prepared directly by ball milling for 1.5 hours. As
for the solid-state electrolyte (SE), the dense electrolyte pellet
with a thickness of 900 mm was produced by compressing the
LPSC powder (120 mg) in a mold of 10 mm diameter at 640 MPa
for 3 minutes. Aer that, 6.7 mg SPAN@MaCP-ca or 5.5 mg
S@MaPC-ca was evenly dispersed and pressed to one side of the
dense solid electrolyte pellet, while 4 mg lithium foil was
pressed to the other side to form a sandwich structure of
SPAN@MaCP-cajLPSC-SEjLi or S@MaPC-cajLPSC-SEjLi, which
was then packaged in the 2032-coin cell and pressurized for 3
minutes at about 5 MPa to obtain the ASSLSBs. Note that the
anode of ASSLSBs used for the long-cycle testing was the Li–In
layer (Li : In ¼ 1 : 1 in atomic ratio), which was obtained by
pressing the indium foil (66 mg) on the SE pellet followed by
lithium foil (4 mg) pressed on the top of indium. The mass
loading of the pure sulfur in the above cathodes was about 1 mg
cm2. Additionally, other ASSLSBs with pre-sulfurized PAN or
LPSC as active materials were assembled using the above-
described process, respectively, to determine the capacity
contribution of these cathode components. Also, the symmetric
steeljLPSC-SEjsteel, SPAN@MaCP-cajLPSC-SEjSPAN@MaCP-ca,
and LijLPSC-SEjLi cells were assembled for electrochemical
performance tests.
For comparison, a conventional lithium–sulfur cell with 1 M
LiPF6 in EC/DEC/EMC (v/v/v ¼ 1 : 1 : 1) as the electrolyte was
assembled in the 2025-coin cell. The cathode of this cell
consists of SPAN@MaCP, Ketjen Black (ECP, Lion Co., Japan),
and polyvinylidene uoride (PVDF, Arkema Co., France), with
a pure sulfur loading of about 1.1 mg cm2. It is noteworthy that
all the above battery assemblies were executed in a glove box
lled with argon, and electrochemically tested at 25 C.

Results and discussion
The micromorphology of MaPC, PAN@MaPC, SPAN@MaPC,
and ordinary S/C composite (S@MaPC) was observed by scan-
ning electron microscopy (SEM). The MaPC features macro-
pores with a size of about 100 nm (Fig. S1a, ESI†), which are
lled by PAN to form the compact PAN@MaPC (Fig. S1b†). Aer
the vulcanization of PAN@MaPC, as-prepared SPAN@MaPC
appears as compact spherical particles with a size of approxi-
mately 6.5 mm as shown in Fig. 2a. In contrast, the S@MaPC
sample is sparse, and the macropores of the MaPC are still
visible in it (marked by the red circle in Fig. 2b). This illustrates
that direct co-heat treatment does not ll and anchor sulfur well
in the MaPC matrix, which is used to accommodate enough
sulfur
and
provide
an
electron-conducting
framework.
However, through the PAN injection process followed by
vulcanization, relatively dense spherical SPAN@MaPC particles
can be obtained (Fig. 2a), which can provide increased energy
density due to the lling of SPAN in the MaPC and possess
a better electrical conductivity because of the more intimate
contact between SPAN and MaPC. The elemental maps from the
EDX in Fig. 2c and d show that the sulfur is uniformly distrib-
uted in both SPAN@MaPC and S@MaPC. But the signal inten-
sity of the S@MaPC element mapping is relatively weak, which
also indicates that the direct co-heat treatment of sulfur and
MaPC cannot x sulfur well. Additionally, the morphology of
the LPSC as the cathode component and the battery electrolyte
is shown in Fig. S1c–e.† It can be seen that the LPSC particle
with a size of 21.5 mm features secondary spheres composed of
primary grains of 150–300 nm in size. The SPAN@MaCP
composite cathode (SPAN@MaPC-ca) obtained by ball milling
of the SPAN@MaPC, VGCF, and LPSC directly is pressed onto
the electrolyte pellet. The morphology of the pressed pellet
above was revealed by the surface and the cross-sectional SEM
images in Fig. 2e and f. It is observed that the pressed cathode
layer is relatively at on the surface and dense inside, with
a thickness of around 57 mm illustrated by imaging with back-
scattered electron (BSE) in Fig. 2g. And the BSE image (Fig. 2h)
of the surface of the cathode layer indicates that the VGCF
conductive carbon (nanobers), LPSC electrolyte (oﬀ-white),
and SPAN@MaPC (dark gray dots) are homogeneously mixed,
which is further demonstrated by the uniform distribution of C,
N, P, Cl elements shown in Fig. 2i–m.

The chemical states of PAN@MaPC, SPAN@MaPC, and
SPAN@MaPC-ca before cycling were subjected to further char-
acterization by XPS. It has been reported that aer vulcanization
of PAN, the PAN polymerization chain cleaves and forms pyri-
dine ring chains.20,27 As illustrated in Fig. 4a, the N 1s spectra of
PAN@MaPC consist of two partial peaks at 399.30 and 397.96
eV, which can be assigned to pyrrole N (C–NH–C) and pyridine
N (C]N–C), respectively.40 A slight cyclization of PAN@MaPC in
the drying treatment is evidenced by the presence of pyridine N.
The same two distinct peaks are also observed in the N 1s
spectra of SPAN@MaPC and SPAN@MaPC-ca (Fig. 4b and c).
Among them, the signal peak of C]N–C in SPAN@MaPC-ca is
signicantly more intense, in agreement with the Raman
results. This further implies that the ball milling potentiates the
vulcanization and the simultaneous cyclization of the PAN
component in SPAN@MaPC-ca. As for the S 2p spectra of the
SPAN@MaPC (Fig. 4d), the tted envelope curve can be clearly
split into three pairs of S 2p2/3 and S 2p1/3 peaks, where the S
2p2/3 peaks at 162.31 and 164.03 eV may belong to the S–C
bonds and S–S bonds deriving from amorphous S or Sx,
respectively, further supporting the Raman results that the
sulfur is bonded to carbon atoms and/or exists as short chain Sx.
The electrochemical performance of SPAN@MaPC was
evaluated in assembled all-solid-state and conventional liquid
lithium–sulfur batteries. The prepared LPSC was used as the
electrolyte in ASSLSBs. The Li+ conductivity of the LPSC is 3.85
 103 S cm1 measured using the symmetric steeljLPSC-
SEjsteel cell at room temperature (calculation method is
detailed in Fig. S3 of the ESI†). In addition, the specic capacity
of all the cells in this work is obtained according to the mass
loading of pure sulfur in the composite cathode.
Galvanostatic charge–discharge tests were performed on the
SPAN@MaPC-cajLPSC-SEjLi and S@MaPC-cajLPSC-SEjLi cells
at 0.1C. As depicted in Fig. 5a, SPAN@MaPC with initial
discharge capacity up to 1888.2 mA h g1 achieves a reversible
capacity of 1492.1 mA h g1 in the 2nd cycle over 1.0–3.0 V (vs.
Li/Li+) operating voltage. Even discharging to 1.5 V, the capacity
of SPAN@MaPC (975.7 mA h g1) is substantially superior to
that of S@MaPC (694 mA h g1) in the initial cycle (Fig. 5b),
which attests to an obviously advanced performance of
SPAN@MaPC. However, both cathodes suﬀer from a low initial
coulombic eﬃciency of 72.55% for SPAN@MaPC and 75.15%
for S@MaPC. As for S@MaPC, the poor conductivity of bulk
sulfur with inferior physical contact with the conductive matrix
contributes to the less utilization of the active material, in turn
resulting in lower capacity and coulombic eﬃciency. Consid-
ering the enhanced theoretical capacity at the rst discharge of
SPAN@MaPC, the main driver of the low initial coulombic
eﬃciency is the irreversible bonding of Li to the C]N bond in
the SPAN copolymer backbone to form Li–C and Li–N, as re-
ported previously.30,41 In addition, charge/discharge tests were
conducted at an applied current of 0.13 mA cm2 with LPSC
and PAN@MaPC as the cathode active substances. As shown in

has not been established clearly and requires further research.

4.
Conclusions
In summary, a vulcanized polymer composite cathode material
SPAN@MaPC with PAN@MaPC as the conducting framework
was designed and synthesized. The sulfur in this material is
covalently bonded to the conductive carbon skeleton instead of
physically tied and adsorbed. It not only greatly enhances the
electrical conductivity and structural stability of the cathode,
but also greatly improves the utilization of sulfur. Importantly,
the LPSC applied in ASSLSBs eliminates the issue of irreversible
parasitic reactions of the SPAN@MaPC cathode in a carbonate-
based electrolyte, and dramatically boosts the reversible specic
capacity and cycling performance. The assembled ASSLSBs
achieve a high reversible capacity of 1396.2 mA h g1 at room
temperature and still retains 715.5 mA h gs
1 aer 200 cycles. In
addition, the existence of irreversible C–Li bonds during the
rst charge/discharge may be responsible for the enhanced
theoretical specic capacity in the rst cycle. And the dead Li2S
may cause capacity degradation of all-solid-state lithium–sulfur
batteries in subsequent cycles. Further, it is imperative to
optimize the ion-electron conductivity of S/Li2S, increase the
sulfur loading, and stabilize the Li/LPSC interface to advance
the electrochemical performance of ASSLSBs.
