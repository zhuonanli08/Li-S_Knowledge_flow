ABSTRACT
All-solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) are emerging as promising candidates for next-generation energy storage
solutions, owing to their exceptional specific energy, inherent safety, and cost-effectiveness. However, sluggish solid–solid sulfur
redox kinetics hinder their rate performance and cycling longevity, posing a critical challenge for practical implementation. Herein,
we introduce diphenyl diselenide (DPDSe) as a multifunctional additive that simultaneously addresses both the limitations of
sulfur cathodes and interfacial issues in ASSLSBs. DPDSe serves as an efficient redox mediator, reacting with lithium polysulfides
(LiPSs) to generate phenyl selenylated intermediates (LiPhSePSs), which enhance charge transfer kinetics. Furthermore, DPDSe
improves the ionic conductivity of solid polymer electrolytes (SPEs) and reacts with lithium metal to form a robust solid electrolyte
interphase (SEI) enriched with aromatic moieties. This aromatic-rich SEI mitigates electrolyte degradation and suppresses lithium
dendrite formation, ensuring stable interfacial contact during prolonged cycling. Remarkably, ASSLSBs incorporating DPDSe
achieve an exceptional specific capacity of 400 mAh g−1 after 200 cycles at 1 C. This performance marks the highest cycling stability
reported for organic mediator-enhanced solid-state lithium-sulfur systems. This work establishes a novel multifunctional additive
strategy to fully exploit the electrochemical potential of sulfur cathodes in solid-state battery architectures.
1
Introduction
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries have been considered as next-
generation energy storage systems as they have overwhelming
advantages in terms of high energy density (a theoretical
value of 2600 Wh kg−1), abundance of sulfur resources, low
cost, and environmental benignancy [1, 2]. Despite consider-
able advancements, the practical application of Li–S batter-
ies remains constrained by the notorious shuttle effect and
safety concerns related to liquid electrolytes [3, 4]. Solid-
state electrolytes (SSEs) offer a promising solution to suppress
the shuttle effect and improve the safety level by replac-
ing flammable liquid electrolytes. While substantial advance-
ments have been achieved in all-solid-state lithium–sulfur
batteries (ASSLSBs), their ion transport efficiency contin-
ues to encounter critical interfacial barriers arising from the
solid–solid interaction between active materials and SSEs,
resulting in sluggish lithium-ion diffusion dynamics. Worse
still, the extremely poor electrical conductivity of both ele-
mental sulfur (S8) and its discharge product, lithium sul-
fide (Li2S), severely restricts electron transport during battery
operation [5]. While preliminary investigations into ASSLSBs

In this study, based on the principle of redox synergistic
strategy, diphenyl diselenide (DPDSe) is employed as a redox
mediator to accelerate the sulfur redox reaction and enhance
the performance of ASSLSBs (Scheme 1a). Specifically, DPDSe
spontaneously reacts with LiPSs to form phenyl selenylated
lithium polysulfides (LiPhSePSs) [14]. The resulting LiPhSePSs
exhibit improved redox mediation capabilities, which lower
the dissolution energy barrier (Scheme 1b). Additionally, the
small DPDSe molecule acts as a functional additive in SPEs,
reducing the crystallinity of PEO and significantly enhancing
the ionic conductivity and lithium-ion transference number of
SSE (Scheme 1c). Importantly, DPDSe is preferentially reduced
on the surface of the lithium anode, forming lithium phenyl
selenate (PhSeLi) [15]. The aromatic organic components in
the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI), characterized by a pla-
nar backbone structure and π–π interactions, contribute to a
more flexible interphase, thereby promoting stable and efficient
Li deposition and stripping (Scheme 1d) [16]. Consequently,
DPDSe serves as a multifunctional redox mediator, endowing
ASSLSBs with outstanding performance, achieving an ultra-
high theoretical specific capacity of 1441 mAh g−1 at 0.05 C,
and sustaining a theoretical specific capacity of 400 mAh g−1
at 1 C with stable cycling for 200 cycles without capacity
degradation.
2
Results
2.1
Morphological and Structural
Characterizations of SPEs
The PEO-based SPEs were prepared by a doctor blade coating
process and then characterized by various technologies. After
the addition of DPDSe (Figure 1a), the PEO-based SPEs change
from a semi-transparent state in pure PEO-LiTFSI electrolyte
(denoted as PL) to a light yellow state in the PEO-LiTFSI-
DPDSe electrolytes (denoted as PLD), and then turn into a
totally yellow state when the amount of DPDSe is excessive
(Figure 1b,c; Figure S1). Because of the support of the bacterial
cellulose (BC) membranes (Figures S2 and S3), all the SPEs can
maintain a thickness of approximately 35 µm (Figure 1d) and a
smooth surface. As shown in the scanning electron microscopy
(SEM) elemental mapping, the uniform distribution of critical
elements indicates that the DPDSe additive is evenly dispersed
within the SPEs (Figure 1e). Additionally, all samples exhibit no
aggregation of electrolyte components (Figure S4). The crystal
structure of SPEs is investigated via X-ray diffraction (XRD)
spectra and summarized in Figure 1f; Figure S5. After adding
DPDSe, the crystallinity degree of PEO decreases, as evidenced
by a significant reduction in intensity of the characteristic peaks
at 19◦and 23◦, indicating that the addition of DPDSe more
effectively suppresses PEO crystallization [17]. The degree of
crystallinity was further calculated based on the area of the
XRD diffraction peaks. With the increase of DPDSe, the samples
exhibit a significant decrease in crystallinity degree from 7.19 of
PL to 4.09 of PLD-4 (Figure 1g; Figure S6). A lower crystallinity
by DPDSe is beneficial for improving the chain mobility of PEO,
allowing for faster Li+ transfer in the interlayer channels [18].
The thermal properties of SPEs were characterized via thermo-
gravimetric (TG) analysis and differential scanning calorimetry
(DSC) tests. The thermal stability of the SPEs deteriorates with
increasing amounts of DPDSe because of the dissolution ability
of small molecules (Figure 1h; Figure S7). However, even with
an excessive DPDSe in PLD-4, the SPEs can still maintain a
thermal stability up to 200◦C. To investigate the physical state
of SPEs at high temperatures, PL and PLD-3 (the SPEs with a
PEO:LiTFSI:DPDSe weight ratio of 5:2:2) were subjected to var-
ious temperatures for 1 h. Despite the low melting temperature of
the PEO host, the as-prepared SPEs can maintain their structural
integrity at 80◦C because of the presence of the BC membrane.
(Figure S8). These results indicate that the PLD-3 can maintain
its integrity at a high test temperature of 60◦C. The melting
point (Tm) and glass transition temperature (Tg) of SPEs without
BC membrane were further obtained from DSC curves. As
shown in Figure 1i, the incorporation of DPDSe inhibits polymer
crystallization, resulting in a reduction of the melting point from
45.2◦C in PLD-1 to 37.0◦C in PLD-3. Moreover, the Tg of SPEs
shows a similar tendency, decreasing from −44.9◦C to −52.7◦C
when the amount of DPDSe increases. However, excessive DPDSe
can aggregate within the PEO polymer electrolyte, leading to
phase separation by forming DPDSe-enriched and PEO-enriched
phases in PLD-4 and an obvious increase in Tm and Tg. The
thermal analysis results indicate that the PLD-3 sample has the
lowest melting point and glass transition temperature, which

2.2
Electrochemical, Mechanical, and Thermal
Evaluation of SPEs
The electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) is used to
obtain the impedance values of SPEs at different temperatures,
as shown in Figure 2a; Figure S9. The impedance values rapidly
decrease as the temperature increases from 30◦C to 90◦C.
The ion conductivity is calculated from the impedance values
(Figure 2b), revealing that with the increase in the DPDSe ratio,
the ion conductivity of SPEs initially increases and subsequently
decreases. Among these samples, the PLD-3 shows the highest
ionic conductivity of 2.149 × 10−4 S cm−1 at 60◦C (Figure 2c).
However, when the LiTFSI/DPDSe weight ratio rises to 1:2 (PLD-
4), the ion conductivity sharply decreases to 1.741 × 10−4 S cm−1.
This can be attributed to the excess DPDSe, which disrupts the
continuity of the PEO chain in the SPEs, thereby blocking the
ion transfer pathways, in agreement with the conclusions derived
from the previously obtained DSC curve data [21, 22]. PLD-3
exhibits a lower activation energy (Ea = 32.03 kJ mol−1) than PL
(Ea = 37.70 kJ mol−1) in Figure 2d, indicating a reduced barrier
for Li+ migration and more efficient segmental motion, which
collectively facilitate ion transport and thereby enhance ionic
conductivity [23, 24]. To investigate the lithium salt dissociation
in SPEs, the current evolution of the Li symmetric cell at 60◦C
was obtained (Figure 2e,f). The calculated results show that
the lithium-ion transference number of PLD-3 (tLi
+ = 0.45) is
significantly higher than that of PL (tLi
+ = 0.22). As shown
in Figure 2g, the high voltage stability of SPEs decreases with
the addition of DPDSe. The results indicate that the DPDSe
additive accelerates the decomposition of the PEO electrolyte
at high voltages. Considering both the high ionic conductivity
and high voltage stability of the SPEs, PLD-3 is the optimal
electrolyte choice for the ASSLSBs. It should be noted that
the incorporation of BC into the SPEs significantly enhances
their mechanical strength, with the tensile stress far exceeding
that of BC-free PL and PLD-3 (Figure 2h; Figure S10). In
flame tests, PEO-based polymer electrolytes demonstrate superior
flame-retardant performance compared to commercial liquid
electrolytes (CLE), significantly mitigating combustion hazards
(Figure 2i; Figure S11). This difference is mainly attributed to
the electrolyte composition. Commercial Li–S liquid electrolytes
generally contain volatile and flammable ether-based solvents,
which can readily ignite and sustain combustion. By contrast, the
PEO-based polymer electrolyte has much lower volatility and no
free-flowing liquid phase, resulting in reduced flammability and
better safety [25, 26].

2.4
Working Mechanisms of DPDSe on
Enhancing Lithium-Ion Diffusion
A mechanism is proposed to explain the mechanisms of DPDSe
on promoting Li+ transport, as illustrated in Figure 4a,b. In PL,
Li+ migration occurs through coordination and de-coordination
with ether oxygens in the polymer chains. The strong O─Li+
coordination leads to slower Li+ transport, which is unfavorable
for the dissociation of lithium salt [33, 34]. Therefore, adjusting
the solvation structure of Li+ in PL is considered an effective
approach for facilitating rapid Li+ transport, thereby achieving
a stable, low-resistance lithium/electrolyte interface. Although
selenium atoms in DPDSe have high electron negativity, their
electron density is anisotropically distributed whenever cova-
lently bound to a carbon atom. Actually, there is a region of
low electron density (the so-called σ hole) on the elongation
of the covalent bond. The attractive interaction between the
σ-hole in DPDSe and the electron-rich oxygen atom (on the
PEO) is a manifestation of chalcogen bonding [35]. The mutual
attraction between the selenium atom of DPDSe and the oxygen
atom of the PEO chain weakens the strong O─Li+ coordination,
resulting in a “weak solvation” structure that releases coordi-
nated lithium-ion. This process leads to a significant increase
in ion conductivity and lithium-ion transference number. First,
the presence of chalcogen bonding between oxygen atoms and
selenium atoms in PLD-3 is confirmed by X-ray photoelectron
spectroscopy (XPS). As shown in Figure 4c, the oxygen peak
of the ethylene oxide repeat unit in PL is located at 532.60 eV,
whereas the oxygen peak of PLD-3 shifts to a higher binding
energy (532.80 eV), indicating that the electron cloud density
around the oxygen atoms in PLD-3 is lower than that in the PL
electrolyte [36]. In contrast, compared to the selenium doublet

3
Conclusion
In this study, diphenyl diselenide (DPDSe) was investigated
as a redox promoter for all-solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries
(ASSLSBs) to enhance sulfur redox kinetics while addressing
the inherent challenges of low ionic conductivity and lithium-
ion transfer associated with conventional PEO-based electrolytes.
The improved reaction kinetics and lithium-ion diffusion capa-
bilities resulted in enhanced rate performance and high specific
capacity for the ASSLSBs. Additionally, DPDSe facilitated the
formation of a stable solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer
on the lithium metal anode through the generation of lithium
phenyl selenide, thereby promoting efficient lithium deposition
and stripping. The symmetric cell demonstrated stable cycling
for 2000 h at a current density of 0.1 mA cm−2. Furthermore,
the fabricated ASSLSBs achieved an impressive initial discharge
capacity of 1441 mAh g−1 at 0.05 C, maintaining a specific capacity
of 400 mAh g−1 after 200 cycles at 1 C. Even with a higher sulfur
loading of 4.97 mg cm−2, the PLD-3-based cell recorded an initial
capacity of 579.26 mAh g−1 at 0.02 C. We believe this work offers
a promising direction for the development of novel polymer solid
electrolytes for ASSLSBs.
