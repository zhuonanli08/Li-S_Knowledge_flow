ABSTRACT
Sluggish sulfur reaction kinetics present a critical barrier to the practical application of sulfide-electrolyte (SE) based all-solid-
state lithium-sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs). Achieving high performance requires both lowering the intrinsic energy barrier for
sulfur conversion and engineering efficient transport pathways. Herein, we address these challenges by designing atomically
dispersed cobalt sites on carbon nanotubes to directionally catalyze sulfur conversion at the triple phase interface. Strong orbital
hybridization between Co 3d and S 3p states strengthens chemical bonding, effectively accelerating both sulfur reduction and
lithium sulfide oxidation. The interfaces tailored for directional catalysis maximize highly ordered C/S/SE triple-phase interfaces
and minimize SE/C interfaces, establishing hierarchical ionic/electronic transport networks while mitigating side reactions.
Consequently, the engineered cathode delivers a high reversible capacity of 1108 mAh g−1 at 0.5 C, retaining 97 % capacity over 500
cycles. The resulting batteries also demonstrate remarkable robustness under demanding conditions. This work offers a powerful
catalysis-driven strategy for high-performance ASSLSBs.

Introduction
Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries, based on the electrochemical
conversion of sulfur (6Li + S8 →Li2S), are distinguished by a
remarkable theoretical energy density of 2600 Wh kg−1, posi-
tioning them as a leading candidate for next-generation energy
storage systems [1–3]. However, the practical application of Li-S
batteries with conventional liquid electrolytes is severely impeded
by the polysulfide shuttle effect, which leads to poor sulfur
utilization, and by inherent safety risks, collectively limiting
their cycle life [4, 5]. The replacement of liquid electrolytes
with solid-state alternatives, particularly sulfide solid electrolytes
(SEs) featuring high ionic conductivity and favorable mechanical
properties, presents a viable strategy to eliminate the shuttle effect
and enhance battery safety [6–8].
Despite these advantages, the transition to a solid-state archi-
tecture introduces formidable interfacial challenges. In all-
solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs), electrochemical
performance is critically dependent on the formation of stable
and continuous triple-phase boundaries among sulfur, the SE,
and a conductive carbon framework [9, 10]. Conventional cath-
ode fabrication, typically involving mechanical ball-milling of
these components, fails to establish such effective interfaces.
Instead, this method generates a high proportion of inactive two-
phase interfaces (S/C or S/SE) and deleterious SE/C interfaces.

These defects not only reduce sulfur utilization but also trigger
significant SE decomposition [11–14]. Consequently, these inter-
facial deficiencies impose a high energy barrier for solid-state
sulfur conversion and create tortuous charge transport path-
ways, resulting in sluggish reaction kinetics (Figure 1a) [15–17].
Furthermore, upon prolonged cycling, the continuous volume
variation of sulfur, coupled with the chemical degradation of
the SEs, exacerbates the instability of the triple-phase interface,
thereby severely deteriorating the electrochemical performance.
These multifaceted challenges necessitate a multiscale design for
the sulfur cathode to simultaneously achieve rapid sulfur con-
version and efficient lithium-ion transport. While strategically
integrating catalysts is a promising route to lowering the energy
barrier and enhancing charge transfer, the pivotal challenge lies
in engineering effective catalytic sites while sustaining efficient
ion transport across multiple scales [18, 19]. Solid-solid catalysis,
in particular, demands a high density of active sites coupled with
a precisely engineered interface. Meeting these demands requires
a strategy that simultaneously resolves the dual challenges of
catalytic site design and interfacial optimization.
Atomically dispersed catalysts anchored on carbon-based sub-
strates represent a promising solution, a strategy explicitly
designed to maximize atom utilization efficiency and gener-
ate an ultra-high density of exposed active sites [20, 21]. In
this configuration, transition metal-modified carbon matrices
function synergistically as electronic conductors, sulfur hosts,
and catalysts, thereby significantly simplifying the cathode
architecture [22]. Among various transition metals (Fe, Co,
Ni, and Mn) candidates, Cobalt (Co) exhibits an optimized d-
band center position that facilitates a moderate binding strength
with sulfur intermediates, effectively balancing the adsorption–
desorption equilibrium [23–25]. However, conventional strategies
for fabricating such Co/carbon-based atomic catalysts, such as
impregnation or solvothermal synthesis, predominantly rely on
wet-chemical processes. These methods inevitably introduce
polar solvent residues that can induce detrimental reactions with
sensitive SEs. Furthermore, unlocking the full potential of these
catalysts in ASSLSBs necessitates the precise spatial regulation
of catalytic sites to selectively promote sulfur conversion rather
than SE decomposition. Unfortunately, conventional mechanical
mixing fails to achieve the requisite intimate contact between
catalytic sites and sulfur species. Consequently, the synergistic
design of atomic catalysts combined with nano-scale interface
regulation to guarantee the specific binding of catalytic sites with
target sulfur constitutes a pivotal scientific challenge.
Herein, we propose an innovative strategy to engineer homoge-
neous and directional sulfur catalysis by exploiting the matched
low sublimation points of a Co precursor and elemental sulfur.
Through vapor-phase molecular mixing within the pores of car-
bon nanotubes (CNTs), a continuous and uniformly distributed
carbon-catalyst-sulfur composite layer along the nanotube scaf-
fold was constructed. This strategy simultaneously ensures the
homogeneous dispersion of the catalyst and achieves intimate
contact between the catalytic sites and sulfur molecules. The
resulting architecture optimizes performance across multiple
scales (Figure 1b). At the atomic scale, the CNT@Co catalyst
exhibits strong chemical adsorption to sulfur species, which
significantly lowers the Gibbs free energy barrier for sulfur reduc-
tion. For the reverse reaction, the catalyst substantially reduces
the energy barrier for Li2S oxidation by weakening the internal
Li─S bond and increasing interfacial charge transfer. At the
nanoscale, the CNT pores confine sulfur nanoparticles, effectively
buffering volume changes during cycling. Upon incorporation
of the SE, a tailored microstructure is formed, establishing
hierarchical transport pathways that maximize the extent of
C/S/SE triple-phase boundaries while minimizing detrimental

Results and Discussion
2.1
High-Density Co Atom Catalytic Sites
Construction
Nitrogen-doped multi-walled carbon nanotubes (CNTs), recog-
nized for their excellent electrical conductivity, are employed as
sulfur hosts and conductive agents to construct efficient long-
range electron transport networks. The inherent nanoporosity
of CNTs not only provides an ideal sulfur-host architecture for
uniform nanoscale sulfur dispersion but also effectively mitigates
volume fluctuations during electrochemical cycling. To enhance
sulfur redox kinetics, cobalt elements were precisely anchored
onto CNT surfaces through a vapor deposition strategy. Cobalt
acetylacetonate (Co(acac)2) serves as the cobalt source, and its
basic information is detailed in Figure S1a,b. During fabrication,
the Co(acac)2 and CNT powders were placed inside the quartz
tube, with Co(acac)2 positioned upstream and CNT downstream,
as illustrated in Figure 2a. Upon heating, Co(acac)2 underwent
thermal decomposition at 185◦C, producing Co vapor and leaving
behind irregularly spherical powders at its original location
(Figure S1c,d). With an argon gas flow, the Co vapor was precisely
deposited onto the surface of the dispersed CNTs. The resulting
Co-anchored CNT (CNT@Co) was treated with acid pickling to
remove low nuclear clusters, thus serving as the sulfur host.
Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-
OES) analysis reveals approximately 1.6 wt. % Co content in the
CNT@Co samples (Table S1).
The crystalline structure of the CNT and CNT@Co powders was
characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD). As shown in Figure
S2a, peaks at 2θ of approximately 26.5◦, 44.3◦, 54.6◦, and 77.9◦
correspond to the graphite lattice planes of (002), (100), (004),
and (110), respectively (ICSD: PDF#97-007-6767) [26]. Notably,
no diffraction peaks were attributed to metallic Co or Co(acac)2
crystals observed in the CNT@Co, indicating the absence of
undesirable metallic or organic cobalt aggregates. Fourier Trans-
form Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) (Figure S2b) spectra further
corroborate the absence of organic residuals, as evidenced by the
disappearance of characteristic Co(acac)2 vibrations at 1657 cm−1
(C═C─C═O), 1600 cm−1 (C═O/C═C), and 1512 and 1456 cm−1
(C═O/C─H) [27]. Raman spectroscopy reveals structural modi-
fications by Co, with the D band (1348 cm−1) and G band (1578
cm−1) exhibiting blue shifts of 9 and 14 cm−1, respectively, relative
to pristine CNTs (Figure S3). Concurrently, the increased ID/IG
ratio from 0.174 to 0.193 indicates elevated defect density induced
by cobalt incorporation [28]. These results collectively confirm
the successful deposition and integration of Co elements into the
CNT framework. The mesoporous characteristics of CNT@Co
were confirmed by N2 physisorption (Figure S4), revealing an
average pore width of 3.057 nm. This size is conducive to ion trans-
port and facilitates the subsequent nanoscale dispersion of sulfur
particles. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) conclusively
identifies Co2+ species through the Co 2p3/2 peak at 781.3 eV with
a satellite feature at 786.5 eV, excluding metallic cobalt formation
(Figure 2b) [29]. The C1s spectrum of CNT@Co materials shows
peaks at 284.6 and 285.4 eV, corresponding to the C═C and C─C
(or C─N) bonds, respectively, which are consistent with the peaks
in the pristine CNTs [30] (Figure S5). The N 1s spectrum displays
the presence of three types of nitrogen species, namely graphitic
N, pyrrolic N, and pyridinic N. Interestingly, compared to CNTs,
the graphitic N signal is significantly diminished in the CNT@Co
material, suggesting that the very likely coordination between
graphite N and the Co atoms during doping (Figure 2c) [31].
To observe the distribution state of the Co element, scanning
electron microscope (SEM) and transmission electron micro-
scope (TEM) images were obtained. As shown in Figure S6a–d,
CNT powders exhibit a cylindrical morphology with diameters
ranging from 5 to 10 nm and a wall thickness of approximately
10 nm, while maintaining 10–30 µm lengths to ensure continuous
electron transport pathways. Similar morphologies are observed
in the CNT@Co materials, indicating that the multi-walled
tubular structure remains well-preserved after low-temperature
heat treatment (Figure 2d). Moreover, no obvious Co particle
aggregation was observed on the surface or inside the CNT@Co
tubes, which aligns well with the corresponding ring-like selected
area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern (Figure S6e–g). High-
angle annular dark field of scanning transmission electron
microscopy (HAADF-STEM) reveals the atomic-scale distribu-
tion of Co within the CNT@Co materials (Figure 2e; Figure
S7). In addition to the visible graphene lattice characteristic of
pristine CNTs, numerous brighter spots, highlighted by the red
circles, represent Co atoms. A significant proportion of these
atoms form paired configurations with an interatomic distance
of ∼2.63 nm, confirming the successful synthesis of diatomic
catalysts (Figure S8). To further elucidate the chemical state and
coordination environment of Co atoms in CNT@Co material,
synchrotron X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) analysis was
employed. Figure 2f shows that the Co K-edge near-edge adsorp-
tion energy is close to that of the CoO reference, confirming
the predominant +2 oxidation state, which is consistent with
the findings from XPS analysis. The Fourier transform of the R-
space extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectra
reveals a primary characteristic peak and a secondary peak at
approximately 1.6 Å and 2.2 Å, corresponding to the Co─N
and Co─Co coordination in the first shell, respectively, which
is concluded by combining with the results of XPS analysis
(Figure 2g). To distinguish the backscattering atoms clearly,
wavelet transform (WT) EXAFS analysis on the k3-weighted
EXAFS signal was performed (Figure 2h; Figure S9). As shown
in Figure 2h, the maximum intensity of contour plots is observed
at approximately 6.0 Å−1, attributed to Co─N coordination, and
another distinct feature at around 8.0 Å−1, corresponding to
Co─Co pairing [32]. Quantitative EXAFS curve fitting analysis
was subsequently conducted to determine the Co and N coor-
dination structure parameters in the CNT@Co materials. The
R/k space fitting curves for CNT@Co and control materials are
presented in Figure 2i and Figures S10 and S11, respectively, with
corresponding fitting results summarized in Table S2. The fitting

2.2
Interface Design Tailored for Directional
Catalysis
Following the successful preparation of the CNT@Co host,
sulfur was loaded via the melt-diffusion method. Thermal sub-
limation at 155◦C enabled uniform dispersion of sulfur within
the CNT@Co matrix, with sulfur evenly depositing onto its
surfaces upon cooling. Vapor-phase mixing ensures intimate
contact between sulfur and Co sites while ensuring effec-
tive catalysis (Figure 3a). The produced CNT@Co/S materials
preserve the original nanotubular architecture without sul-
fur agglomeration, as evidenced by EDS mapping confirming
homogeneous S, C, and Co distributions (Figure 3b; Figure
S12). High-resolution TEM of individual CNT@Co/S samples
reveals nanoscale-uniform dispersion of sulfur on the CNT@Co
framework, establishing a rapid electron transfer network for
sulfur conversion (Figure 3c). Ionic conductors were introduced
by ball-milling pre-prepared CNT@Co/S with Li6PS5Cl (LPSC)
solid electrolyte to form CNT@Co/S-LPSC composite cathode.
This architecture features a sulfur molecular layer distributed
between the electrolyte and carbon matrix, establishing the
hierarchical electronic/ionic transport network (Figure 3d). The
configuration generates numerous C/S/SE triple-phase interfaces

2.3
Electrochemical Performance Evaluation
To clarify the role of the Co site and establish microstructure-
electrochemical performance relationships, ASSLSBs were fab-
ricated with CNT-S-LPSC or CNT@Co/S-LPSC cathodes, an
LPSC SE layer, and lithium-indium (Li-In) alloy. LPSC elec-
trolytes synthesized via mechanical ball-milling followed by
heat treatment crystallized in an argyrodite structure, exhibiting
high ionic conductivity of 7.7 mS cm−1 at 30◦C (Figure S18).
The stable Li-In electrode eliminates anode interfacial interfer-
ence, enabling a more accurate performance evaluation of the
cathode. Figure 4a shows the galvanostatic discharge–charge
profiles of the CNT-S-LPSC and CNT@Co/S-LPSC cathode with
a sulfur loading of 1.5 mg cm−2 in the voltage range of 1.1–
2.2 V (vs. Li-In). Both cathodes exhibit a single and continuous
discharge plateau, demonstrating effective suppression of high-
order polysulfide dissolution. Significantly, the CNT@Co/S-LPSC
cathode delivers an initial discharge capacity of 1184 mAh
g−1 at 0.2 C, greatly higher than the 784 mAh g−1 for the
CNT-S-LPSC cathode. This substantial capacity enhancement is
directly attributed to optimized C/S/SE triple-phase interfaces
that maximize active sulfur utilization. Cyclic voltammetry (CV)
tests reveal a distinct reduction peak at 1.22 V, corresponding
to the conversion of S8 to Li2S (Figure 4b), and an oxida-
tion peak at 1.89 V for the reverse reaction during charging.
Compared to the CNT-S-LPSC cathode, the CNT@Co/S-LPSC
cathode displays reduced polarization and enhanced peak cur-
rents, confirming the catalytic effect of Co atoms that accelerated
sulfur conversion kinetics. The CNT@Co/S-LPSC cathode also
demonstrates superior long-term cycling stability, maintaining
a specific capacity of 970 mAh g−1 after 500 cycles at 0.2 C,
corresponding to a capacity retention of 82% (Figure 4c). In
sharp contrast, the CNT-S-LPSC cathode presents rapid capacity
fading, retaining only 229 mAh g−1 after 500 cycles (29% capacity
retention).
Figure 4d,e, and Figure S19a display the rate performance of
the CNT-S-LPSC and CNT@Co/S-LPSC cathodes. The CNT-S-
LPSC cathode exhibits severely limited capacity at high current
densities, delivering only 130 mAh g−1 at 1 C due to both sluggish
reaction kinetics and insufficient ionic diffusion. In comparison,
the CNT@Co/S-LPSC cathode demonstrates superior rate perfor-
mance with reversible capacities of 1358, 1253, 763, and 367 mAh
g−1 at 0.1 C, 0.2 C, 0.5 C, and 1 C, respectively. When returning
to 0.2 C, capacity recovers to 1291 mAh g−1, demonstrating
exceptional electrochemical reversibility. Long-term cycling at
high rates is further shown in Figure 4f. The CNT@Co/S-LPSC
cathode undergoes a slight capacity increase during the first 10
cycles at 0.5 C, attributed to progressive activation, and subse-
quently stabilizes at approximately 1150 mAh g−1, with a superior
capacity retention of 97 % over 500 cycles. In contrast, the CNT-
S-LPSC cathode delivers only ∼600 mAh g−1 at equivalent cycles.
Under high sulfur loadings, the CNT@Co/S-LPSC cathode fur-
ther outperforms the CNT-S-LPSC cathode, maintaining higher
and more stable capacities, highlighting the effectiveness in
mitigating sulfur volume expansion and suppressing electrolyte
decomposition (Figure 4g). Elevated-temperature electrochemi-
cal testing at 50◦C elucidates enhanced reaction kinetics, yielding
higher specific capacities of 1466 and 1223 mAh g−1 at 0.1 C
for the CNT-S-LPSC and CNT@Co/S-LPSC cathodes, respec-
tively. However, thermal acceleration intensifies adverse effects,
including parasitic reactions, accelerating cell electrochemical
degradation. Consequently, the CNT-S-LPSC cathode exhibits
severe capacity fading, retaining only 379 mAh g−1 after 60 cycles
(Figure 4h). In stark contrast, the CNT@Co/S-LPSC cathode
maintains a high specific capacity of 1292 mAh g−1 after 60
cycles, attributed to the thermodynamically stable S/C/SE triple-
phase interfaces and suppressed side reactions. This performance
advantage persists during 0.5 C cycling at 50◦C, where the
CNT@Co/S-LPSC cathode retains a capacity of 1054 mAh g−1
after 250 cycles, significantly outperforming that of 389 mAh
g−1 for the CNT-S-LPSC cathode (Figure S19b). Compared to
published literature (Table S3; Figure 4i) [34–47], the designed
CNT@Co/S-LPSC cathode delivers a higher specific capacity
and superior high-rate capacity retention, validating the positive
role of structural catalysis and interface design. This exceptional
performance, maintained across high current densities, high
sulfur loadings, and a wide temperature range, stems from the
dual benefits of atomic Co sites accelerating redox kinetics
and a hierarchical network ensuring efficient ion and electron
transport.

2.4
Reaction Kinetics Evaluation and Catalytic
Mechanism Analysis
To decouple the multiscale kinetic processes in ASSLSBs, in
situ electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) coupled with
distribution of relaxation times (DRT) analysis was employed.
Figure 5a,d, and Figure S20 present the initial discharge–charge
curves and impedance evolution during discharge–charge cycles.
The typical EIS spectra comprise an integrated semicircle in
the high-frequency region and a tail in the low-frequency
region, representing charge transfer resistance (Rct) and ion
diffusion, respectively. Notably, the CNT@Co/S-LPSC cathode
demonstrates a smaller Rct compared to the CNT-S-LPSC at
equivalent discharge–charge depths, indicating superior charge
transport properties. DRT analysis was conducted to reveal the
specific reaction kinetics process, identifying four characteristic
relaxation processes with peak areas quantifying impedance
contributions. Specifically, relaxation time τ in the range of 10−6 ∼
10−5 s corresponds to LPSC electrolytes grain boundary response,
while peaks within 10−4 ∼10−2 s represent the formation of
the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) (denoted as RSEI) [48].
Relaxation processes within 10−2 to 10−1 s, and 10−1 to 100 s
exhibit high reversibility, corresponding to the charge transfer
resistance (Rct). As shown in Figure 5b,e, the CNT@Co/S-
LPSC cathode exhibits substantially reduced RSEI and Rct values
compared to the CNT-S-LPSC cathode, indicating enhanced Li+
transport through bulk and interfacial pathways. This improve-
ment suggests stabilized S/C/SE triple-phase boundaries with
suppressed parasitic reactions. During discharge, both CNT-
S-LPSC and CNT@Co/S-LPSC cathodes exhibit decreasing Rct
trends because the continuous lithiation of the cathode active
material leads to an increase in conductivity. However, in the

2.5
Electrolyte Decomposition Evaluation
Within the Cathode
To probe interfacial parasitic reactions in sulfur cathodes, in situ
Raman and XPS tests were conducted. Despite the analytical
challenges posed by trace-level electrolyte decomposition side
reactions and the ongoing sulfur redox processes, elucidating
these parasitic reactions is crucial as SEI degradation products
form electrically insulating barriers that would impede interfacial
charge transport. This work specifically targeted LPSC within the
sulfur cathode for Raman characterization to track its structural
evolution during electrochemical processes. Figure 6a,b displays
the Raman spectra of LPSC SEs within the sulfur cathode at dif-
ferent discharge–charge depths. The primary peak at 425.0 cm−1
represents the PS4
3−tetrahedron vibrations in LPSC powders [49].
For the CNT-S-LPSC cathode, the intensity of the peak weakens
initially, subsequently increases, but fails to reach its initial
signal strength (Figure S27a,b). This signal fluctuation indicates
continuous LPSC decomposition exhibiting partially reversible
behavior as reported [50]. The irreversible byproducts drive a
continuous increase in interfacial impedance during prolonged
cycling, acting as a parasitic reaction that ultimately leads to grad-
ual capacity decay. In contrast, the CNT@Co/S-LPSC electrode
maintains constant peak intensity under identical conditions,
demonstrating negligible SE decomposition (Figure 6b; Figure
S27c,d), which is attributed to the reduced SE/C interface within
the CNT@Co/S-LPSC microstructure.
The decomposition products were identified through TEM and
XPS analysis. In the cycled CNT-S-LPSC electrode, byproducts
such as Li2S, Li3P, and LiCl were observed on the electrolyte
surface, which hindered ion transport (Figure 6c). In contrast,
no such by-products were detected on the LPSC electrolyte
in the CNT@Co/S-LPSC electrode (Figure 6f). As shown in
Figure 6d, the S 2p spectra of cycled CNT-S-LPSC cathodes exhibit
characteristic PS4
3−peaks at 161.7 and 162.9 eV alongside S─S
bonds at 163.3 and 164.6 eV, with an additional Li2S peak emerging
at 160.4 eV, while corresponding P 2p spectra confirm Li3P forma-
tion at 130.4 eV (Figure 6e) [51]. These decomposition byproducts
demonstrate extensive SE degradation at prevalent SE/C inter-
faces in CNT-S-LPSC cathodes. Conversely, negligible Li2S and
no Li3P are detected in the CNT@Co-LPSC cathode after long-
term cycling, indicating the suppression of the parasitic reactions
(Figure 6g,h). The enhanced electrochemical stability originates
from the hierarchical ionic/electronic transport microstructure,
which drastically reduces SE/C contact and thereby mitigates SE
degradation. Consequently, the CNT@Co/S-LPSC cathode simul-
taneously improves electrochemical performance and accelerates
sulfur conversion through stabilized triple-phase interfaces.

Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. The CNT@Co samples
were prepared using the vapor deposition method. First, the
150 mg of Co(acac)2 and 100 mg of CNT powders were placed
separately into a small quartz tube. These were then inserted
into the large quartz tube of the furnace, with Co(acac)2 powders

4.2
Synthesis of the CNT@Co/S Samples
CNT@Co/S samples were synthesized using the melting diffu-
sion method. First, the as-synthesized CNT@Co samples and
sulfur powders, in a weight ratio of 2:3, were mixed by ball-
milling for 2 h to achieve uniform distribution. The mixtures
were then heat-treated in a hydrothermal kettle at 155◦C for 10 h.
The resulting black products were CNT@Co/S composites, which
served as active materials.
4.3
Synthesis of LPSC Electrolytes
Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 (LPSC) sulfide solid-state electrolytes were synthe-
sized via conventional solid-state reaction. Specifically, Li2S (99.9
wt. %, Sigma), P2S5 (99 wt. %, Macklin), and LiCl (99.99 wt. %,
Aladdin) powders were mixed in a ratio of 4.5:1:3, along with
zirconia balls (diameter: 10 mm), and placed in a zirconia pot.
Subsequently, the mechanochemical synthesis was performed
using ball-milling at 550 rpm for 12 h. The resulting composites
were pressed into pellets and calcined at 500◦C for 2 h, with a
heating rate of 10◦C min−1. Finally, the product was ground to
obtain LPSC powers. The entire process was carried out in an
argon-filled atmosphere.
4.4
Synthesis of CNT-S-LPSC and
CNT@Co/S-LPSC Cathode
The CNT-S-LPSC composites are synthesized by ball-milling CNT
materials, sulfur powders, and LPSC powders in a weight ratio
of 2:3:4 at 500 rpm for 4 h. The CNT@Co/S-LPSC composites
consist of CNT@Co/S and LPSC in a weight ratio of 5:4, and are
synthesized in the same manner as the CNT-S-LPSC composites.
The entire mixing process is conducted under an argon gas
atmosphere.
4.5
Materials Characterization
The crystal structure of CNT, CNT@Co, and LPSC powders was
analyzed using X-ray diffractometer (XRD, Smartlab, Cu Kα) over
the 2θ range from 10◦to 80◦. Raman spectroscopy was performed
using a Laser Confocal Raman Microspectrometer (LabRAM
Odyssey; Horiba Jobin Yvon) with an excitation wavelength of
532 nm. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were acquired
using a Nicolet iS50 spectrophotometer. The Co content in CNT
was quantified using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrom-
etry (ICP-MS; Agilent7800). Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)
was conducted using a TAQ500 instrument under a nitrogen flow,
with a heating rate of 10◦C min−1. Specific surface area and pore
size distribution were determined using nitrogen adsorption–
desorption isotherms using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET;
AUTOSORB-IQ2-MP) method at 77 K. X-ray photoelectron spec-
troscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientific KAlpha+) was employed to
analyze the surface chemical properties. The argon ion polisher
(Fishione, Model 1061) was used to prepare samples for cross-
sectional observation of the electrodes. The morphologies of the
samples were characterized using a Hitachi SU-8010 field emis-
sion scanning electron microscope (FESEM). Elemental mapping
was performed using an energy-dispersive spectroscope (EDS)
attached to the FESEM. High-resolution transmission electron
microscopy (HRTEM) was carried out using a JEOL-JEM-F200
double Cs-corrected scanning transmission electron microscope
(STEM) at 200 kV, equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray
spectroscope. High-angle annular dark field-scanning transmis-
sion electron microscope (HAADF-STEM) images were obtained
using an FEI TITAN Chemi STEM equipped with a CEOS
probe corrector, operating at 200 kV. X-ray Absorption Near
Edge Structure (XANES) and Extended X-ray Absorption Fine
Structure (EXAFS) data were collected at Beamline 14 W at
Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF). Data reduc-
tion, analysis, and EXAFS fitting were performed using the
Athena and Artemis programs within the Demeter data analysis
packages, which utilize the FEFF6 program for EXAFS data
fitting. Energy calibration of the sample was performed using a
standard Co foil, which served as a reference and was measured
simultaneously.
4.6
ASSLSBs Assembly
All assembly processes were carried out in an argon-filled glove
box. The ASSLSB was assembled using prefabricated CNT-S-
LPSC or CNT@Co/S-LPSC composites as the cathode, a Li0.5In
(Li-In) anode, and an LPSC layer located between the cathode
and anode. The Li-In alloy anode, approximately 100 µm thick,
is prepared by cold-pressing lithium and indium foil at 110 MPa
for 20 min. The battery assembly procedure is outlined as follows:
First, 200 mg of LPSC powder was cold-pressed at 200 MPa to
form a solid electrolyte pellet with a diameter of 15 mm. The
composite cathode was then pressed onto one side of the SE layer
at 220 MPa. Finally, the Li-In anode was attached to the other side
of the LPSC pellet. An external pressure of 100 MPa was applied
to bulk-type cells during electrochemical tests.
4.7
Electrochemical Measurements
The galvanostatic discharge–charge processes of ASSLSBs were
performed within the voltage range of 1.1–2.2 V (vs. Li+/Li-In)
using a Neware battery test system (CT-4008T, Shenzhen, China).
Current densities of 0.1 C, 0.2 C, 0.5 C, and 1 C (where 1 C =
1675 mAh g−1) were applied to evaluate the rate performance of
the composite cathodes. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements
were conducted using a Biologic electrochemical workstation at a
scan rate of 0.1 mV s−1 from 1.1 to 2.2 V. AC impedance tests were
obtained in the frequency range of 106−10−1 Hz using the same
Biologic electrochemical workstation. The sulfur mass loading in
the composite cathode was varied at 0.7, 1.5, or 3 mg cm−2.
