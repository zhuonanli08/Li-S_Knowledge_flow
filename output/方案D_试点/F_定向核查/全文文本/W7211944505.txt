=== 第 1 页 ===
Electrode potential control of Li–Si alloys for safe
and high-energy-density all-solid-state Li–S
batteries
Hitoshi Nagata
* and Kunimitsu Kataoka
Electrochemical short-circuiting caused by lithium dendrite growth remains a major safety concern in high-
energy-density all-solid-state batteries. However, the tolerance of the negative-electrode potential against
lithium plating has rarely been evaluated under realistic full-cell conditions because three-electrode
measurements are experimentally challenging. In this study, we establish a simple and practical three-
electrode full-cell conﬁguration that enables direct monitoring of the negative-electrode potential in
high speciﬁc energy density all-solid-state S/Li–Si batteries. Using Li–Si alloy negative electrodes with
controlled lithium content, we show that the negative-electrode potential depends strongly on the Li/Si
ratio. A Li/Si ratio of 4.2 drives the negative-electrode potential below 0 V (vs. Li+/Li), entering the lithium
deposition regime, whereas Li/Si = 2.33 maintains the potential above 0 V (vs. Li+/Li) even at 6.4 mA
cm−2 and 25 °C. Notably, Li/Si = 2.33 preserves this safety margin for over 300 cycles at 1.3 mA cm−2
and 25 °C while maintaining a practical areal capacity of 3.3 mA h cm−2 and a negative-electrode
potential above 0.1 V (vs. Li+/Li). These results establish negative-electrode potential engineering as
a practical design strategy for safe and high-energy-density all-solid-state batteries.
1.
Introduction
All-solid-state lithium batteries (ASSLBs) are widely regarded as
promising next-generation energy-storage systems because non-
ammable inorganic solid electrolytes (SEs) oﬀer intrinsic
safety advantages over conventional liquid electrolytes. Recent
advances in sulde- and halide-based SEs have delivered room-
temperature ionic conductivities exceeding 10 mS cm−1,
approaching those of liquid electrolytes and enabling high-
power operation in practical cell architectures.1–6 Nevertheless,
improvements in SE conductivity alone do not directly translate
into higher energy density, which is governed primarily by the
intrinsic properties of the positive- and negative-electrode active
materials and by their combination. Among positive electrodes,
sulfur is particularly attractive because of its exceptionally high
theoretical capacity relative to conventional lithium transition-
metal oxides, making all-solid-state Li–S batteries a compel-
ling platform for high energy density.7–10 Because sulfur
contains no lithium, full-cell congurations require negative
electrodes that supply lithium; two major candidates are
lithium metal and lithium–silicon (Li–Si) alloys.11–16 A critical
challenge common to both choices is the risk of internal short-
circuiting caused by lithium dendrite growth through the SE
separator layer, which threatens safety and durability under
practical operating conditions.17,18 Strategies to address this risk
can be broadly categorized into two safety paradigms operating
at diﬀerent levels. The rst is structural/mechanical suppres-
sion, which aims to suppress dendrite penetration by densi-
fying the SE layer, enhancing its reduction resistance, and
stabilizing the electrode–electrolyte interface with interlayers or
articial interphases.19–24 Representative approaches include
hot-press densication of deformable sulde SEs, the use of
more reduction-resistant SE, and the insertion of thin metallic
interlayers to improve interfacial stability. These methods are
essential for lithium-metal-based systems and have shown
substantial progress in limiting dendrite propagation. The
second, complementary paradigm is electrochemical suppres-
sion of lithium plating: maintaining the negative-electrode
potential
above
the
thermodynamic
lithium
deposition
boundary (0 V vs. Li+/Li) to suppress dendrite nucleation.25–29
This concept is well established in liquid-electrolyte lithium-ion
batteries, where high-potential negative electrodes such as
Li4Ti5O12 (LTO) are known for superior safety because they
operate with a large margin from 0 V (vs. Li+/Li). By contrast,
lithium metal anodes inherently operate at or below 0 V (vs. Li+/
Li), rendering electrochemical control of lithium deposition
fundamentally challenging and placing the burden of safety
predominantly on structural/mechanical countermeasures. Li-
based alloy anodes such as Li–Si oﬀer a distinct advantage:
their operating potential can, in principle, be tuned by
composition, enabling potential-guided design that trades
energy density against an electrochemical safety margin.

2.
Experimental
All experimental procedures, including sample preparation, cell
assembly, and characterization, were conducted either in an Ar-
lled glovebox or in sealed containers under an Ar atmosphere
unless otherwise noted.
2.1.
Materials preparation
Reagent-grade Si (Niraco Co., 99.999%), Li (Honjo Metal Co.,
Ltd, 99.8%), Li2S (Mitsuwa Chem., 99.9%), P2S5 (Sigma-Aldrich,
99%), LiCl (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.999%), LiBH4 (Sigma-Aldrich,
95%), sulfur (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.5%), carbon nanober (CNF;
Sigma-Aldrich, 100 nm × 20–200 mm), and activated carbon
(AC; MSC-30(SS), Kansai Coke and Chemicals Co., Ltd) were
used as starting materials. Sulfur was puried by sublimation.
The Si reagent was pre-ground using a planetary ball mill
apparatus (Premium Line P-7, Fritsch Co.) at 370 rpm for 2 h
with 40 g of 5 mm diameter ZrO2 balls.
Li4.2Si (Li21Si5), Li3.25Si (Li13Si4), Li2.33Si (Li7Si3), and Li1.71Si
(Li12Si7) were synthesized by thermally reacting Li metal sheets
with crushed Si powder on a hot plate at the appropriate molar
ratios, following a previous method.16 All mixtures were rst
heated at 200 °C on a hot plate for 1 h in an Ar-lled glovebox,
ground with an agate mortar, reheated at 200 °C for another 1 h,
and ground again. Li4.2Si and Li2.33Si were obtained aer an
additional heat treatment at 200 °C for 1 h. Li3.25Si and Li1.71Si
were obtained by applying the same heat treatment as that used
for Li4.2Si and Li2.33Si, followed by an additional annealing step
at 400 °C for 2 h. LPSBHC (Li3PS4–2LiBH4–0.5LiCl) and
Li1.5PS3.3
(60Li2S–40P2S5)
as
the
SEs,
were
prepared
by
mechanical milling at 500 rpm for 10 h with ZrO2 balls (4 mm
diameter; total mass 160 g) in a ZrO2 pot. The reagents were
mixed at Li3PS4 : LiBH4 : LiCl = 1 : 2 : 0.5 and Li2S : P2S5 = 3 : 2
(mol), yielding ionic conductivities of 6.2 and 0.027 mS cm−1 at
25 °C, respectively, following previously reported procedures.4,30
The positive composite electrode (highly reactive sulfur elec-
trode) was prepared by mechanical milling at 370 rpm for 2 h
with ZrO2 balls (5 mm diameter; total mass 40 g) in a ZrO2 pot,
using a weight ratio of S : Li1.5PS3.3 : AC = 50 : 40 : 10, following
a previous report.31
X-ray diﬀraction (XRD; Cu Ka1, SmartLab II, Rigaku Co.) was
employed to examine the crystalline phases. Measurements
were conducted in a sealed cell assembled in an Ar-lled
glovebox.

Electrochemical tests

and In as the counter electrode. LPSBHC (100 mg) was placed in

stainless steel rods. The resulting three-layer pellet was sand-

collectors.

negative electrode, and a Li metal reference electrode. First, a Li

collector) were placed in the Li pocket of a separable PEEK tube
(inner diameter, 10 mm) and assembled using restraining
equipment (Fig. 1). Next, LPSBHC (100 mg) was loaded into the

3.
Results and discussion
3.1.
Preparation of Li–Si alloys
As shown in Fig. 2, the XRD patterns conrm that Li4.2Si,
Li3.25Si, Li2.33Si, and Li1.71Si were successfully synthesized via
thermal reaction of lithium metal with milled Si powder. Li4.2Si
and Li2.33Si were readily obtained aer heat treatment at 200 °C,
whereas Li3.25Si and Li1.71Si required an additional heat
treatment at 400 °C to achieve the targeted phases. These
results suggest that Li4.2Si and Li2.33Si form preferentially in
this solid–solid reaction system. The facile formation of Li4.2Si
can be attributed to the fact that it is the most Li-rich phase in
the Li–Si system, whereas the relatively easy formation of
Li2.33Si may reect its comparatively high stability with respect
to neighboring compositions. Because the synthesis proceeds
via a solid–solid reaction between Li metal and Si particles, the
thermodynamically favored Li4.2Si phase likely forms rst at the
particle surfaces, followed by compositional equilibration
toward the target phases through intra- and interparticle
diﬀusion. This interpretation is consistent with the XRD results
in Fig. S1a, where residual Li4.2Si was still observed for Li3.25Si
aer treatment at 200 °C, indicating that heating at 200 °C for
3 h was insuﬃcient to reach equilibrium at the nominal starting
composition. In contrast, for Li1.71Si, the diﬀraction pattern
aer treatment at 200 °C (Fig. S1b) was dominated by the Li7Si3
phase, while the targeted Li12Si7 phase was obtained only aer
the additional heat treatment at 400 °C. This result suggests
that the phase transformation from Li7Si3 to Li12Si7 requires
overcoming
a
higher
activation-energy
barrier
than
that
required for the formation of the more readily accessible Li-rich
phases. Overall, these ndings indicate that phase-selective
synthesis of LixSi alloys is governed not only by the nominal
Li/Si ratio but also by the kinetic accessibility and relative
stability of the corresponding equilibrium phases. It should be
noted that, for Li3.25Si, although the diﬀraction peaks of Li4.2Si
observed aer heat treatment at 200 °C disappeared aer the
additional heat treatment at 400 °C (Fig. S1a), the possibility of
a small amount of residual secondary phases cannot be
completely excluded owing to its relatively low crystallinity.
However, since the electrochemical process in this study begins
with
delithiation,
during
which
lithium
is
preferentially
extracted from the more Li-rich phases, such minor phase
inhomogeneity is not expected to signicantly aﬀect the evalu-
ation of the negative-electrode potential. Therefore, all the
synthesized samples are considered to be evaluable as their
respective target compositions.
3.2.
Electrochemical tests of all-solid-state S/Li–Si alloys
batteries with Li reference electrode
To establish a practical electrochemical design criterion for Li–
Si negative electrodes in all-solid-state S/Li–Si full-cells, it is
essential to directly monitor the potential evolution of both the
positive and negative electrodes during charge–discharge
cycling. In particular, determining whether the negative-
electrode potential reaches the lithium plating potential is
critical for evaluating the risk of dendrite-induced short circuits
and for identifying Li–Si compositions that can be safely
employed as negative electrodes in sulfur-based full-cells. To
this end, we developed a simple three-electrode full-cell
conguration that enables simultaneous monitoring of both
electrode
potentials
while
maintaining
electrochemical
behavior comparable to that of a conventional test cell. As
illustrated in Fig. 1, the three-electrode cell was constructed

approximately 0.05 V (vs. Li+/Li) at 6.4 mA cm−2, demonstrating
high resistance to short-circuiting even at high rates; however,
its discharge capacity was lower than that of the Li2.33Si cell. To
examine the change in the charge–discharge behavior with
current density in more detail, the charge–discharge curves at
the h cycle of each current density, together with the corre-
sponding negative-electrode potential proles, are shown in
Fig. S5. Although the polarization of the charge–discharge
curves increased with increasing current density, these cells
maintained a relatively small polarization and a high discharge
capacity. For the S/Li2.33Si cell, the negative-electrode potential
proles in Fig. S5e indicate that the rise in the negative-
electrode potential during discharge partly contributes to the
decrease in the discharge capacity at 6.4 mA cm−2. Collectively,
these results indicate that Li2.33Si is the most suitable Li–Si alloy

suppresses lithium dendrite deposition while retaining high
specic energy density. Regarding the adequacy of this potential
margin, the negative-electrode potential of graphite, which is
widely used as a negative electrode in commercial lithium-ion
batteries, operates at around 0.1 V vs. Li+/Li or lower. Based
on this practical precedent, a margin of approximately 0.1 V vs.
Li+/Li can be regarded as a reasonable target for suppressing the
risk of lithium plating. From this perspective, the Li2.33Si cell,
which maintained a minimum negative-electrode potential
above 0.1 V vs. Li+/Li even at a high current density of 1.3 mA
cm−2, is expected to provide a suﬃcient safety margin under
practical operating conditions.

These results demonstrate that Li2.33Si maintains MNEP
above 0.1 V (vs. Li+/Li) even at the practically relevant current
density of 1.3 mA cm−2 at 25 °C, thereby suppressing short-
circuiting even when the separator layer is thinned. Accord-
ingly, Li2.33Si appears to be the most suitable Li–Si alloy
composition for the negative electrode in sulfur-based all-solid-
state full-cells, because it can eﬀectively reduce the risk of
lithium deposition while maintaining high specic energy
density. Direct measurement of electrode potentials under
practical operating conditions using a three-electrode full-cell is
therefore valuable not only for assessing the risk of lithium
dendrite deposition, but also for evaluating degradation of the
positive and negative electrodes independently. Three-electrode
measurements are widely used for evaluating the individual
potential of the positive and negative electrodes. However, in
all-solid-state batteries, which are generally evaluated using
pressed-powder cells, the introduction of a reference electrode
has been considered diﬃcult and detailed three-electrode
evaluation of full cells has rarely been performed. The three-
electrode conguration developed in this study enables such
evaluation to be carried out in a simple manner and is therefore
expected to be broadly applicable to other all-solid-state battery
systems
based
on
pressed-powder
cells.
Its
applicability
depends on the chemical and electrochemical stability among
the solid electrolyte, the reference electrode, and the electrodes;
in particular, since a lithium metal reference electrode is used,
the solid electrolyte in contact with the reference electrode must
be stable against lithium metal. For systems in which this
condition is not readily satised, the methodology can still be
applied by selecting a solid electrolyte with appropriate stability
or by employing a multilayer separator structure in which each
layer is chosen based on its stability against the adjacent elec-
trode, thereby extending its applicability to a wide range of
electrode and electrolyte combinations. Such analysis provides
essential design guidelines for selecting and optimizing elec-
trode combinations and architectures and should be regarded
as an useful evaluation methodology for practical imple-
mentation of all-solid-state batteries.
4.
Conclusions
In this study, we established a simple and practical three-
electrode full-cell conguration that enables direct monitoring
of electrode potentials in high-energy-density all-solid-state S/
Li–Si batteries under realistic operating conditions. Using this
platform, we demonstrated that the negative-electrode potential
depends strongly on the Li/Si ratio and directly governs the risk
of lithium plating and short-circuiting. Among the composi-
tions examined, Li2.33Si was identied as the most suitable
negative-electrode material because it maintained MNEP above
0.1 V (vs. Li+/Li) at a practically relevant current density of 1.3
mA cm−2 and 25 °C over 300 cycles. In addition, the S/Li2.33Si
full-cell maintained a high areal capacity of 3.3 mA h cm−2 and
a specic energy density of 395 Wh kg−1 (based on the
combined mass of the positive and negative electrodes) at 1.3
mA cm−2 and 25 °C aer 300 cycles. Consistently, the S/Li2.33Si
full cell avoided short-circuiting even with a thinned SE sepa-
rator layer during cycling at 1.3 mA cm−2 and 25 °C for 100
cycles. These results establish negative-electrode potential
engineering as a practical design strategy for sulfur-based all-
solid-state batteries. More broadly, direct potential measure-
ment in three-electrode full-cells is valuable not only for
assessing lithium dendrite deposition risk, but also for eluci-
dating degradation behavior of the positive and negative elec-
trodes
individually.
This
methodology
therefore
provides
essential design guidelines for electrode combinations and
architectures and should be regarded as a useful evaluation
approach for the practical implementation of all-solid-state
batteries.
