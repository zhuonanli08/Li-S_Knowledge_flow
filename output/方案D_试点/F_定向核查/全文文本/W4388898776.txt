Transition-Metal Sulfides for High-Performance Lithium Sulfide
Cathodes in All-Solid-State Lithium−Sulfur Batteries

Cite This: ACS Omega 2023, 8, 45557−45565

ABSTRACT: All-solid-state lithium−sulfur batteries (ASLSBs) have been
attracting attention as next-generation batteries because of their high
theoretical energy density, which exceeds that of traditional lithium−ion
batteries. However, the performance of ASLSBs is limited by the sluggish
redox reaction kinetics of lithium sulfide (Li2S) and S8 cathodes and the
electrochemical degradation of cathode materials and solid electrolytes
during cycling. Herein, we report a cathode design consisting of Li2S and
transition-metal sulfides. This cathode design enhances the redox reaction
kinetics of the cathode and suppresses interfacial degradation between the
cathodes and solid electrolytes in the composite cathodes. The interface
design uses titanium disulfide, molybdenum sulfide (MoS2), and tungsten
sulfide to facilitate redox reaction kinetics, which improves the practical
performance of ASLSBs. Among the composite cathodes examined in this
work, the Li2S−MoS2 composite cathode exhibited the highest discharge capacity of 661 mA h g−1 (2.09 mA h cm−2) after 100
cycles. Electrochemical impedance analysis demonstrated that transition-metal sulfides, particularly MoS2, suppressed the increase in
resistance through cycling of the composite cathodes. This finding suggests that transition-metal sulfides in Li2S composite cathodes
multifunction as redox mediators and buffer layers, improving practical battery performance. Therefore, the electrode design offered
in this study enhances the electrochemical utilization and long-term stability of ASLSBs.
1. INTRODUCTION
Lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries represent one of the most
promising energy storage technologies for electric vehicles
because of their extremely high theoretical energy density
(reaching up to 2600 mA h g−1 when sulfur is used as the
cathode material).1,2 However, the commercialization of Li−S
batteries is hindered by several obstacles, including the self-
discharge caused by the dissolution of lithium polysulfides in
the charge−discharge process, poor electrical conductivity of S
and lithium sulfide (Li2S) cathodes, lithium dendrite growth,
and large volume expansion in the conversion reaction of S/
Li2S cathodes.3 In the 2000s, the concept of all-solid-state Li−
S batteries (ASLSBs) was demonstrated; they replaced organic
liquid electrolytes with inorganic solid electrolytes (SEs) to
inhibit polysulfide dissolution and inherently avoid self-
discharge.4 However, the performance of ASLSBs remains
limited by the insulating nature of S8 and Li2S, which are the
fully charged and discharged products of sulfur electrodes,
respectively.5 The limited performance of ASLSBs can be
improved through several strategies: incorporation of various
elements into Li2S to improve its conductivity6−10 and
fabrication of composites blended with faster ionic conductors
or high-surface-area carbon materials.11−13 According to
Hakari et al., LiI-doped Li2S considerably improved the ionic
conductivity and electrochemical utilization of ASLSBs,
emphasizing the importance of an ionic conduction network
in composite cathodes.7,8,14 Subsequently, extensive research
has focused on material designs that improve the ionic
conductivity of cathode materials, which led to the develop-
ment of high-performance materials, Li2S−CaI2,9 Li2S−Li2O−
LiI,15 etc. However, large amounts of additives, such as iodide,
reduce the energy density of ASLSBs by decreasing the loading
of cathode materials in composite cathodes. To overcome this
challenge, researchers proposed an electrode design solely
comprising bifunctional Li2S−V2S3−LiI, which simultaneously
served as an active material and a SE and achieved a high
capacity of 370 mA h g−1 (per the weight of the composite
cathode) after 100 cycles.16 Li2S cathodes added with trace
amounts of AlI3 and Y2S3 also improved the practical
performance of ASLSBs without reducing the loading of
cathode materials.17,18

Other studies reported that the adaption of iodide9 and
LiVS2
19 as redox mediators improved the electrochemical
utilization of Li2S. Redox mediators are electrochemically
oxidized at their oxidation potential, and oxidized redox
mediators chemically oxidize Li2S. Such a charging process
facilitates the redox reaction of Li2S cathode materials.20 These
electrode designs highlight the need for interface engineering
to enhance the redox reaction kinetics of ASLSBs. A Raman
study on sulfide-based ASLSBs indicated the reaction of Li3PS4
(LPS) SEs with cathode materials, which results in their
decomposition into thiophosphates with S−S bonds, during
the charge/discharge process.21 Given such knowledge, surface
modification for the desired interface design must be
conducted not only to increase the redox reaction kinetics of
cathode materials but also to prevent the severe electrolyte
decomposition that causes capacity fading.21−23 The interfacial
degradation between SEs and sulfur cathode materials can be
suppressed by a molybdenum sulfide (MoS2) buffer layer,
which exhibits high chemical and electrochemical compatibility
with sulfide SEs.24 Moreover, studies on liquid Li−S batteries
demonstrated that the interface design using transition-metal
sulfides, such as titanium disulfide (TiS2),25−27 zirconium
disulfide,25 MoS2,
28,29 and tungsten sulfide (WS2),30 increases
the redox reaction kinetics of the S/Li2S cathode through their
high electric conductivity and strong bond with Li ions. Thus,
high-performance liquid-phase Li−S batteries can be achieved
via the introduction of transition-metal sulfides. However,
knowledge of the influence of transition-metal sulfides on the
performance of ASLSBs is limited.
Herein, we report an electrode design consisting of a Li2S
cathode and MS2 (M = Ti, Mo, or W). This design is aimed at
enhancing the redox reaction kinetics and suppressing
interfacial degradation between the SE and cathode. These
transition-metal sulfides can be leveraged as candidate redox
mediators to facilitate redox reaction kinetics because their
redox reaction occurs at a potential close to the redox potential
of S/Li2S. These materials exhibit reversible electrochemical
redox activity in the operating voltage range of ASLSBs. Given
these electrochemical behaviors, two-dimensional transition-
metal sulfides TiS2, MoS2, and WS2 were selected, and their
effects on the performance of ASLSBs were investigated. Li2S−
MS2 cathodes (M = Ti, Mo, or W) were prepared via a
mechanochemical process, and they enhanced the redox
reaction kinetics of ASLSBs, thereby improving their practical
performance. The cells with Li2S−MS2 cathodes (M = Ti, Mo,
or W) exhibited higher discharge capacities of 552, 661, and
523 mA h g−1 after 100 cycles than the cell with a pristine Li2S
cathode (427 mA h g−1). The Li2S−MoS2 cathode suppressed
the increase in resistance related to electrolyte decomposition
and delivered the best battery performance among the
cathodes investigated in this study.
2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
2.1. Chemicals. Li2S (99.9%, Mitsuwa), TiS2 (99.99%,
Sigma-Aldrich), MoS2 (99.99%, Sigma-Aldrich), WS2 (99.99%,
Sigma-Aldrich), LiCl (99.99%, Wako Fujifilm), P2S5 (99%,
Merck), and vapor-grown carbon fibers (VGCF, Showa
Denko) were used without additional purification.
2.2. Synthesis of Li2S-Transition-Metal Sulfide Cath-
odes and SEs. For the synthesis of Li2S or Li2S−MS2
cathodes (M = Ti, Mo, or W), Li2S powder or a mixture of
Li2S and MS2 with a molar ratio of 99:1 was ball milled using a
planetary ball mill (Fritsch Pulverisette 7 Premium line) and
45 mL zirconia pots containing φ 10 mm zirconia balls at a
rotation speed of 510 rpm for 10 h. For the SEs blended in the
cathode, amorphous LPS SEs were prepared through the ball
milling of Li2S and P2S5 (molar ratio: 3:1) under the same
conditions described above. Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 (LPSCl) SEs for the
electrolyte layer were prepared through ball milling of a
mixture of Li2S, P2S5, and LiCl with a stoichiometric ratio at a
rotation speed of 600 rpm for 20 h, followed by heat treatment
at 450 °C for 2 h. The prepared LPS and LPSCl have ionic
conductivities of 10−4 and 10−3 S cm−1 at room temperature,
respectively.21
2.3. Preparation of Composite Cathodes. The
composite cathodes were synthesized by ball milling a mixture
of Li2S or Li2S−MS2 and VGCF at a weight ratio of 5:1 in 45
mL zirconia pots containing 35 g φ 4 mm zirconia balls at a
rotation speed of 510 rpm for 10 h. The resultant Li2S−MS2−
VGCF (M = Ti, Mo, or W) was ball milled with amorphous
LPS at a weight ratio of 6:4 using 45 mL zirconia pots
containing 35 g φ 4 mm zirconia balls at a rotation speed of
250 rpm for 2 h. The weight fraction of the Li2S cathode
material in the prepared composite cathodes was in the range
of 47.5−50.0%.
2.4. Cell Assembly. For the galvanostatic cycling tests,
indium (In)/Li | LPSCl | 50(Li2S or Li2S−MS2)−40LPS−
10VGCF (M = Ti, Mo, or W) cells were assembled as follows.
First, 80 mg of LPSCl powder was filled into a polyether−
ether−ketone cylinder using two stainless steel rods, followed
by the application of a uniaxial pressure of 256 MPa at room
temperature to form pellets with a diameter of approximately
10.0 mm. Then, 5 mg composite cathodes (corresponding to
the Li2S mass loading of 3.0−3.2 mg cm−2) were spread over
one side of the SE layer and pressed under 256 MPa at room
temperature. In and Li foils were placed on the other side of
the SE layer, and a 90 MPa pressure was applied at room
temperature. The fabricated cells were allowed to rest for 1 h
before galvanostatic cycling tests were conducted.
2.5. Electrochemical Measurements. Cycling of the
fabricated cells was carried out at 0.1 C (corresponding to a
current density of ∼0.37 mA cm−2), within a voltage range of
0.6−3.0 V (vs In/Li) and at 30 °C using a charge−discharge
device (Nagano, BST-2004H). The cell was subjected to
electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) before cycling
and after 30 cycles. EIS was performed using an electro-
chemical measurement system (Hz-Pro, Hokuto Denko) in the
frequency range of 1 MHz to 1 mHz and an applied voltage of
10 mV. All cells were measured in fully discharged state.
2.6. Material Characterization. Powder X-ray diffraction
(XRD) measurements were conducted to investigate the
crystal structure of the samples under Cu Kα radiation at a
scan rate of 5° min−1 (Rigaku SmartLab SE). The micro-
structural characteristics of the composite cathodes were
analyzed through characterization using field-emission scan-
ning electron microscopy (SEM) (Hitachi-S4800) with energy
dispersive spectroscopy (EDS; ULTIM MAX, Oxford Instru-
ment).

3.2. Electrochemical Properties. The redox reaction
kinetics of Li−S batteries can be enhanced by leveraging
transition-metal sulfides as potential redox mediators.25,27 To
investigate the effect of MS2 (M = Ti, Mo, or W) on battery
performance, we conducted galvanostatic cycling tests on cells
with Li2S−MS2 (M = Ti, Mo, or W) cathodes. Figure 3a
depicts the initial galvanostatic voltage profiles of all the
studied cells, which showed discharge capacities of 292 mA h
g−1 for Li2S, 398 mA h g−1 for Li2S−TiS2, 508 mA h g−1 for
Li2S−MoS2, and 529 mA h g−1 for Li2S−WS2, and
corresponding Coulombic efficiencies of 79.0, 85.9, 96.0, and
89.5%, respectively. At the 50th cycle, the discharge capacities
reached 350, 550, 688, and 550 mA h g−1 for Li2S, Li2S−TiS2,
Li2S−MoS2, and Li2S−WS2, respectively (Figure 3b). A larger
cell polarization was observed at the initial cycle than at the
50th cycle for all of the voltage profiles. This large polarization
was caused by the high activation barrier due to the strong
ionic bonds between Li+ and S2−in the pristine Li2S crystal.31
The cells using Li2S−MS2 cathodes (M = Ti, Mo, or W)
exhibited a lower initial polarization than the cell with pristine
Li2S. This finding suggests that the studied transition-metal
sulfides decrease the activation barrier of Li2S during the
charging process. Redox mediators facilitate reactions through
the electrochemical redox reaction itself; thus, the redox
mediation capability of transition-metal sulfides depends on
their redox reaction potential.32 MoS2 and WS2 demonstrate
desirable redox mediation capabilities because their oxidation
reactions occur at a slightly higher potential (2.3 V vs Li+/Li)
than that of Li2S (∼2.1 V vs Li+/Li).33−35 However, TiS2
presents a lower redox mediation capability because its
oxidation potential is lower than that of Li2S.36,37 MoS2 and
WS2 form LixMoS2 and LixWS2, respectively, through lithium−
ion intercalation and irreversibly convert to transition metals
and Li2S upon full discharge (0 V vs Li+/Li).33−35,38−41 In our
study, the cathodes with MoS2 and WS2 were operated at a
voltage range that only allowed for the intercalation reaction of
transition-metal sulfides. These conditions allow transition-
metal sulfides to function reversibly as redox mediators via an
intercalation-based redox reaction. Thus, the high redox
mediation capabilities of MoS2 and WS2 facilitate Li2S
activation, leading to lower polarization in the initial cycle.
The Li2S−MoS2 cathode exhibited the lowest overpotential
after 50 cycles among the cathodes reported in our study. As
shown in Figure 3c, the capacities of all the cells increased with
an increase in the cycle number during the early cycles, that is,
during the activation process of the Li2S cathode.21,42−44 A
possible association of the activation process with the
decomposition reaction of sulfide SEs in the cathodes has
been reported.21 After the activation process, the capacity
gradually decreased in the cell with the Li2S cathode after
seven cycles and then increased again with the increase in the
cycle number. This behavior is consistent with that observed in
a previous study and may be ascribed to the modified redox
activity of the cathode materials.21 At the 100th cycle, the
discharge capacities were 427, 552, 661, and 523 mA h g−1 for
Li2S, Li2S−TiS2, Li2S−MoS2, and Li2S−WS2, respectively, with
a Coulombic efficiency of ∼100%. After the conversion to
capacity per electrode area, the capacities were 1.28, 1.75, 2.09,
and 1.52 mA h cm−2 for Li2S, Li2S−TiS2, Li2S−MoS2, and
Li2S−WS2, respectively. Thus, the cells with Li2S−MS2

3.3. Redox Behavior. The performance of sulfide-based
ASLSBs is influenced by the redox reactions of sulfide SEs
blended in the composite cathode and the decomposition
products generated from SEs.21,23,45,46 To gain insights into the
electrochemistry of ASLSBs using Li2S−MS2 (M = Mo, Ti, or
W) cathodes, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of the
redox reactions of the cathode materials and the decom-
position products derived from sulfide SEs on the basis of
differential capacity curves. The differential capacity curves
calculated from the galvanostatic voltage profiles are shown in
Figure 4a−d. All of the cells showed evident oxidation peaks
and two reduction peaks. This redox behavior is consistent
with previously reported results.9,9,47,48 The initial oxidation
peak (○) is derived from the activation process of Li2S, and
the oxidation peaks (◇) at a potential of ∼1.7 V vs In/Li
originate from the oxidation reaction of cathode materials. The
reduction peak (△) at ∼1.3 V vs In/Li indicates the reduction
reaction of redox-active decomposition products generated
from LPS SEs in the cathode. A previous Raman spectroscopy
study revealed that sulfide SEs react with cathode materials
during charge/discharge cycling to decompose into long-chain
bridging sulfur species.21 The generated redox-active decom-
position products can enhance the capacity during the early
cycles. An alternative interpretation by Cao et al. suggested
that the chemical species with S−S bonds observed in the
Raman spectra were S8 and Li2S2 intermediates rather than
thiophosphates with S−S bonds.49 Furthermore, density
functional theory calculations indicated the high chemical
and electrochemical compatibilities of sulfide SE/Li2S and
sulfide SE/S8 interfaces.50 Given these previous reports, sulfide
SEs in composite cathodes may undergo chemical reactions
with cathode materials after their electrochemical decom-
position; that is, chemical reactions between the cathode
materials and redox-active decomposition products occur due
to the presence of sulfide SEs at the highly electroactive
interface area of carbon. The reduction peak (☆) observed at
low potentials (1.1−1.2 V vs In/Li) corresponds to the
reduction reaction of cathode materials.9,47 In the cell with a
Li2S cathode, the reduction peak at low potentials disappeared
after 50 cycles. This observation suggests the deactivation of
cathode materials through long-term cycling.21 Thus, the

3.4. Impedance Spectroscopy. To investigate the effect
of transition-metal sulfides as buffer layers on the impedance
properties, EIS measurements were conducted on all the cells
before and after cycling. Figure 5a−d shows the Nyquist plots
obtained from the EIS measurements of the cells with Li2S and
Li2S−MS2 (M = Ti, Mo, or W) cathodes before cycling and
after 30 cycles. The Nyquist plots of all of the as-prepared cells
showed a straight section with a 45° slope and a sharp
capacitive tail in the low-frequency region (Figure 5a). This
behavior is indicative of a nonfaradaic transmission-line
model.22 After 30 cycles, the Nyquist plot of the cell with
Li2S revealed a large semicircle in the frequency region below 5
mHz. This semicircle corresponds to the charge transfer
resistance of the cathode material, according to the result of an
impedance study on liquid Li−S batteries.21 The increased
resistance can be attributed to the deactivation of the cathode
materials after long-term cycling (Figure 4a). In addition, a
resistance component emerged in the middle-frequency range
(∼20 Hz). Electrochemical decomposition products generated
from sulfide SEs lead to increased resistance in the middle-
frequency range.21,51 Thus, the resistance in the middle-
frequency range should result from the charge transfer
resistance of the decomposition product from the SEs. These
resistance components in the middle- and low-frequency
ranges were confirmed in the cell with the Li2S−TiS2 cathode
after 30 cycles (Figure 5b). According to computational
studies, TiS2 has a desirable chemical compatibility with fully
discharged Li2S, fully charged S8 cathodes, and sulfide SEs.50 In
contrast, other calculation studies suggested the presence of
strong chemical bonds between Li in the Li2S4 intermediate
and S in TiS2.52,53 The strong interaction of the Li−S bond
leads to the charge transfer from nearby S atoms in Li2Sn redox
intermediates to TiS2 and weakens the Li−S bonds in Li2Sn,
thereby facilitating the decomposition of Li2Sn.53 Taking this
into consideration, the increased resistance of the cell with the
Li2S−TiS2 cathode may be due to the degradation evolution of
sulfide SEs induced by the strong interaction between TiS2 and
the redox-active decomposition product from sulfide SEs
during the charge/discharge process. The degradation
products of SEs showed limited conductivity, resulting in
capacity fading at high C rates. In contrast to the Li2S and
Li2S−TiS2 cathodes, the additional resistance in the cell after
30 cycles was not clarified in the Li2S−MoS2 and Li2S−WS2
cathodes (Figure 5c,d, respectively). As shown in Figure 5e, no

metal sulfides, particularly MoS2, enhanced the electrochemical
redox reaction kinetics of Li2S/S cathodes and suppressed
electrolyte degradation at the SE/cathode interface. Thus, the
proposed strategy improves the practical performance of the
ASLSBs.
4. CONCLUSIONS
In this study, we developed Li2S−TiS2, Li2S−MoS2, and Li2S−
WS2 cathodes for ASLSBs and investigated their effects on
battery performance. More broadly, our study provided a
valuable strategy that utilizes transition-metal sulfides to
enhance redox reaction kinetics and suppress interfacial
