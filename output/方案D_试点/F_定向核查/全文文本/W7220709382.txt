Coupling reaction pathways and interfacial
transport in practical all-solid-state lithium–sulfur
batteries
Seong Gyu Lee,

and Yun Jung Lee

All-solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries combine the high theoretical speciﬁc energy (2600 Wh kg−1) with
the prospect of suppressing the polysulﬁde dissolution and shuttling, which limit conventional liquid-
electrolyte cells. Replacing the liquid electrolyte with an inorganic solid electrolyte, however, does more
than suppress intermediate mobility: it fundamentally changes how sulfur conversion proceeds. In a dry
inorganic solid-state composite, electrochemical conversion becomes strongly dependent on solid–
solid contact and on the convergence of electronic and ionic transport at the sulfur–carbon–solid-
electrolyte interface. This coupling among reaction pathway, transport, and chemo-mechanical contact
constrains sulfur utilization and rate capability, with these limitations becoming increasingly severe in
thick, high-loading electrodes operated under reduced stack pressure. This review therefore treats
sulfur-conversion chemistry and interfacial transport as a single coupled design problem rather than as
separate materials challenges. Conductive frameworks, Li2S-activation strategies, and solid-electrolyte
chemistry and interface engineering are compared according to how they increase reaction accessibility
while avoiding parasitic interfacial reactions, excessive intermediate mobility, or loss of contact. Voltage-
proﬁle shape is used as an accessible initial indicator of whether sulfur conversion is predominantly
contact-limited or dissolution-assisted, while direct identiﬁcation of reaction intermediates provides
stronger support for mechanistic assignment. Reported strategies are further evaluated against practical
constraints of sulfur loading, stack pressure, temperature, and lithium inventory. This framework
identiﬁes selective interfacial connectivity and its retention under realistic cell conditions as central
requirements for translating solid-state sulfur chemistry into practical cells.
1.
Introduction
Sulfur is abundant and inexpensive,1–3 and its complete reduc-
tion to lithium sulde (Li2S) provides a theoretical capacity of
1675 mA h g−1 (ref. 4) according to the reaction S8 + 16Li+ + 16e−
/ 8Li2S. When paired with a lithium-metal anode, the result-
ing lithium–sulfur (Li–S) battery has a theoretical specic
energy approaching 2600 Wh kg−1.5 However, Li–S batteries
operating with conventional liquid electrolytes deliver practical
energy densities far below this theoretical value, owing to the
polysulde
shuttle
associated
with
the
multistep
sulfur-
reduction
pathway.
Complete
reduction
to
Li2S
passes
through soluble lithium polysuldes, and once dissolved in the
electrolyte, these intermediates can migrate to the anode,6,7
where they react parasitically with lithium metal and consume
active sulfur and lithium.8,9 Part of the dissolved sulfur is irre-
versibly trapped or consumed at the anode, whereas the
remaining intermediates diﬀuse back to the cathode and are
reoxidized.6 This shuttle drives rapid self-discharge, lowers
coulombic eﬃciency, and accelerates capacity fade,3 while
parasitic interfacial reactions progressively consume active
material and increase cell resistance.7 The sequential sulfur-
reduction steps in this dissolution-mediated pathway typically
give rise to two discharge plateaus, broadly corresponding to
the formation of soluble polysuldes and their subsequent
conversion to insoluble Li2S2/Li2S. Furthermore, the organic
solvents in which the polysuldes dissolve are highly am-
mable, creating a safety concern that becomes increasingly
consequential as cell size increases.10,11 Importantly, replacing
the liquid electrolyte with a polymer or gel phase does not
necessarily eliminate the dissolution-mediated pathway. Many
polymer and gel electrolytes can still solvate polysuldes, and
cells employing these electrolytes commonly retain the dual-

solvent but also on the moisture tolerance and lithium-metal

2.
Coupled reaction pathways and
interfacial transport in solid-state
sulfur conversion
Without a liquid phase to redistribute dissolved species across
the cathode, sulfur conversion becomes spatially tied to local
ionic and electronic access to the active material.7,28 Sulfur and
its discharge product Li2S are both electronically and ionically
insulating, so electrochemical conversion is localized at the S–
C–SE TPB, where an electron path, an ion path, and active
material coincide.7,26 The density and continuity of these TPB
contacts therefore directly determine how much active material
remains electrochemically accessible and how rapidly it can
react.13,21 Ionic access is particularly sensitive to how the solid-
electrolyte phase is distributed through the composite. Unlike
a liquid electrolyte, a solid electrolyte cannot spontaneously wet
and inltrate the cathode; continuous ionic pathways must
instead be established by intimate contact between solid-
electrolyte
and
active-material
particles.
Active
material
disconnected from this network cannot form an eﬀective TPB
and consequently remains electrochemically inactive.13 This
coupling of reaction pathway and interfacial transport is
therefore intrinsic to the solid-state sulfur cathode. Insuﬃcient
or nonuniform TPB connectivity leads to heterogeneous reac-
tion, incomplete utilization, and limited rate, while the intrinsic
conversion barriers of the sulfur couple, particularly during Li2S
oxidation, impose an additional kinetic limitation (Fig. 2).26,27
The most persistent source of contact loss is the large
volume change associated with sulfur conversion. Full lith-
iation of S8 to Li2S expands the active material by nearly 80% of
its initial volume, whereas the reverse delithiation corresponds
to a volume contraction of approximately 45% from the Li2S
state.7,28,31 Repeated over cycling, this volume swing can crack
the
composite
and
separate
active
material
from
the
surrounding electron and ion networks, thereby producing an
electrochemo-mechanical failure common to solid-state elec-
trodes and intensied by the large strains of the sulfur
couple.18,22 Lost contact is not readily recovered, because no
liquid phase is present to restore it. Material cut oﬀfrom either
network becomes electrochemically inactive, and this fraction
can accumulate as additional active material becomes isolated
during cycling.26,30 The importance of contact quality is already
evident in the rst cycle, before cycling-induced mechanical
degradation becomes dominant. A hand-mixed composite can
leave more than half of the discharged Li2S unoxidized aer the
rst charge, whereas the same components milled into intimate
contact recover almost the full capacity.7 The mismatch

Blending insulating sulfur and carbon with the ion conductor
lengthens and increases the tortuosity of the ionic pathways,
resulting in an eﬀective ionic conductivity orders of magnitude
below that of the pristine electrolyte.7 This transport penalty is
reected in the Bruggeman exponent obtained by tting the

Increasing

connectivity, but at the expense of active-material content and
attainable energy density. Cathode composition and micro-
structure therefore require a balance among TPB density,
transport continuity, and sulfur loading rather than simple
maximization of any single component.7 This trade-oﬀbecomes
increasingly important in thick sulfur cathodes. An architecture
that locally maximizes TPB density can simultaneously increase
ionic tortuosity and reduce the through-thickness continuity of
the solid-electrolyte network, so reaction-site density and long-
range transport cannot necessarily be maximized together.32
Because these structural features are diﬃcult to maintain
uniformly throughout a thick composite, regions with better
ionic and electronic accessibility react preferentially, whereas
poorly connected regions remain underutilized, producing
spatially heterogeneous sulfur conversion.21,23
The interfacial area needed to increase TPB density intro-
duces a second trade-oﬀ: the same contacts that enable sulfur
conversion also provide sites for electrolyte decomposition.
Increasing this contact area places more solid electrolyte in
direct contact with carbon and active material, where sulde
electrolytes undergo parasitic oxidation at the cathode within
roughly the 1.5–2.5 V versus Li+/Li window in which the cell
operates.7,13 The decomposition products form interphases less
conductive than the electrolyte they replace, and because they
remain xed at the site where they form, they restrict the local
supply of ions and electrons.7,13 Consequently, resistance grows
both within the S–C–SE contact network and at the cathode–
solid-electrolyte interface, and the added impedance limits rate
capability as cycling proceeds.16,33 These cathode-side contri-
butions add to the resistive terms arising at the lithium–elec-

3.
Conductive frameworks for the
sulfur cathode
For a conductive framework, the primary requirement is
a
percolating
electronic
network
rather
than
high
bulk
conductivity alone. The network must remain connected both to
the ionic pathways provided by the SE and to the insulating
sulfur
or
Li2S
while
the
active
material
swells
and
contracts.13,21,23,39,40 Electron percolation and interfacial contact
must therefore be engineered together. The fabrication route
determines the connectivity of this network and the extent of
this S–C–SE contact. Conductive-framework strategies can
therefore be compared by how eﬀectively they accommodate the
S8–Li2S volume change, extend electronic access throughout the
cathode,
and
preserve
low-resistance
S–C–SE
three-phase
boundaries. These functions depend not only on the intrinsic
conductivity of the framework but also on its geometry, its
contact with the active material and solid electrolyte, and the
way the composite cathode is fabricated.39,40
The fabrication route determines the initial quality of TPB
contact and constrains the sulfur content and areal loading that
the cathode can support.39,41 Mechanical milling provides the
simplest route to forming a composite in a single processing
step.42 However, deposition and in situ routes can create more
intimate interfaces than conventional milling, for example by
producing ner sulfur particles43 and smooth nanoscale Li2S–
carbon interfaces.44–47 This improvement in interfacial contact,
however,
generally
comes
at
the
expense
of
processing
simplicity and scalability. Dry-process sheet fabrication at very
low binder content oﬀers a more scalable route to thick cathode
lms.40,41
Once the initial TPB network is established, the framework
must preserve it against the large volume change of sulfur
conversion. Volume accommodation is achieved through three
main approaches: containment, spatial distribution of the
active material, and mechanical compliance. Containment
itself can take two contrasting structural forms. An open,
porous carbon structure absorbs the expansion and reduces the
stress at the sulfur–carbon interface, at the cost of a lower tap
density and of a structure loose enough for sulfur to disconnect
and become electrochemically dead.39 Conversely, a compact
structure encloses nanoscale sulfur or Li2S in a carbon shell that
contains the swing, which has sustained capacity at Li2S load-
ings well above the usual laboratory range.39 Rather than
conning the volume change within a host, distribution
reduces its local magnitude by spreading the active material
over a larger interfacial area. One route, for example, con-
formally coats a sulfur layer a few nanometers thick onto
reduced graphene oxide.48 The third approach accommodates
the strain through mechanical compliance of the host itself. A
polypyrrole coating on a nitrogen-doped carbon-nanotube
scaﬀold provides a viscoelastic network that absorbs local
stress, thereby lowering the pressure change per unit capacity
by about 11% at a sulfur loading of 3 mg cm−2.49
Volume accommodation alone is not suﬃcient; the carbon
framework must provide an electronic pathway that reaches the
active material throughout the cathode. The spatial reach and
continuity of this pathway provide a useful basis for comparing
framework architectures, with carbon dimensionality serving as
a secondary structural descriptor. One-dimensional carbons
such as nanotubes, bers, and electrospun frameworks give
long-range, continuous electronic conduction. Their high
aspect ratio reduces the dependence on the repeated carbon–

dimensional freestanding skeletons combine long-range elec-
tronic connectivity with a porous host that maintains contact
among the active material, carbon, and SE.39
Beyond long-range electronic connectivity, the framework
must also provide suﬃcient interfacial area and pore volume
while preserving ionic access to the active material. Two-
dimensional carbons such as graphene provide extended
sulfur–carbon contact through conductive, high-surface-area
sheets that can also accommodate expansion and form
binder-free, current-collector-free cathodes.39,56 All-solid-state
sulfur cells based on sulfur–graphene composites demon-
strate the benet of extended interfacial contact,57 whereas
ordered mesoporous carbon uses its pore volume to conne
sulfur along the carbon walls, with scattering measurements
revealing two Li2S phases during discharge.58 When active-
material connement and pore volume become the dominant
requirements, zero-dimensional porous carbons and carbon
blacks provide a diﬀerent structural solution. Interconnected
mesopores sustain the sulfur–carbon contact and a continuous
conduction network more eﬀectively than isolated micro-
pores.39 A core–shell composite of this kind, with sulfur
enclosed in a carbon-black host, shows strong capacity reten-
tion over extended cycling.59 Gas-phase mixing provides another
route to intimate contact by forming a 1–10 nm sulfur–acetylene
black composite.60 Taken together, these examples reinforce
that carbon dimensionality is secondary to the functions
created by the resulting architecture: electronic connectivity,
accessible interfacial area, and pore volume for active-material
accommodation. In practical cathodes, these functions must
be balanced against sulfur loading, the electrolyte-to-sulfur
ratio, and cathode tap density, which jointly determine gravi-
metric and volumetric energy density.51

the bulk conductivity or carbon content alone.
A complementary strategy goes beyond optimizing discrete
S–C and S–SE contacts by introducing mixed ionic and elec-
tronic conduction into the host or composite itself. This
broadens the conventional TPB from a localized junction into
a nite reaction zone, while retaining the fundamental

transition-metal sulde still operates alongside a carbon

the sulfur-conversion pathway framework used here.
Even when ionic and electronic pathways are established,

4.
Chemical strategies for Li2S
activation
Li2S oxidation represents a distinct cathode-side kinetic limi-
tation that conductive-framework design alone does not resolve.
A cathode can be assembled directly with Li2S rather than
elemental sulfur, placing the cell initially in its discharged state.
This starting state makes Li2S oxidation the rst electro-
chemical step while also changing both the mechanical history
of the cathode and the source of initial lithium inventory. A fully
lithiated Li2S cathode corresponds to the expanded end of the
roughly 80% volume change of the sulfur–Li2S couple, so a cell
assembled in this state avoids the one-directional expansion
experienced by a pristine-sulfur cathode on its rst discharge.89
Furthermore, Li2S supplies the initial lithium inventory from
the cathode and could in principle enable an anode-free
architecture with a bare current collector, while its stability in
dry air provides an additional processing advantage.89 These
advantages, however, come with a kinetic penalty at the rst
electrochemical step. Extracting lithium from Li2S on the rst
charge has a high oxidation energy barrier, requiring a high
activation
potential
and
driving
the
cell
voltage
toward
approximately 4 V before full electrochemical activation.89 This
barrier limits the charging rate and promotes electrolyte
decomposition at elevated potential, thereby lowering the initial
coulombic eﬃciency.89 Li2S-based cathodes therefore present
a central trade-oﬀ: assembling the cell from the discharged
product oﬀers mechanical and lithium-inventory advantages,
but makes Li2S oxidation a primary kinetic bottleneck that
requires chemical activation.89

barrier is redox mediation, in which an added redox couple
provides a lower-energy pathway for charge transfer to Li2S. In

Within the sulde class, these coupled requirements are
addressed at three distinct structural levels: bulk doping
modies the properties of the electrolyte itself, catholyte or
composite design controls how the ionic network extends
through the cathode, and protective coatings and interlayers
regulate the chemistry of specic interfaces.12,17,109–111 Despite
acting at diﬀerent locations, all three strategies address the
same underlying limitation. High ionic conductivity in many
sulde electrolytes is accompanied by a limited electrochemical
stability window, so the interfaces at which the reaction
proceeds decompose during cycling unless they are deliberately
engineered.12,29,110 The objective is therefore not simply to
maximize ionic conductivity, but to preserve ionic transport
while limiting decomposition at the relevant interface.12,109
At the bulk-material level, doping modies the sulde itself
rather than introducing a separate interfacial phase. In Li7P3S11
and related thiophosphates, modications that improve the Li+
transport can also alter the interfacial stability.112–114 Depending
on composition and thermal state, such modication can
increase ionic conductivity while also widening stability against
lithium, lowering the interfacial resistance, or delaying dendrite
growth.112–114
Bulk conductivity alone, however, does not ensure ionic
access throughout a thick composite cathode. Catholyte and
composite design therefore addresses a diﬀerent limitation:
whether the ionic network extends far enough through the
electrode to reach each reacting sulfur site. The immobile
electrolyte cannot redistribute around the active material, and
at sulfur content above 50 wt%, the remaining solid-electrolyte
fraction can become insuﬃcient to sustain continuous Li+
pathways.2,90 Catholyte and composite designs address this
limitation by increasing the ionic transport capacity at a given
electrolyte fraction, for example through lower-density cath-
olytes or higher-conductivity electrolyte phases.90,116,117 Beyond
modifying the bulk catholyte network, related approaches act
locally at the sulfur–electrolyte interface or exploit the electro-
lyte's own interfacial chemistry.118,119 At the chemistry level, the
sulde catholyte itself can instead be replaced by a halide,
which combines sulde-like deformability and conductivity
with a wider electrochemical stability window, thereby avoiding
some of the decomposition associated with suldes within the
sulfur

window.120

extend
this

a so, reduction-tolerant sublattice.121 These halide strategies
therefore address cathode- or lithium-side interfacial instability
by changing the electrolyte chemistry itself rather than solely
engineering an unstable sulde interface.120,121

follows from the contact requirement described above rather
than from a preference of the eld. Suldes deform under the

window, which constitutes the primary bottleneck addressed by
the interface-engineering strategies reviewed above. Their wider
electrochemical windows remove part of the decomposition
problem, but each class introduces a limitation of its own at the
sulfur cathode.
Halide electrolytes combine sulde-like deformability with
a wider oxidative stability window, and cold-pressed Li–S cells
based on rare-earth halide solid electrolytes such as bromide-
based Li3HoBr6 have been demonstrated.122 A wide electro-
chemical window, however, does not guarantee chemical
compatibility with the sulfur cathode. A direct comparison of
Li3InCl6, Li3YCl6, and Li3YBr6 in solid-state Li–S cathodes found
that Li3InCl6 degrades by reduction and that Li3YCl6 reacts
chemically with Li2S, whereas Li3YBr6 gave the best perfor-
mance of the three.123 Halide compatibility with Li2S is therefore
composition dependent, and the selection criterion is chemical
stability against the discharge product rather than the electro-
chemical window alone. Iodide-based chemistries extend this
approach toward the lithium contact through a reduction-
tolerant sublattice,121 and the catholyte substitution discussed
above120 follows the same design logic.
Oxide electrolytes present the opposite limitation. Their
electrochemical and chemical stability is the widest of the three
classes, but their mechanical rigidity results in insuﬃcient
solid–solid contact and high grain-boundary resistance, so that
a continuous ionic network with sulfur cannot be established by
cold pressing alone.124 This constraint is visible in the reported
oxide-based Li–S cells. Bilayer and porous–dense–porous garnet
architectures reach sulfur loadings of several mg cm−2, but they
do so by adding a liquid electrolyte to wet the sulfur inside the
porous garnet layer.125,126 A LATP-based cell places polymer
layers between the ceramic and both electrodes, and in that
conguration polysuldes reduce the Ti-containing ceramic
unless the interface is protected by an atomic-layer-deposited
coating.127 A cell with an oxide-glass electrolyte inside the elec-
trodes still uses a sulde separator.128 Similarly, a garnet cell
cycled without applied pressure relies on a gel catholyte.129
Consequently, across all reported oxide-based Li–S systems, the
sulfur cathode is reached through a liquid, gel, polymer, or
sulde phase rather than through the oxide itself. These cells
therefore serve as comparators for interfacial chemistry and
cannot be read as evidence for dry oxide-based sulfur conver-
sion. The same contact problem appears in other conversion
cathodes on garnet, where a melt-penetrated conductive inter-
phase has been used to bond a FeF3 cathode to the rigid elec-
trolyte.130 The requirement for an engineered bonding layer is
therefore a general property of rigid oxides rather than a feature
of sulfur chemistry.

6.
Practical high-loading operation
and cell-level feasibility
The interfacial and transport limitations discussed above
become most consequential when high sulfur loading, a lean
solid-electrolyte fraction, and a low stack pressure are imposed
simultaneously. Conductive frameworks, Li2S-activation strate-
gies,
and
solid-electrolyte
interface-engineering
address
diﬀerent parts of the coupled electronic, ionic, kinetic, and
contact limitations, but their benets must ultimately persist
under these practical operating conditions. Whether these
advantages survive the transition to thick electrodes and
reduced external connement remains a central question for
cell-level feasibility.
Practical cell-level energy has been associated with sulfur
loadings in the approximately 5 mg cm−2 range or higher,
provided that high sulfur utilization and corresponding areal
capacity are maintained.39,141 Most reported all-solid-state
lithium–sulfur cells remain below this range, oen at sulfur
loadings below 2 mg cm−2.39,141 The exact threshold varies
among reports because cell-level energy estimates depend on
assumptions regarding sulfur utilization and inactive compo-
nent fractions; the 5 mg cm−2 range should therefore be
regarded as a practical benchmark rather than a universal
cutoﬀ.39,141 High sulfur loading alone, however, does not guar-
antee high cell-level energy density. The sulfur content of the
cathode, its electrochemical utilization, and the amount of
inactive material must also be considered. Raising the loading
lengthens both the electronic and ionic transport paths through
the cathode. Furthermore, it enlarges the volume of active
material undergoing S 4 Li2S conversion, thereby exacerbating
the mechanical and contact failures identied earlier as the
electrode thickens.41 High loading therefore acts not as an iso-
lated performance metric, but as a simultaneous test of
transport-network continuity, sulfur utilization, and chemo-
mechanical contact across the full cathode thickness.
Because high sulfur loading alone does not establish prac-
tical energy density, cell-level performance is better assessed by
considering several coupled design parameters rather than any
single record value. Four key parameters govern the full-cell
energy density: the sulfur mass loading, the sulfur mass frac-
tion of the cathode, the electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratio, and the
negative-to-positive electrode ratio. Together, these descriptors
account for both the amount of electrochemically active sulfur
and the mass penalty imposed by the remaining cell compo-
nents,143 thereby constraining the achievable gravimetric and
volumetric energy densities.41 A high capacity or areal capacity
measured in an individual laboratory cell is therefore insuﬃ-
cient to establish practicality if it is achieved with a large frac-
tion of inactive material.143 A cell-level specic energy near 500
Wh kg−1 is the frequently used practical target for evaluating
how these parameters must be balanced.143,144
Among these parameters, the electrolyte content is particu-
larly important for a thick solid-state cathode because reducing
inactive electrolyte mass directly competes with the need to
maintain continuous Li+ transport. In a liquid cell, lowering the
E/S ratio toward lean conditions is an established route to

7.
Design considerations and outlook
In all-solid-state Li–S batteries, reaction pathway and interfacial
transport are coupled through the same solid–solid reaction
environment, so improvements in kinetics or transport can
introduce new interfacial penalties.7,26 The three strategy classes
discussed in this review address diﬀerent parts of this coupling.
A conductive framework provides electronic conductivity while
preserving ionic access and contact to insulating sulfur and
Li2S.39 Li2S-activation strategies lower the kinetic barrier of
solid-state conversion through mediation, mixed conduction,
lattice modication, or catalysis.89 An interface-engineered SE
provides the ionic network while its chemistry and interfaces
inuence reaction-intermediate mobility, interfacial stability,
and therefore the dominant conversion pathway.12
Despite their diﬀerent functions, all three strategies face
a common requirement: transport and reaction must be
promoted selectively without enlarging the pathways for para-
sitic reactions or mobile intermediates. Increasing conductive-
framework contact can accelerate sulde-electrolyte decompo-
sition,39 while a mobile mediator can lower the Li2S oxidation
barrier at the cost of reintroducing mobile sulfur intermedi-
ates,92 and the sulde electrolyte itself remains susceptible to
decomposition at reactive interfaces.12,111 The design objective
is
therefore
selective
interfacial
connectivity—maximizing
electronic and ionic access at the S–C–SE reaction sites while
minimizing unnecessary reactive contact and restricting inter-
mediate mobility.39,54,62 This selectivity becomes increasingly
diﬃcult to maintain as sulfur loading and electrode thickness
increase and external stack pressure is reduced, because elec-
tronic
access,
ionic
continuity,
conversion
kinetics,
and
mechanical contact must then be preserved simultaneously.2,143
These design principles also require a corresponding hier-
archy of evidence for their evaluation. Voltage-prole shape
provides an accessible initial screen for possible pathway
changes; conductivity, impedance, pressure dependence, and
structural imaging assess whether the electronic, ionic, and
mechanical networks remain continuous; rm mechanistic
assignment requires direct analysis of reaction intermediates,
preferably complemented by operando or spatially resolved
measurements.

balanced or anode-free operation has also been reported,
although strictly anode-free all-solid-state Li–S cycling at prac-
tical areal capacity remains unestablished.156 The central gap is
therefore not the absence of a single high metric, but the lack of
long-term demonstrations that combine high sulfur loading,
low inactive-material fraction, reduced stack pressure, relevant
temperature,
and
limited
lithium
inventory
within
one
cell.89,124,143 Closing this gap requires the design principles
developed throughout this review to be satised simulta-
neously: useful S–C–SE contact should be maximized without
unnecessarily enlarging decomposition-prone interfaces; Li2S
activation should be accelerated without restoring long-range
intermediate mobility; and chemo-mechanical contact should
be maintained by the internal electrode architecture rather than
by high external pressure alone. These strategies should ulti-
mately be evaluated together under practical loading, stack
pressure, temperature, and lithium-inventory conditions, using
direct mechanistic measurements where pathway assignments
are made and clearly distinguishing calculated cell-level energy
from experimentally demonstrated cell performance.
