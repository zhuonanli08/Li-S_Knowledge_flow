ABSTRACT: Because lithium sulfide (Li2S) as an active material
is both ionically and electronically insulating, it is typically
combined with sulfide solid electrolytes (SSEs) and conductive
carbon in all-solid-state batteries to form a composite positive
electrode. However, the decomposition of the SSEs at the interface
causes capacity degradation. Among the many reported Li2S-based
composite positive electrodes, sulfide active materials incorporating
LiI specifically improve the battery performance by overcoming the
decomposition of SSEs. In this study, the interface between the
Li2S−LiI active material and SSE was analyzed, and the effects of
LiI on the formation of this interphase were elucidated. Structural
analyses revealed the formation of an amorphous interphase
comprising lithium, phosphorus, sulfur, and iodine on the SSE
surface during the initial charging process. This interphase contributed to the charge−discharge capacity and protected the pure SSE
inside it from excessive redox decomposition, which led to an improvement in the battery performance. These findings will facilitate
the design of all-solid-state batteries with sulfide-based active materials.
KEYWORDS: all-solid-state battery, solid electrolyte, lithium sulfide, interface, active material, lithium iodide
■INTRODUCTION
All-solid-state lithium-ion batteries are widely used owing to
their high energy density and safety, attributed to the inorganic
solid electrolytes in them, which have flame retardation
characteristics.1−3 Lithium sulfide (Li2S) is attractive as an
active material in the positive electrode because of its high
theoretical capacity (1167 mAh g−1). To compensate for the
low ionic and electronic conductivities of Li2S,4,5 it is usually
mixed with conductive additives such as sulfide solid
electrolytes (SSEs) and conductive carbon. Researchers have
significantly improved the performance of Li2S-based positive
electrodes for all-solid-state batteries, including their reversible
capacities, rate performance, and high areal capacities.6−20
Despite the enhancement of charge−discharge capacities
using the aforementioned mixing approach, the cycling stability
remains a concern. One reason for capacity degradation is the
decomposition of SSEs.21−26 The electrochemical window of
SSEs is narrow,27−31 and they readily undergo oxidation and
reduction during cycling, producing an insulating layer at the
interface.32 To overcome the high energy barrier required for
the activation of Li2S33,34 and maximize the capacity, most
Li2S-based all-solid-state cells are tested beyond the electro-
chemically stable potential of the SSEs. Therefore, it is crucial
to control the SSE/sulfide active material interphase.35−39
Among the many reported Li2S-based composite positive
electrodes, sulfide active materials incorporating LiI or iodine
compounds specifically improve the battery perform-
ance.17,40−47 In particular, Li2S−LiI solid solutions demon-
strate high charge−discharge capacities comparable to the
theoretical capacity as well as excellent cycling stability over
2000 cycles at 2C rate and 25 °C, even if the cell is operated
above a potential sufficient for the decomposition of SSEs.5,48
LiI promotes lithium diffusion and acts as an ion conduction
pathway to the active materials during cycling, resulting in an
increased charge−discharge capacity.46,49,50 In addition, LiI
enhances Li diffusion at the interface between Li2S and the
SSE.42 However, because SSEs have redox potentials similar to
that of Li2S, their decomposition should be promoted near the
interface with LiI. Nevertheless, cycle degradation caused by
the decomposition of SSEs does not occur significantly in the
cells containing LiI. The most likely reason is that a stable
interphase is formed as a protective layer between the sulfide

■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Synthesis. All procedures were conducted in a dry Ar atmosphere.
Li2S−LiI solid solutions were prepared using a mechanochemical
process. Li2S (99.9%, Mitsuwa Chemicals Co., Ltd.) and LiI
(99.999%, Sigma-Aldrich) at a molar ratio of 80:20 was placed in a
ZrO2 pot (45 mL) with 29 g of ZrO2 balls (4 mm diameter). Ball-
milling was performed using a planetary ball mill (Fritsch Pulverisette
7 classic line) for 10 h at 510 rpm. Li3PS4 (LPS) glass was prepared
via mechanical process. Li2S and P2S5 (99%, Sigma-Aldrich) at a
molar ratio of 75:25 was placed in a ZrO2 pot (250 mL) with 450 g of
ZrO2 balls (4 mm diameter). Ball-milling was performed using a
planetary ball mill (Fritsch Pulverisette 5 classic line) for 70 h at 210
rpm. 54Li3PS4·46LiI glass was prepared via mechanical process. Li2S,
P2S5, and LiI were placed in a ZrO2 pot (45 mL) with 90 g of ZrO2
balls (4 mm diameter). Ball-milling was performed using a planetary
ball mill (Fritsch Pulverisette 7 classic line) for 10 h at 510 rpm.

Composite positive electrodes were prepared through a two-step
process. First, active material and vapor-grown carbon fibers (VGCF,
Resonac HD. Co.) were mixed using an agate mortar and pestle for 15
min. The mixed sample was put in a ZrO2 pot (45 mL) with 29 g of
ZrO2 balls (4 mm diameter). Ball-milling was performed using a
planetary ball mill (Fritsch Pulverisette 7 classic line) for 10 h at 510
rpm. The milled sample was directly used as a positive electrode layer
in some cells. Second, an SSE (LPS or 54Li3PS4·46LiI) was added
using a ball mill (Pulverisette 23 Fritsch GmbH), which is a mild
condition equal to mixing using an agate mortar and pestle. The
weight ratio of Li2S−LiI or LiI to VGCF to SSE is 5:1:4. The weight
ratio of Li2S to VGCF to SSE is 3:1:6.
Material Characterization. SEM and energy-dispersive X-ray
spectroscopy (EDS) were performed using field-emission scanning
electron microscope (FE-SEM, SU-8220; Hitachi High- Technologies
Corp.) and an energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy system
(EMAXEvolution X-MAX, Horiba Ltd.). Cross sections of the pellet
were prepared using an Ar+ ion milling system (IM4000; Hitachi
High-Technologies Corp.). TEM observation was conducted using
the JEM-2100Plus (JEOL Co., Ltd.) at an accelerating voltage of 200
kV. The TEM sample was prepared by a focused ion beam (FIB)
apparatus (JIB-4700F, JEOL Co., Ltd.). The pellet pieces were
subjected to a Ga+ beam treatment to prepare thin films. The resulting
films had a cross-section of 8 μm × 6 μm and a thickness of 100 nm.
During the TEM observations, a cryo vacuum transfer TEM holder
(Mel-Build) was used to avoid exposing the samples to air. The
sample was stabilized at −165 °C using liquid nitrogen. A
complementary metal−oxide−semiconductor camera (OneView,
Gatan Inc.) was used to record and prevent specimen drift. The
microstructures of the sample were examined by recording patterns.
XRD measurements were performed using an X-ray diffractometer
(SmartLab, Rigaku) with Cu Kα radiation. Powder and pellet samples
were sealed in an airtight container with a polymer and Be film
window, respectively. Diffraction data were collected in the 2θ range
of 5°−80° at a scan rate of 10° min−1 with a step size of 0.02°. LiI was
ball-milled at 510 rpm for 10 h using a planetary ball mill (Fritsch
Pulverisette 7 classic line).
7Li and
31P solid-state MAS NMR
experiments were performed by using an NMR spectrometer (JNM-
ECZL500G; JEOL). The sample powders were packed into zirconia
spinners under a dry Ar atmosphere. The observation frequencies of
7Li and 31P were 194.4 and 202.5 MHz, respectively. The spectra were
acquired by a single pulse with a width of 7Li of 3.08 and 31P of 2.33
μs (equivalent to the 90° pulse width). The recycled pulse delays for
the
7Li and
31P MAS NMR measurements were 5 and 10 s,
respectively. The MAS was set to 20 kHz. The chemical shifts of 7Li
and 31P NMR spectra were calibrated using 1.0 M LiCl aq (at 0.0
ppm) and 85% H3PO4 aq (at 0.0 ppm), respectively. Hydrate (LiI·
x(H2O), Alfa Aesar) was grounded by an agate mortar and pestle for
measurements. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measure-
ments were conducted using an XPS instrument (K-Alpha, Thermo
Fisher Scientific) with a monochromatic Al Kα source (1486.6 eV).
Ar+ etching was performed to remove surface impurities. The peaks
were calibrated using the C 1s peak (284.7 eV).
Electrochemical Testing. Charge−discharge measurements were
performed using a BTS-2004 or BTS-2005 charge−discharge device
(Nagano Co.). The alternating-current (AC) impedance for all-solid-
state cells was measured using an impedance analyzer (SI-1287,
Solartron Analytical) at 105 Hz to 0.01 Hz and 10 mV. The AC
impedance for SSEs was measured using an impedance analyzer (SI-
1260, Solartron Analytical) at 10 MHz to 0.1 Hz and 10 mV. SSEs
were pelletized by pressing at 360 MPa for 5 min. Both sides of the
pellet surface were covered with Au as a current collector. The mean
diffusion coefficient (D·R−2) was calculated from the galvanostatic
intermittent titration technique (GITT) profiles.
Cell Assembly. All procedures were conducted in a dry Ar
atmosphere. An SSE (80 mg) was placed in a polycarbonate cylinder
(10 mm diameter) and pressed at a few MPa for a few seconds. The
composite positive electrode was placed on the separator layer and
pressed together at 360 MPa for 5 min. The weight was ca. 10 mg for
the composite containing the SSE and ca. 5 mg for the composite not
containing the SSE. A Li−In alloy, prepared by touching a lithium foil
(6 mm diameter and 0.25 mm thickness) to an indium foil (8 mm
diameter and 0.3 mm thickness), was used as a negative electrode and
reference electrode. The lithium and indium foils were placed on the
separator layer, which was another surface with the positive
composites, and then pressed at approximately 36 MPa for 2 min.
Stainless steel rods as current collectors were placed on either side of
the prepared three-layer pellet. The assembled cell was tightened as
much as possible. The stack pressure is over 50 MPa.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Figure 1a schematically shows the all-solid-state cell and the
target interphase. The composite positive electrode comprised
a Li2S−LiI solid solution, VGCF, and LPS glass. LPS glass was
selected as a representative SSE owing to its simple
composition and excellent ductility.51,52 Figure 1b shows the
charge−discharge curves obtained at C/20 rate and 25 °C
using the GITT, which can promote a uniform reaction.53,54
Figure S1 shows differences in the charge−discharge behavior
due to different charge−discharge conditions. By decreasing
the charge−discharge rate and increasing the temperature, the
overpotential is lowered as much as possible, leading to the
appearance of the hidden plateau. Figure S2 shows the cells
with Li2S−VGCF−LPS and LiI−VGCF−LPS as a comparison.
The capacity components in the cells using Li2S−LiI−VGCF−
LPS were determined by comparing the cells using Li2S−LiI−
VGCF−LPS with the cells using Li2S−VGCF−LPS and LiI−
VGCF−LPS, as well as by further reviewing the liter-
ature5,23−25,30,49,55 (details are discussed in the Supporting
Information). The state of charge (SOC) was calculated by
dividing the obtained capacity by the theoretical capacity of
Li2S. In the cells using Li2S−LiI−VGCF−LPS, the oxidation of
Li2S dominated the capacity until SOC80% (approximately
270 mAh g−1 based on the weight of the positive electrode,
corresponding to 930 mAh g−1 based on the weight of the
Li2S). The oxidation of LiI became dominant after SOC80%.
However, note that the contributions of all components cannot
be precisely determined, because the interphase formation,
decomposition of SSE, and oxidation of lithium polysulfides
should also contribute to the charge capacity.23,25,47,49,55 In
addition, an irreversible capacity was observed, because the
oxidation products of Li2S (such as polysulfides) may not have
been completely reduced.
To observe the interphase formation, the all-solid-state cells
were disassembled after reaching SOC80%, the first full charge,
and the first full discharge for SEM observations. Figure 1c−f
shows the cross-sectional SEM images of the positive electrode
layers during the initial cycle. Although only two contrasts
derived from Li2S−LiI−VGCF and LPS were detected before
cycling (indicated by AM and SE, respectively), another
contrast derived from the interphase appeared after reaching
SOC80% (indicated as “Interphase”). This interphase was
formed only in a thin layer ranging from several hundred
nanometers to several microns on the surface of LPS particles.
Also, its detection after the first discharge suggests that it was
maintained during cycling. The thickness of the interphase
does not appear to be changed after discharging. Figures 1g
and S3 show the EDS mappings after the first full charge.
Interestingly, in addition to phosphorus and sulfur, iodine was
also distributed in the interphase. This indicates that iodine
was supplied from the Li2S−LiI−VGCF particles and
integrated into the interphase. This interphase must have
been formed during cycling rather than during the preparation
of cross-section or other experimental steps, since it was not

Next, TEM analysis was carried out on specific cells to
observe the interphase in detail. The cell of (Li−In/LPS/

interphase was formed between the LPS separator and Li2S−

specific areas including three layers: the electrode, interphase,
and the LPS separator. Figure 2c shows a bright field image,

2d−f shows the corresponding selected area electron

crystallization of LPS was observed during the charging

the low-temperature cooling caused by the cryo-TEM holder.
The interphase had a mostly amorphous structure. The
diffraction spots observed in the SAED pattern of interphase
are thought to originate from the adjacent crystallized LPS. In
addition, the SAED pattern of the Li2S−LiI−VGCF electrode
showed amorphous structures because Li2S was converted to
amorphous sulfur or lithium polysulfides during charging. LiI is
sensitive to high electron beam for the TEM observations and
LiI crystals are considered to disappear. Based on the SEM
images in Figure 1, we conclude that the interphase was an
amorphous (or glass) material composed of Li, P, S, and I.

To obtain further structural information on the Li2S−LiI−
VGCF−LPS composite during cycling, XRD and 31P and 7Li
MAS NMR analyses were conducted. Figures 3a and S6 show
the XRD patterns obtained during cycling. The charge−
discharge mechanism of Li2S−LiI−VGCF has been previously
investigated,47,49 and the XRD patterns in this study showed
changes similar to those in the previous report. The difference
between ref.47,49 and this study is the contact area between
Li2S−LiI−VGCF and LPS, and more interphase formation is
expected in this study. Nevertheless, no new peaks were
detected in the XRD pattern, confirming that the interphase
was amorphous, in agreement with the TEM observations.
Figure 3b,c shows the 31P and 7Li MAS NMR spectra before
and after cycling. PS4
3−was mainly detected in all 31P MAS
NMR spectra, indicating that LPS was not dramatically
oxidized or reduced because of the interphase formed around
it. Peaks originating from the interphase were not detected,
because of either their low abundance ratio or overlap with the
peak of PS4
3−. In the 7Li MAS NMR spectra before cycling,
peaks derived from Li2S−LiI and LPS were observed at
approximately 1.8 and 2.2 ppm, respectively.42,56,57 After
charging, the peak of Li2S−LiI disappeared, and those of LiI
and LiI hydrate appeared at approximately −4.7 and −0.6 ppm
(see Figure S7), and the latter two phases were also observed
in XRD. In addition, a new peak assigned as the interphase was
observed at approximately −1.6 ppm. Considering the
presence of a chemical shift between the peaks of LPS and
LiI, Li in the interphase should exist in an intermediate
environment consisting of LPS and LiI. Similar peaks were
previously reported in the GeS2−Ga2S3−LiI system.58 After
discharge, the peaks of LiI, LiI·H2O, and LPS remained, and
that of Li2S−LiI reappeared. In addition, the peak derived from
the interphase disappeared, suggesting that the interphase was
reduced during discharge and contributed to the discharge
capacity. During subsequent charging and discharging, this
interphase was oxidized and reduced similar to the active
material, and pure LPS inside the interphase was protected
from further redox decomposition.

VGCF−LPS composite, and Figure S8 shows the S 2p, P 2p,
and I 3d spectra before and after charging and discharging. The
S 2p spectra confirmed the redox of S species. The P 2p
spectra slightly shifted to the higher binding energy, indicating
that LPS was partially oxidized. In addition, the I 3d spectrum

Based on the above experiments, we propose the charge−
discharge mechanism of the interphase shown in Scheme 1. An
amorphous interphase composed of Li, P, S, and I was formed
on the surface of SSE particles until SOC80% with iodine
supplied from the Li2S−LiI−VGCF particles. The amorphous
interphase was related to LPS−LiI glass (LPS-LiI). The LPS−
LiI glass improved the tolerance to reduction with Li metal and
enhanced the Li dissolution/deposition cycling perform-
ance.59,60 Therefore, the interphase acts as a protective layer
to prevent further decomposition of LPS. Both Li2S−LiI−
VGCF and the interphase were oxidized and reduced
continuously. Thus, LiI promoted the formation of an
amorphous interphase on the SSE surface, enhanced the
diffusion of lithium ions, contributed slightly to the charge−
discharge capacity, and prevented further decomposition of the
SSE.
The results discussed above demonstrated that an interphase
was formed on the surface of bulk LPS. It was consequently
inferred that the interphase would also be formed when LiI is
already present in the solid electrolyte rather than in the active
material of the composite positive electrode. To provide
further evidence of how LiI affects the interphase formation,
we compared the performance of cells using Li2S−VGCF−LPS
and Li2S−VGCF−LPS−LiI composites as the positive electro-
des. The XRD pattern and Nyquist plots of the LPS−LiI
sample are shown in Figure S11. Figure 4a displays the Li2S
cells with LPS and LPS−LiI, and Figure 4b shows their
charge−discharge curves. Both cells demonstrated an
enhanced capacity with each cycle due to the contribution of
the SSE to the capacity. After 20 cycles, the cell with LPS−LiI
exhibited a slightly higher capacity and lower overvoltage than
that with LPS. This suggests that the formed interphase
contributed slightly to the capacity and enhanced Li diffusion.
Figure 4c,d shows the Nyquist plots of both cells after the first
and 20th charge. After the 20th cycle, the cell with LPS-LiI
showed a lower resistance in the low-frequency range than that
with LPS. The resistance component in the low-frequency
range originates from lithium diffusion in the positive
electrode.24,61 In addition, the presence of LiI improves the
lithium diffusion,49 and possibly the interphase assists the
lithium diffusion after cycling. Thus, LiI formed the interphase
even though it was only included in the SSEs, confirming the
effectiveness of LiI.24,61
■CONCLUSION
The interphase between Li2S−LiI active materials and SSEs
was investigated to clarify why positive electrodes with sulfide
active materials and iodine compounds have enhanced
electrochemical capacity, including charge−discharge and
cycle performances. Structural analysis using SEM, TEM,
XRD, and NMR confirmed that an amorphous interphase
consisting of Li, P, S, and I was formed on the LPS surface
during the initial charging process. The interphase was
suspected to be an LPS−LiI glass-like material. This interphase
contributed to the charge−discharge capacity and protected
the pure LPS inside it from excessive redox decomposition for
continuous cycling, leading to high capacity, high rate, and
stable cycle life. Nevertheless, the precise structure, diffusion
coefficient, and ionic conductivity of the interfacial phase
remain unknown, and there is scope for further discussion of
the role of the interface by extracting only the interphase and
conducting a detailed analysis. These results could further
facilitate the design of artificial solid electrolyte interphases and
advance our understanding of the role these interphases play in
all-solid-state batteries with sulfide-based active materials.
