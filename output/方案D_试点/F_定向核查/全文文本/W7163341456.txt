Unlocking the kinetics of solid-state Li-S batteries by redox mediation

Rapid development of 3C (computer, communication and con-
sumer electronics) devices, electrical vehicles (EVs) and artiﬁcial 
intelligence (AI) technologies is calling for next-generation energy 
storage systems to surpass the limitations of commercial Li ion 
batteries including energy density, cost and sustainability. Among
the various candidates, Li-S batteries have been attracting signiﬁ-
cant attention due to their exceptional energy density, low cost
of sulfur, and environmental friendliness.
However, in conventional liquid electrolyte-based Li-S batter-
ies, the notorious ‘‘polysulﬁdes shuttling”, especially under lean
electrolyte conditions, hinders further development of Li-S batter-
ies [1]. To mitigate these challenges, solid-state Li-S batteries 
(SSLSBs) have emerged as promising candidates. In addition to 
their potential of suppressing the shuttle effect and mitigating 
the risk of thermal runaway in solid-state conﬁgurations, SSLSBs 
can also deliver a theoretical energy density of approximately 
542.7 Wh kg−1 when achieving a practical sulfur utilization of 
90% and a sulfur content of 60 wt% within the composite cathode 
as well as an average operating voltage of 2.0 V. With further opti-
mization on the battery system such as reducing the thickness of
electrolyte and increasing sulfur content, the practical energy den-
sity of SSLSBs can even exceed 600 Wh kg−1. Furthermore, the low
cost of sulfur ($108 per metric ton) compared Co and Ni (thousands
of US dollars per metric ton) provides a considerable economic
advantage, rendering S an attractive cathode material in a cost-
effective battery [2]. 
The exploration of SSLSBs can be traced back to 1986, when 
polyethylene oxide (PEO) was employed as an electrolyte in Li-S
battery. PEO-based SSLSBs facilitate a multistep redox process
involving
the
formation
of
soluble
long-chain
polysulﬁdes
(Fig. 1a). Consequently, PEO-based systems remain susceptible to 
the polysulﬁdes shuttling, undermining the advantages associated 
with solid-state conﬁgura tions. To mitigate these limitations, the
practice of SSLSBs with inorganic solid-state electrolytes (SSEs)
started [3]. Inorganic SSEs not only offer higher ionic conductivity 
but also enable a one-step ‘‘solid-solid” conversion directly from S
to Li2S (Fig. 1b). The unique sulfur conversion process makes it 
more feasible to achieve the ‘‘shuttle free” and high-performance
SSLSBs. However, the one-step solid-solid conversion process is 
always accompanied by sluggish sulfur redox kinetics, due to the 
large energy barrier of direct S/Li2S conversion. These kinetics lim-
itations are particularly pronounced under harsh conditions such 
as high C rates and low operation temperatures, severely restrict-
ing the application scenarios of SSLSBs. As a result, research atten-
tion in recent years has increasingly shifted towards to the
optimization of sulfur redox kinetics in SSLSBs.

Redox mediators (RMs), which are reversibly oxidized and 
reduced during the electrochemical process and serve as interme-
diate electron carriers, are expected to promote sulfur species con-
version and sulfur utilization by changing sulfur redox chemistries
[4]. An effective RM serves as an intermediate electron carrier or 
reservoir without changing the ﬁnal products. In Li-S batteries, 
the energy barrier of the electrochemical process can be signiﬁ-
cantly decreased in the presence of RMs (Fig. 1c). 
Long-chain polysulﬁdes, which are known as the primary cause 
of the shuttle effect, are revisited as effective RMs in Li-S batteries
[5]. Therefore, PEO and its derivatives-based Li-S batteries continue 
to attract much attention. The formation and diffusion of polysul-
ﬁdes offer more accessible redox active sites; meanwhile, they can 
chemically oxidize/reduce the sulfur species during the electro-
chemical process, enabling smooth energy delivery. An average 
discharge potential of around 2.2 V shows the promise of unlocking 
the full energy potential of SSLSBs. However, the uncontrollable
dissolution of polysulﬁdes in PEO signiﬁcantly decreases PEO’s
coordination number, leading to hindered Li ions transport. As a
result, the electrochemical process can be potentially impaired or
even ceased, resulting in capacity decay and even cell failure.
To further optimize the sulfur redox kinetics and alleviate the 
negative effect from polysulﬁdes, extra RMs beyond polysulﬁdes 
are necessary. Typical quinone-based organic materials, featuring
redox-active C=O groups, have been reported as promising cathode
materials [6]. With moderate working potential in the range of 2 to 
3 V, they are promising to serve effectively as RMs in Li-S batteries.
To achieve effective redox mediation of quinone-based organic
materials in solid-state conﬁguration, Cui and co-workers [7] 
explored 
1,5-bis(2-(2-(2-methoxyethoxy)ethoxy)ethoxy)anthra-
9,10-quinone (AQT) as RM in PEO-based SSLSBs. With desirable 
redox potential and solubility in PEO solution (Fig. 1d), AQT 
enables high efﬁciency of redox mediation. In the presence of

In terms of the inorganic SSEs-based SSLSBs, the rigid nature of 
inorganic SSEs and elimination of polysulﬁdes RMs poses addi-
tional challenges of charge transfer. These limitations make it more 
imperative to optimize the sulfur redox kinetics in inorganic SSE-
based systems. However, unlike solvent-like PEO, inorganic SSEs 
allow negligible dissolution and diffusivity of RMs, compromising 
the effectiveness of conventional redox mediation strategies. Inter-
facial redox mediation, which depends on the immobilized RMs at
the cathode/electrolyte interface to promote the charge transfer,
has emerged as a more promising strategy for redox mediation
in inorganic SSEs based systems. It has been reported that VS2-
based solid-state Li-ion batteries showed impressive performance
and redox peaks in the range of 1.5 to 3 V (vs. Li/Li+) [8]. Addition-
ally, both LiVS2 and VS2 feature low Li+ ions barrier and excellent 
electronic conductivity. These properties make LiVS2/VS2 a promis-
ing candidate to be an effective RM. The initial attempts using S/
VS2 composite as cathode [9], which was prepared by a simple 
melt-diffusion technique, showed the feasibility of VS2 as RM to 
work with high active material loading of 7.7 mg cm−2. Building
on this, Li2S-LiVS2 core-shell conﬁguration (Fig. 1e and f), in which 
the 30-nm LiVS2 shell served as charge carrier and electron transfer
mediator, was designed to further boost sulfur redox kinetics [10]. 
Optimized microstructure effectively facilitate s the sulfur redox
kinetics and enables a stable capacity around 2.5 mAh cm−2 for 
50 cycles under high mass loading of 6 mg cm−2.

Besides, halide-based additives like LiI also hold the promise to 
function as RMs in SSLSBs due to the potential iodine redox chem-
istry [11]. Wagemaker et al. [12] found that the LiI coating can 
facilitate the Li ions exchange between the electrode and elec-
trolyte. Additionally, Kim et al. [13] found LiI can effectively lower 
the activation barrier for the molecular conversion from S to Li2S2/ 
Li2S, leading to optimized redox kinetics and remarkable reversibil-
ity (82% to 100%). More recently, rather than adding LiI as extra
additives, iodine has been directly incorporated into sulfur to form
an S9.3I compound as cathode material [14]. By using a simple 
grinding and heating procedure, S9.3I achieved an outstanding con-
ductivity of 5.9 × 10−7 S cm−1 , which is approximately 11 times 
greater than sulfur. Additionally, the low melting point around 
65 °C of S9.3I induces better interfacial compatibility between cath-
ode and SSEs particles, addressing the contact issue in solid-state
conﬁguration and enabling lower operation pressure of SSLSBs.
As a result, the Li-S9.3I battery demonstrated impressive capacity
in a wide temperature range from 25 to 100 °C under a facile pres-
sure of 30 MPa.
However, in the above cases, iodine functions as a catalyst 
rather than RMs, since no evident redox behavior associated with 
iodine species is observed during the charge/discharge process of
SSLSBs. Further optimization and modiﬁcation are required to
effectively activate the stable iodine redox chemistry, enabling LiI
to function as an active RM. Pang et al. [15] designed a lithium

In summary, RMs, which offer alternative electron-transfer 
pathways and potentially alter the thermodynamic reaction path 
of sulfur redox, have demonstrated strong potential for modulating 
sulfur redox chemistry and accelerating sulfur redox kinetics. An 
effective RM in SSLSBs should generally satisfy three fundamental 
criteria: (1) stable electrochemical cyclability to enable long-term 
redox mediation, (2) proper redox potential to enable smooth
charge transfer or oxidize/reduce sulfur species, and (3) good com-
patibility towards electrolytes to avoid side reactions and enable
long-term stability. Beyond these basic requirements, the ability
of an RM to facilitate sulfur redox reactions is strongly relevant
to the speciﬁc solid-state systems employed (Fig. 2). 
In PEO-based solid-state systems, due to the solvent-like prop-
erties of PEO, RMs are generally required to be soluble in the elec-
trolyte so that they can diffuse and create more effective RMs-
carbon and RMs-sulfur interfaces, thereby accelerating both their
own redox reaction and sulfur conversion. A variety of organic
materials, especially quinone-based materials, reveal moderate 
redox potentials (1.5–3 V vs. Li/Li+ ) as cathode, which are suitable 
to be RMs for sulfur redox mediation. Additionally, organic species 
allow precise regulation of redox potential and solubility in poly-
mer matrix through introducing electron-withdrawing or elec-
tron-rich functional groups, offering substantial design ﬂexibility 
for RMs optimization. However, it should be noted that the disso-
lution of RMs inevitably induces trade-offs: excessive dissolution 
and diffusion of RMs may hinder the Li-ions transport and lead 
to extra shuttling, which must be considered in future exploration. 
Besides, the redox mediation in the PEO system shows promise for 
extension to interfacial redox mediation, due to the dissolution and 
diffusion of polysulﬁdes in PEO. In this context, the organic small
molecules can be used to form the 2D or 3D COFs, in which the
remaining carbonyl groups preserve the desired redox potential
and enable faster ion transport, while the dissolved sulfur species
can effectively interact with the undissolved COFs, enabling efﬁ-
cient redox mediation. Furthermore, the introduction of additional
O
and

ﬁdes, which were previously employed as redox comediator in
liquid-based Li-S batteries, also hold the promise for extending
their capability to SSLSBs.

In contrast, in solid-state systems (including other polymer 
SSEs like poly(vinylideneﬂuoride) (PVDF)), the molecular mobility 
of RMs is strongly suppressed in solid-state networks. And the rigid 
particle-particle contact induces more signiﬁcant steric hindrance 
and spatial constraints. Therefore, compared with PEO-based sys-
tems, in addition to the compatibility of the thermodynamic 
potential window between SSE and RMs, the RMs should possess 
strong interfacial compatibility and localized redox capability. 
Interfacial redox mediation, in which RMs are immobilized at the
active material/SSE interface, is a more effective pathway for inor-
ganic-based systems. In addition to the desirable working poten-
tial, both Li ions and electron transport efﬁciency of RMs should
be taken into consideration to achieve sufﬁcient redox mediation.
However, the limited structure tunability of inorganic materials
signiﬁcantly narrows the pool of viable candidates, with examples

Besides, halide-based compounds, especially iodine-based 
materials, are discussed as a potentially effective RM. Nonetheless, 
when LiI is simply added into a solid-state cathode composite, its 
electrochemical redox reaction generally relies on a LiI-carbon-
solid electrolyte three-phase interface, which still involves a sub-
stantial kinetic barrier. It leads to insufﬁcient iodine redox. This sit-
uation changes when iodine species are integrated into the SSEs
[15]. In this case, iodine species can be oxidized/reduced at the 
SSE/carbon two-phase interface, rather than depending on the 
LiI-carbon-SSE three-phase contact. Additionally, the solid elec-
trolyte itself is a fast Li+ conductor; the surface iodine species 
can be preferentially oxidized during charging under high C rate 
conditions, where direct electrochemical oxidation of Li2S suffers 
from a large overpotential. The oxidized iodine species (I2/I3 
− ) can 
then chemically oxidize Li2S and thus mediate sulfur conversion. 
In addition to the materials optimization, the rigid interfacial con-
tact inherent to inorganic SSEs-based systems and the large vol-
ume change in sulfur batteries remain critical challenges to make
full use of RMs. To overcome this challenge, developing more
advanced cathode fabrication techniques that enable uniform dis-
tribution of RMs and intimate interfacial contact is essential to
unlock the full potential of interfacial redox mediation. Addition-
ally, future research should place greater emphasis on monitoring
the evolution of internal microstructure in SSLSBs during cycling to
unveil the possible failure mechanism of RMs due to the loss of suf-
ﬁcient contact.
Finally, for the practical application of RMs in long-term SSLSBs, 
their possible failure and passivation mechanisms must also be 
carefully considered. First, it is related to the cyclability of RM 
itself. Its redox kinetics and electrochemical stability are impor-
tant. Second, an RM should be chemically inert towards elec-
trolytes to avoid side reactions, and the reversibility after 
reacting with sulfur species should be maintained. Third, the vol-
ume change associated with S8-Li2S conversion may induce void 
formation and loss of interfacial contact and thereby disable RM.
Additionally, Future studies should evaluate RMs under more prac-
tical and stringent conditions to avoid overstating their actual con-
tribution to SSLSBs. In particular, testing under low operating stack
pressure (<5 MPa), low RMs content, and practically relevant sulfur
loadings and areal capacities is essential for a realistic assessment
of mediation effects. Such evaluation would help distinguish the
genuine improvements derived from redox mediation from those
mainly enabled by favorable testing conditions, thereby providing
a more reliable benchmark for the practical development of RMs in
SSLSBs.
