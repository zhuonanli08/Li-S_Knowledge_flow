OPEN ACCESS | Award lecture
Development of carbon materials for sulfur cathodes in
inorganic-based solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries
Zhao Yang, Qihang Yu, Anna Thinphang-nga, and Xia Li
Department of Chemical and Materials Engineering, Concordia University, Montreal, QC, Canada
Corresponding author: Xia Li (email: xia.li@concordia.ca)
Abstract
Inorganic-based solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries (SSLSBs) with high energy density and high safety have attracted wide
attention as they are one of the most promising energy storage devices to meet future market requirements. However, the
development of SSLSBs faces various challenges due to the unreasonable structural design in sulfur cathodes. Carbon is one
of the indispensable components in sulfur cathodes. The rational design of carbon materials becomes an important strategy
to address the challenges in sulfur cathodes. This review summarizes recent literature about the design and application of
carbon materials for sulfur cathodes in inorganic-based SSLSBs. It starts with the introduction of different carbon materials
from zero-dimensional to three-dimensional carbon materials. Particularly, this review paper highlights the structural design
of carbon materials and the cathode fabrication methods, toward improving the conductivity of cathodes, buffering volume
changes in cathodes, reducing interfacial resistance among cathode components, and increasing the mass loading of active
materials. Finally, the existing challenges and promising solutions for carbon materials in the cathodes are discussed and
proposed.
Key words: carbon materials, sulfur cathodes, solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries, structural design, cathode fabrication meth-
ods
1. Introduction
The increasing fossil fuel consumption and rapid economic
development have ignited global interest in renewable en-
ergy technologies, such as solar and wind energy sources.
Electrochemical energy storage devices are essential for the
power supply system to achieve an efficient and sustainable
supply of these intermittent clean energy sources. Among
various rechargeable batteries, lithium-ion batteries (LIBs),
with lithium metal oxides (e.g., LiCoO2) as the cathodes and
graphite as the anodes, have been commercialized and ap-
plied in electric vehicles and portable electric devices for
decades.1,2 However, with years of research and develop-
ment, the performance of LIBs has almost reached the theo-
retical limits of their electrode materials, which inhibits the
applications of LIBs, especially where high energy density (in-
cluding the gravimetric and volumetric energy densities) or
high power density are required.3 Therefore, searching for
an alternative to LIBs to break through the bottleneck has
attracted unprecedented attention.4,5
Beyond LIBs, lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries have been
considered one of the most promising candidates among
various explored batteries due to their excellent superior-
ity. Sulfur cathode and lithium anode of the Li–S battery
have ultrahigh theoretical gravimetric capacities of 1672
and 3860 mAh g−1; thus, the Li–S battery is capable of de-
livering much higher energy densities (2500 W h kg−1 and
2800 W h L−1) than insertion-type electrodes-based LIBs (typi-
cal theoretical energy densities are 420 W h kg−1 and 1400 W
h L−1, respectively) even at a relatively lower working volt-
age (average cell voltage of 2.15 V) than conventional LIBs
(3.7 V).6–9 In addition, the abundance, cost-effectiveness, and
nontoxicity of sulfur in nature make it promising for large-
scale application. In recent years, tremendous accomplish-
ment has been achieved by organic liquid electrolyte-based
Li–S batteries. Some literature reported the liquid-based Li–S
batteries with a very promising battery life,10–12 as well as a
good performance with high sulfur loading for practical ap-
plications.13,14
Nevertheless, the development of safe and high-energy Li–S
batteries still faces many challenges. The use of organic liquid
electrolytes brings severe concern in practical applications.
What’s worse, the dissolution of intermediate product poly-
sulﬁdes in the liquid electrolyte will migrate from cathode to
anode, and react with the lithium metal anode, resulting in
the severe loss of sulfur active materials as well as the highly
insulated interface at the anode. In addition, the lithium den-
drite induced by the uneven deposition of lithium ions is an-
other severe issue that needs to be addressed.
In the past few years, many strategies have been devoted
to addressing the aforementioned challenges, such as the ap-
plication of polar catalysts in the cathodes,15,16 the develop-
ment of novel interface materials,17 and the fabrication of

the use of inorganic-based SSEs. However, despite these su-
perior advantages and promising prospects, solid-state Li–S
batteries (SSLSBs) still face many challenges and the commer-
cialization of SSLSBs still has a long way to go. Especially some
intractable drawbacks of sulfur cathodes severely inhibit the
improvement of SSLSBs performance, including (1) the insu-
lating nature of S and Li2S. Both the charging product S and
discharging product Li2S have low electrical conductivity (5
× 10−30 and 3.6 × 10−7 S cm−1) and slow Li+ transport, lead-
ing to low active materials utilization and limited reaction
kinetics.23 (2) Large volume change (>80%) of S/Li2S will oc-
cur during the charge/discharge process, due to different den-
sities of S and Li2S (2.07 and 1.66 g cm−3).23 The cathode is
composed of active materials (typically S or Li2S), electronic
conductors (typically carbon materials), and Li-ion conductor
SSEs. The severe volume change will cause the detachment of
active materials from carbon materials and SSEs and result
in the blockage of electrons and Li+ transport paths, lead-
ing to severe polarization and capacity decay. (3) High inter-
facial resistance between active materials and electronic and
ionic conductors. To achieve fast electronic and ionic trans-
port in cathodes, a large contact area and intimate solid–solid
contact in S (or Li2S)/carbon materials/SSEs triple phases are
required. However, in the cathode of SSLSBs, the contact be-
tween the three components is point-to-point contact, which
greatly limits the electrochemical kinetics. For this reason,
constructing an intimate contact in S (or Li2S)/carbon mate-
rials/SSEs triple-phase mixture is one of the most effective
strategies.
Carbon materials have become the most popular host ma-
terials for sulfur-based cathodes, owing to their high elec-
tronic conductivity, large pore volume for buffering the volu-
metric changes of S/Li2S, and enabling high sulfur mass load-
ing. Furthermore, the shuttle effect of polysulﬁdes can be
effectively avoided thanks to the physical/chemical conﬁne-
ment effect of carbon materials on polysulﬁdes. Therefore,
the design of carbon materials in sulfur cathodes is essen-
tial for the development of SSLSBs. In this review, we sum-
marized recent progress on the classiﬁcation, functions, and
effects of various carbon materials for the sulfur-based cath-
odes in the inorganic SSEs-based SSLSBs. First, we brieﬂy dis-
cussed the carbon materials applied in the electrochemical
energy storage ﬁeld and in conventional liquid electrolyte-
based Li–S batteries. After that, the recent progress of carbon
materials applied in SSLSBs is summarized and highlighted.
Especially, we put emphasis on the functional and rational
design of carbon materials toward the challenges of sulfur
cathodes, such as the improvement of electronic conductiv-
ity, the accommodation of volumetric changes, and the de-
crease of interfacial resistance in the cathodes; related re-
search works are discussed in detail. Furthermore, the chal-
lenges of carbon materials in the application of SSLSBs are
discussed. Finally, we concluded with a summary and pro-
vided our perspectives on the future directions of sulfur cath-
odes in SSLSBs. We hope this review can bring comprehensive
insights into the design and application of carbon materials
for the development of solid-state batteries.
2. Carbon materials in batteries
Carbon materials have been widely used in energy stor-
age and conversion devices, such as the electronic conductors
in supercapacitors, and functional catalysts and catalyst sup-
port materials in fuel cells.24–26 Carbon materials have also
been widely investigated and applied in battery devices. Fur-
thermore, especially in LIBs, graphite carbon materials are
the most important and popular anode materials in the mar-
ket due to their high electron conductivity, high Li-ion stor-
age capacity, and low cost in fabrication.24,27 In addition to
graphite anode materials, advanced carbon materials have
also been widely applied in next-generation batteries beyond
LIBs. For instance, carbon materials have been widely em-
ployed in Li–S batteries from cathodes, separators, to an-
odes.28,29 In the cathodes of Li–S batteries, the carbon mate-
rials are the most potential sulfur host materials due to their
high conductivity, various pore structure, and high surface
areas. Many research works have been devoted to investigat-
ing and optimizing the pore structure, pore size, composi-
tion, and surface modiﬁcation of carbon materials to achieve
physical/chemical conﬁnement/adsorption of lithium poly-
sulﬁdes and volumetric buffering of S/Li2S.28,29 Moreover, the
rational structural design of carbon materials is essential to
achieve high-loading sulfur cathodes.30 For the anodes of Li–S
batteries, while ensuring fast lithium ions transport, carbon
materials are used to protect the lithium anode from side re-
actions with the organic electrolyte and polysulﬁdes to avoid
insulating layers and lithium dendrite.31 The carbon materi-
als with high surface area and tunable pore size can be con-
tributed to modiﬁed separators,29 thus preventing dissolved
lithium polysulﬁdes from shuttling from cathode to anode.
Recently, the development of SSLSBs has been increasing
extensive attention due to their potential to achieve high
safety and high energy density over conventional liquid-state
Li–S batteries. Carbon materials are one of the most impor-
tant components in both liquid and SSLSBs. However, the
structural design strategies of carbon materials are signiﬁ-
cantly different in both. Liquid-state Li–S batteries suffer from
severe lithium polysulﬁde shuttling problems; thus, appro-
priate porous carbon structures are designed to physically
conﬁne the diffusion of soluble lithium polysulﬁdes. Also,
the carbon hosts with polar surfaces have been developed to
chemically anchor lithium polysulﬁdes. Nevertheless, SSLSBs
are exempt from this problem because of the use of SSEs.
Compared to liquid-state Li–S batteries, SSLSBs face new chal-
lenges. In the absence of liquid-state electrolytes as the inter-
facial modiﬁcation layer, the rigid SSEs contact with cathode
materials and cathode by point-to-point contacts. The Li+/e−
transformation across the interfaces is limited, leading to low

Carbon materials can be classiﬁed into three categories
by dimension, from zero-dimensional (0D), one-dimensional
(1D), two-dimensional (2D), to three-dimensional (3D). These
various nanostructured carbons have been demonstrated as
excellent components to serve high-performance Li–S batter-
ies, including liquid-state and SSLSBs.
2.1. 0D carbon materials
0D materials refer to materials with all dimensions at the
nanoscale, and 0D carbon materials used in SSLSBs, generally,
refer to porous carbon particles/spheres, which are capable to
conﬁne active materials within pores to improve the utiliza-
tion of active materials. Also, the pore size and morphologies
of porous carbon materials can be manipulated to achieve
better performance. Porous carbon materials can be classi-
ﬁed into microporous (<2 nm), mesoporous (2–50 nm), and
macroporous (>50 nm) carbon materials according to their
different pore size.
2.1.1. Sulfur hosts
In liquid-state Li–S batteries, microporous carbon materi-
als42–44 act as the physical barrier that can restrict the dis-
solution of polysulﬁdes into electrolytes.29 Carbon materials
with mesopores45 and macropores46,47 can afford high sulfur
loading and are more conducive to providing sufficient space
for lithium ions transport due to their larger pore size.48 As
a result, hierarchical pores49 with multifunction have been
proven to have a great beneﬁt in improving electrochemical
performance, and thus have been widely studied.50,51
2.1.2. Coating materials
0D conductive carbon powder is favourable as a coating
layer for separators to prevent the migration of polysulﬁdes.

2.1.3. Carbon-conductive additives
The adoption of carbon-conductive additives is to ensure
good electron percolation in sulfur cathodes. The 0D carbon
materials, such as carbon black, acetylene black (AB), and su-
per P are conventional conductive additives in Li–S batteries,
due to their low cost and high electronic conductivity. The
conducting networks are dominated by the degree of inter-
particle contact.
The same as liquid-state Li–S batteries, in SSLSBs, the pore
structure effect of carbon materials on the electrochemical
performance of sulfur cathodes has also been investigated.
Early work of 0D porous carbon materials for the cathode of
SSLSBs was conducted by Kanno et al., who reported the use
of porous AB with pore sizes ranging from 1 to 100 nm. Low
resistance for the sulfur/AB composite electrode has been
achieved by using the gas/solid mixing method. Tatsumisago
et al.54 reported a carbon material composed of large quan-
tities of 5-nm diameter interconnected mesopores, and they
demonstrated that the interconnected pores enabled highly
electronic and ionic conductivity network and a larger sul-
fur/carbon contact area, which delivered a high discharge ca-
pacity of 110 mAh g−1 after 400 cycles at a high current den-
sity of 1.3 mA cm−2. In the same year, Zhang et al.40 reported
porous BP2000 carbon material with a pore size of about 5 nm
to form a core-shell sulfur@BP2000 nanocomposite. The au-
thors proposed that this mesoporous structure can act as a
conductive network to facilitate the electron transfer in the
cathode and alleviate the large volumetric changes from sul-
fur to Li2S. High-rate performance of 678.6 mAh g−1 at 4 C
and outstanding cycling performance of nearly 100% capacity
retention after 1200 cycles at room temperature can be ob-
tained. As mentioned before, the inorganic-based SSLSBs typ-
ically eliminate the formation of soluble polysulﬁdes. There-
fore, the design of porous carbon materials does not require
the function to conﬁne the polysulﬁdes, which is different
from liquid-state Li–S batteries. Hayashi et al.55 investigated
the carbon materials containing multiple types of porous
structures, mesopores, and micropores less than 1 nm and
examined the effect of micropores on cycling performance.
The results showed that the cycle performance of SSLSBs us-
ing microporous carbon is worse than that of using intercon-
nected mesoporous carbon, indicating the advantages of in-
terconnected mesopores in SSLSBs. In addition to the porous
structure, other properties of 0D carbon materials, such as
the wall thickness, the conductivities, and the mixing meth-
ods with sulfur, have also been investigated in pioneer re-
search works.56,57
2.2. 1D carbon materials
1D carbon materials, such as carbon nanotubes (CNTs)58
and ﬁbers,59 have been widely studied in Li–S batteries due
to the following advantages: (1) fast and continuous elec-
tron and ion transport pathways with a large aspect ra-
tio, which facilitate the construction of the conductive net-
work; (2) high mechanical strength, which is excellent to
tolerate the stress and strain in the cathode caused by
volume changes of sulfur; and (3) high surface area to
offer more active space for sulfur loading and the con-
struction of S (or Li2S)/SSEs/carbon materials triple-phase
interfaces. In addition to that, 1D carbon materials have
the advantages of lightweight and high chemical/thermal
stability.60,61
2.2.1. Sulfur hosts
The addition of non-conducting binder results in extra
weight and decreases the continuous Li-ions and electrons
transfer. Thus, the application of 1D carbon materials as
sulfur hosts is beneﬁcial for providing continuous Li-ions
and electron transfer networks.62 Moreover, the long length–
diameter ratios of 1D carbons are beneﬁcial for assembling
freestanding cathodes.63 In addition, 1D carbons/other car-
bon composites and heteroatoms-doped 1D carbons are also
applied as sulfur hosts.64

2.2.2. Coating materials
1D carbon materials, such as CNTs, are employed as coat-
ing materials applied to separators in Li–S batteries.65,66 In
addition to bare CNTs, surface-modiﬁed CNTs with excel-
lent polysulﬁde-trapping ability have been developed. Man-
thiram et al.67 presented boron-doped CNTs-coated separa-
tors in Li–S batteries, which exhibit effective trapping of poly-
sulﬁdes. Carbon nanoﬁbers (CNFs) and multiwalled CNTs, as
freestanding interlayers, have also been developed for trap-
ping polysulﬁdes, channelling Li-ions, and facilitating charge
transfer.68,69
2.2.3. Carbon-conductive additives
The 1D carbon materials include CNF and CNTs with high
length–diameter ratios. The conductive networks formed by
them are less sensitive to interparticle contact, and thus are
more efficient in forming a network with high conductivity.
1D carbon materials have also been used in SSLSBs. Vapour
grown ﬁber (VGCF) is a type of 1D carbon material about
150 nm in diameter and 15 μm in length.60 Kinoshita et al.70
ﬁrst reported the use of VGCF in the cathode for SSLSBs.
In the synthetic process, the sulfur/VGCF composites were
prepared through a high-energy ball milling method. The
composites showed high discharge capacity with 1300 mAh
g−1 at room temperature and kept high capacity retention
with 1200 mAh g−1 after 50 cycles. CNF is another promising
1D carbon material with high electrical conductivity from
10−7 to 103 S cm−1.61 The merits of high surface area with
20–2500 m2 g−1 and large porosity, as well as the large-scale
mass production, make CNF a favourable candidate in both
liquid-state and SSLSBs.60 An early work employing CNF in
sulfur/carbon composite was reported by Aihara’s group.71
In this study, the authors illustrate that an intimate three-
phase contact structure with CNF as the conductive skele-
ton, electrolyte as the ion-conducting network, and sulfur
as active materials can be built by optimizing the cathode
synthetic process. A similar concept has also been demon-
strated by Wen’s group72 by in situ coating Li7P3S11 elec-
trolyte on the surface of carbonized dispersed cotton ﬁbre
via a liquid-phase strategy to obtain a three-phase interface
contact structure. CNTs are one of the most widely used
1D carbon materials as carbon additives and sulfur hosts
in the cathode for SSLSBs, contributing to their extremely
high electrical conductivity (102–106 S cm−1), the large as-
pect ratio of 1.3 × 108, and outstanding mechanical/chemical
stability and ﬂexible features.60 Yao et al.73 designed sul-
fur/carbon composites using CNT as sulfur host through the
liquid method. The intercrossed CNT not only created inte-
gral conductive pathways but also realized an improved uti-
lization of sulfur. High discharge capacity with 660.3 mAh
g−1 after 400 cycles and 100% columbic efficiency have been
achieved. Recently, Chen et al.74 prepared a sulfur/multiwall
CNTs (MWCNTs)/Li7La3Zr2O12 (LLZO) network by thermal dif-
fusion method, which delivered high capacity retention of
72.62% after 50 cycles. Nan et al.75 applied MWCNT and
Li6PS5Cl as sulfur hosts and electrolytes, taking advantage
of the high electronic conductivity of MWCNT and the high
ionic conductivity of Li6PS5Cl electrolyte; the excellent dis-
charge capacity of 1850 mAh g−1 at room temperature and
1393 mAh g−1 after 50 cycles have been shown.
2.3. 2D and 3D carbon materials
In liquid-state Li–S batteries, 2D 76,77 and 3D carbon mate-
rials 78 have been employed as sulfur hosts and coating ma-
terials.
2.3.1. Sulfur hosts
In addition to pure 2D graphene, the pre-prepared 2D
graphene ﬁlm with good ﬂexibility and 2D/3D graphene
foams/sponges with large surface area and abundant pore
size distribution are directly used as sulfur cathodes. These
freestanding materials possess high strength, ﬂexible proper-
ties, and well-deﬁned interpenetrating structures, which en-
able good electronic and ionic conductivity.79,80

In addition to using a single type of carbon materials in
sulfur cathodes, hybrid carbon materials have been applied
to achieve enhanced electrochemical performance. Nan’s
group88 prepared nanohybrid composites with different di-
mensions of carbon materials (1D MWCNT, 0D super P, and

3. Design strategies of carbon materials
for sulfur cathodes in SSLSBs
The structural design of carbon materials plays an im-
portant role to overcome the challenges in sulfur cathodes
with the following main strategic goals: (1) improving the
electrical conductivity of sulfur and discharge product Li2S;
(2) accommodating the volume change of sulfur and Li2S
and reducing the stress/strain at the interface of the compo-
nents in the cathodes (sulfur, SSEs, and conductive carbon);
(3) decreasing the interfacial resistance of sulfur–carbon–
electrolyte three phases; and (4) achieving high active ma-
terial loading to approach practical applications. In the fol-
lowing section, the carbon structural design strategies and
corresponding research work to achieve these goals will be
introduced.
3.1. Improvement of electronic conductivity
Carbon materials are acting as electron suppliers for sul-
fur cathodes due to their promising electronic conductiv-
ity and have become the most popular materials as sulfur
hosts in SSLSBs. Synthesis methods are essential to deter-
mine the formed sulfur–carbon composite structures to ful-
ﬁll the following requirements: (1) enabling a uniform dis-
persion of active materials in the pores of carbon materials
while maintaining sufficient active material mass loading,
so as to realize a large contact area between active materi-
als and carbon materials and (2) minimizing the size of ac-
tive materials into nanoscale to gain shorter electrons/ions
transport pathways. In the following sections, various syn-
thetic strategies combined with different carbon structures
are discussed. In Table 2, the studies using various syn-
thetic methods for carbon/sulfur composites to improve elec-
tronic conductivity of sulfur cathodes are summarized and
compared.
3.1.1. Ball milling method
The mechanical mixing method generally includes hand
grinding and ball milling.89,90 Tatsumisago et al.89 compared
the synthetic methods of sulfur/AB composites by hand grind-
ing with a mortar and ball milling with a planetary ball mill
apparatus. The results showed that the sulfur/carbon compos-
ites mixed by the ball milling method can deliver higher ca-
pacity due to the reduced particle size and better contact be-
tween sulfur and carbon materials. Later, this research group
reported a Li2S/AB composite90 with 500 nm Li2S and AB par-
ticles; they emphasized the reduced particle size and uniform
distribution of sulfur in the composites are the reason for the
improved battery performance. The operating time for the
mechanical mixing method has also been studied by Kanno’s

However, the capacity faded severely during 30 cycles be-

optimized ball milling method resulted in smaller sulfur
particle size and large contact area, which lead to excel-
lent cycling performance (above 800 mAh g−1 after 40 cy-

that investigated the temperature effect of the ball milling
process.92
3.1.2. Liquid-phase deposition method
It is widely used in the preparation of sulfur/carbon com-
posites to synthesize nano-sized sulfur particles. During the
liquid-phase deposition process, the nano-sized sulfur will be
ﬁrst dispersed in a liquid solution, along with the deposition
process on the carbon materials. By minimizing the size of
sulfur particles, the electrons/Li+ pathways can be shortened
and the utilization of sulfur in the electrochemical reaction
can be improved. As reported by Yao’s group,73 sulfur/CNTs
composites were prepared by depositing nano-sized sulfur
on the surface of CNTs using the thiamine method. SSLSBs
with this sulfur/carbon composites delivered a high capacity
of 660.3 mAh g−1 after 400 cycles at 1.0 C with 100% coulom-
bic efficiency. Wang et al.93 reported a sulfur/CMK-3 com-
posite by mixing sulfur–tetrahydrofuran solvent and CMK-3-
tetrahydrofuran solvent. After stirring and drying, the sulfur
could uniformly disperse into the pores of CMK-3. As shown
in Fig. 2B left, Zhang et al.33 used the liquid-phase deposition
method that dropped sulfur/CS2 solution onto the CNF mat
surface. The thickness of sulfur layer was tailored to 8–18 nm
by optimizing the ratio of sulfur/CS2. Wang et al.86 also em-
ployed the liquid-phase deposition method that uses an an-
hydrous ethylenediamine solution to deposit sulfur onto the
surface of rGO, as shown in Fig. 2B right. The thickness of sul-
fur layer can be controlled within 2 nm, which increased the
contact area between sulfur and rGO, and could shorten the
electrons/ions transfer pathways. As reported by the authors,
the developed sulfur cathode exhibited a high capacity of 830
mAh g−1 after 750 cycles at 1.0 C in SSLSBs.

3.2. Accommodation of volume change
Sulfur cathodes in SSLSBs suffer from the problem of
large volume expansion (approximately 80%) during lithia-
tion, which leads to the severe detachment of sulfur from
the electrical conductor and SSEs, and ﬁnally the pulveriza-
tion of cathodes, resulting in the severe capacity degradation
with limited battery life. As a result, most of the reported
SSLSBs required external pressure control during the battery
operation, which is a big challenge for practical applications.
In this respect, carbon materials have the potential to over-
come this challenge based on their tunable morphologies and
structures. Based on the structural design, two main concepts
of carbon materials are summarized to mitigate the sulfur
volumetric expansion, (1) open structure of carbon materials
with free space for sulfur volume expansion and (2) encap-
sulation of nano-sized sulfur in compact carbon structures.
In Table 3, the studies based on two carbon structural design
strategies to accommodate volume change of sulfur cathodes
are summarized.

3.2.2. Compact structure
Contrary to the open structure, the design and construc-
tion of a compact structure in which sulfur is tightly en-
closed with carbon hosts and SSEs is also a promising strat-
egy. This is highly related to the particle size of sulfur and
the morphology and ﬂexibility of carbon hosts. Yang et al.36
reported a Li2S@C nanocomposite with compact encapsula-
tion structure. In the synthetic process, the authors mixed
lithium metal foil and CS2, and then heated them at 300 ◦C;
after the combustion reaction, Li2S@C nanocomposites with
uniform distribution of Li2S in carbon matrix can be obtained
(Fig. 3B-d). Notably, the grain boundaries of sulfur and car-
bon materials were fused (Fig. 3B-e), which resulted in a tight
adherence between sulfur and carbon materials. Beneﬁting
from this unique structure, the assembled SSLSBs exhibited a
high initial capacity of 1067 mAh g−1 and outstanding capac-
ity retention (nearly 100%) after 30 cycles even at high Li2S
mass loading of 7 mg cm−2, as shown in Fig. 3B-f. In 2020,
the same group39 used nitrogen-doped carbon (NC) to act as
a carbon layer to coat Li2S particles by pyrolysis deposition
approach. The TEM images (Fig. 3B-g) of Li2S@carbon showed
that the thin carbon layer intimately covered Li2S particles,
which formed a compact Li2S@NC structure. This Li2S@NC
composite delivered a high capacity even under a high Li2S
mass loading from 2.83 to 10.4 mg cm−2 (as shown in Figs. 3B-
h and 3B-i). In addition to supplying a high strength barrier
against stress/stain caused by the volume change of sulfur,
the achievement of this compact structure is generally ac-
companied by a high active material mass loading due to
its unique preparation method, which will be discussed in
Section 3.4.

3.4. Increase mass loading of active materials
Active materials’ areal mass loading is essential to deter-
mine the energy density of battery devices.4,5 Based on the
calculation results from other review papers, the areal load-
ing should be at least reach to 4 mg cm−2 toward the energy
density of 500 Wh kg−1.111 It should be noted that the devel-
opment of SSLSBs is still in the early stage, and therefore most
of the research papers reported relatively low areal mass load-
ing of active materials (mostly below 2 mg cm−2). Addition-
ally, several challenges are suffered by SSLSBs, such as capac-
ity decay, short life, and low active material utilization, which
will become more serious when active materials’ mass load-
ing increases to a practically accepted level (>4 mg cm−2).23
Accordingly, the construction of a reasonable carbon struc-
ture for cathodes is essential to achieve a balance between
high active materials’ areal loading and ideal electrochemical
performance. Taking advantage of the unique porous struc-
ture of most carbon materials, and the property that melting
or gaseous sulfur can be fully injected into the pores of the
carbon matrix, Fu et al.37 prepared a sulfur/3D graphene free-
standing cathode by using a liquid-phase method and the fol-
lowing heat treatment, the ultimate sulfur-loading reached
8.0 mg cm−2, and the assembled battery realized 88% sulfur
utilization and an extraordinary energy density of 588.8 Wh
kg−1. Also, high mass loading and good electrochemical per-
formance can be achieved by the in situ synthesis method.
Xie’s group109 immersed AB and Li2S precursor (Li2SO4) into
CH3CH3OH solvent and water; after a thermal process, an
Li2S–AB composite with 5.78 mg cm−2 Li2S loading was ob-
tained, and the battery delivered a high speciﬁc discharge
capacity of 908.9 mAh g−1 after 30 cycles. Yang et al.112 uti-
lized Li foil as a precursor, and then mixed Li foil with CS2,
followed by a combustion process, the carbon matrix was in
situ formed and wrapped the Li2S particles with compact en-

high Li2S mass loading of 7 mg cm−1. An ultrahigh Li2S mass

4. Challenges of carbon materials
4.1. Decomposition of SSEs
In addition to the advantages and design strategies of car-
bon materials in sulfur cathodes, carbon materials also face
challenges in SSLSBs. A phenomenon is commonly observed
in SSLSBs, where the capacity in the ﬁrst charge/discharge
cycle is higher than the theoretical speciﬁc capacity of sul-
fur and Li2S.113,114 The extra capacity is contributed by the
reversible SSEs decomposition process occurring simultane-
ously during charge/discharge cycles. Researchers indicate
that the origin of the SSEs decomposition is due to the elec-
tron supplier by carbon materials along with SSEs (electron
insulator) together. The mechanisms of SSE decomposition
were investigated in depth by using various advanced char-
acterizations, such as time-of-ﬂight secondary ion mass spec-
trometry, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction,
solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance, and electrochemical
tests.115 Meng116 took the sulﬁde electrolyte Li6PS5Cl as an
example. As shown in Fig. 5A-a, the operating voltage range
of Li–S batteries precisely occupies part of the oxidative and
reductive decomposition voltage range of Li6PS5Cl, result-
ing in reversible electrolyte decomposition-derived capacity.
The authors proposed the reversible electrochemical reaction
pathways of Li6PS5Cl based on the analysis of the characteri-
zation results motioned, as illustrated in Fig. 5A-b.
4.2. Effect of carbon on electrolytes
decomposition
Zeier et al.117 (Fig. 5B-c) compared the ﬁrst charging curves
of solid-state LIBs with (red) and without (blue) carbon. Com-

growing in the battery with carbon additives due to the
larger interfacial resistance at the carbon/Li10GeP2S12 inter-
faces caused by the generation and accumulation of insulat-
ing decomposition products S0 or polysulﬁdes, leading to iso-
lated lithium ion transportation pathways and capacity de-
cay. Later, Zeier et al.115 (Fig. 5B-d) studied the effect of VGCF
as carbon additives on the decomposition of LPS by assem-

proved electrical conductivity of active materials and extra
capacity contribution by the electrochemical reaction of LPS.

position process along with carbon materials. It should be

of carbon materials as an additive in solid-state LIBs. There-

5. Conclusion and perspectives
SSLSBs have become important on the road to achieving
high-performance energy storage devices, attributed to their
high energy density, low cost, and superiority in solving the
severe shuttle effect issue and safety concerns of SSLSBs. Car-
bon materials with diverse geometries and functional capa-
bilities play an important role in addressing the challenges of
the sulfur cathodes, which hinder the development of SSLSBs.
This review summarized carbon materials employed in the
cathodes of SSLSBs. The review introduced the structural fea-
tures of different carbon materials, and the corresponding
functions and design strategies of carbon materials in cath-
odes. Some important conclusions and insights can be drawn
from this review.
(1) Carbon materials always play the role of electron conduc-
tors for sulfur-based species. Proper structural design of
carbon materials and reasonable synthetic methods are
the keys to a highly conductive network, such as ball
milling, heat treatment, liquid-based deposition, etc. The
key strategies behind the different synthetic methods are
(a) larger sulfur/carbon contact area and (b) nanosized
particles of sulfur/Li2S, resulting in shorter electron/ion
diffusion pathways.
(2) Volume change of sulfur cathodes is one of the main
challenges in the SSLSBs’ long-life operation. As summa-
rized from the literature, we deﬁned two structural de-
sign strategies for carbon materials that can effectively
mitigate the volume changes in cathodes, (a) open struc-
ture that provides free space for volumetric changes to
avoid cathode cracks and (b) compact structure in which
sulfur is tightly enclosed with carbon host and SSEs to
avoid the volumetric change of S/Li2S. Interestingly, the
two concepts are in opposite directions for the design of
sulfur cathodes and both are demonstrated as effective
ways to overcome the challenges of volume change. It
should be noted that these reported strategies are highly
related to the particle sizes of sulfur/Li2S, the selection
