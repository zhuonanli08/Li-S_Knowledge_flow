The improvement of Li2S utilization is an important issue for 
achieving cells with high energy density. The utilizations of 
Li2S have remained only 70% even though many efforts[5] have 
been paid such as the use of several mixing techniques,[5a,b] the

organic liquid electrolytes, the highest reversible utilization is 
75% although extensive studies such as mixing techniques with 
carbon and development of carbon matrix have been done.[6] 
Therefore, effective approach which is entirely different from 
previous conventional techniques should be investigated for the 
full utilization of Li2S. Recently, reaction mechanism of lithium 
and sulfur has been investigated by using in-situ transmission 
electron microscope.[7] These researches have suggested a new 
lithiation process, where electronic conduction paths are not 
only in carbon but also at the interface between Li2S and S. On 
the other hand, an ionic conduction path is only in Li2S, but

All-solid-state Li/S batteries have received a lot of attention in the view 
point of high safety and long cycle life. However, the all-solid-state Li/S cells 
have suffered from the low utilization of Li2S. In this study, for improving 
the utilization and understanding the conversion reaction of Li2S/S in the 
all-solid-state cells, Li2S-based solid solutions composed of Li2S and one of 
lithium halides of LiCl, LiBr, and LiI are applied as active materials to the all-
solid-state cells. Capacity of the cells with 80Li2S·20LiI (mol%) reaches more 
than 1100 mAh g−1 at 0.5 C, corresponding to 95% of theoretical capacity. 
The reversible full utilization of Li2S is first demonstrated in this work. The 
significantly improved utilization of Li2S is originated by the increase of 
electrochemical reaction sites rather than the ionic conductivities of Li2S-
based solid solutions. Furthermore, the cell retains the capacity of 980 mAh 
g−1 at 2 C for 2000 cycles without capacity degradation. These are the best 
cycling performances in the reported Li2S cells with not only solid electrolytes 
but also organic liquid electrolytes.

Li-S Batteries
All-solid-state Li/S batteries have received a lot of attention 
from the viewpoint of high safety and long cycle life because 
of eliminating the dissolution of lithium polysulfides species 
to organic liquid electrolyte. However, the all-solid-state Li/S 
cells have suffered from low utilization of Li2S. In this study, 
Li2S–LiCl, Li2S–LiBr, and Li2S–LiI solid solutions are applied as 
active materials to the all-solid-state cells. Capacity of the cells 
with Li2S–LiI is gradually increased during electrochemical 
cycling and reaches more than 1100 mAh g−1 at 0.5 C, cor-
responding to 95% of theoretical capacity. Furthermore, the 
cells retain the capacity of 980 mAh g−1 at 2 C for 2000 cycles 
without capacity degradation. To our best knowledge, the cells 
with Li2S–LiI show the highest utilization and best cycling per-
formances in not only all-solid-state cells but also liquid type 
cells using a Li2S active material reported so far.

tigated in the view point of high safety and long cycle life.[1] 
However, in these cells, Li–In alloy has been used as counter 
and reference electrode because of high reversibility and

In this study, for understanding the conversion reaction 
Li2S/S, the effects of the ionic conductivity and type of Li2S-
based solid solutions on the Li2S utilization have been inves-
tigated. Solid solutions in the systems Li2S–LiCl and Li2S–LiBr 
with ionic conductivities comparable to Li2S–LiI are prepared 
and then applied to the all-solid-state cells.
Li2S–LiX(X = Cl, Br, and I) solid solutions are prepared by 
ball-milling. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the obtained 
samples are shown in Figure 1a–c. Si powder is added as an 
internal standard for calculating lattice constants of the pre-
pared samples. In all the prepared samples, all the diffraction 
peaks are indexed by the cubic structure of Li2S. In the Li2S–
LiCl and Li2S–LiBr samples, the peaks attributable to LiCl and 
LiBr disappear in the compositions of x ≤ 30. On the other 
hand, in the Li2S–LiI sample, the peaks attributable to LiI are 
observed at x = 25. For the region in which the diffraction 
peaks attributable to lithium halides disappear, average lat-
tice constants of the prepared samples are calculated from the 
(111), (200), (220), and (311) peaks. The composition depend-
ence of the lattice constants is shown in Figure 1d. The lattice 
constants of Li2S–LiCl solid solutions are not changed signifi­
cantly from that of Li2S. On the other hand, the lattice con-
stants of Li2S–LiBr and Li2S–LiI solid solutions are increased 
with an increase of the lithium halide contents. In particular, 
the change of lattice constants for Li2S–LiI solid solutions is 
the most remarkable in the prepared samples, suggesting that 
the lattice constants are affected with an ionic radius of anions 
(S2−:184 pm, Cl−:181 pm, Br−:195 pm, and I−:216 pm).[9] The 
disappearance of the diffraction peaks attributable to lithium 
halides and the changes of lattice constants indicate that the 
Li2S-based solid solutions are formed.
Ionic conductivities of the prepared solid solutions are 
measured by using impedance measurements. The composi-
tion dependence of ionic conductivities for the prepared solid 
solutions is shown in Figure 1e. The ionic conductivities of the 
solid solutions are increased with an increase of the content for 
lithium halides regardless of the kind of lithium halides. These 
results suggest that the defects of Li+ ions are introduced by 
charge compensation accompanied with the substitution of 
anions. The Li2S–LiI solid solutions show relatively high ionic 
conductivities compared to other solid solutions in the same 
contents of lithium halides. This would be because the lat-
tice constant of the Li2S–LiI solid solutions is larger than that

Charge–discharge curves of the cells with Li2S, 70Li2 
S·30LiCl, 70Li2 S·30LiBr, and 80Li2 S·20LiI for 50 cycles are 
shown in Figure 2a–d, respectively. The charge–discharge 
measurements are controlled with the initial charge capacities. 
An overpotential for the curves is relatively large at the initial 
cycle and decreased for first 10 cycles. One plateau is observed 
during charge–discharge processes; the observed discharge 
potential at around 1.8 V versus Li+/Li is in accordance to the 
lithiation potential of the small sulfur allotropes such as S4, S3, 
and S2.[10] The conversion reaction of Li2S/S without forming 
liquid electrolyte-soluble polysulfides (Li2Sx, x = 4–8) proceeds 
in the all-solid-state Li/S cell.
The cells with Li2S, 70Li2 S·30LiCl, 70Li2 S·30LiBr, and 
80Li2 S·20LiI show the capacities of 500, 600, 600, 900 mAh 
per gram of Li2S at the 50th cycle, respectively. The cells with 
Li2S, 70Li2 S·30LiCl, and 70Li2 S·30LiBr exhibit the almost 
same reversible capacities, indicating that the ionic conductivity 
of Li2S-based solid solutions does not affect the utilization of 
Li2S. The cell with 80Li2 S·20LiI exhibits the highest utilization, 
suggesting that the charged 80Li2 S·20LiI has a higher ionic 
conductivity than the other charged solid solutions. On charge 
process, the 80Li2 S·20LiI solid solution may be decomposed to 
form LiI dispersed in Li2S matrix. The formation of LiI would 
provide electrochemical reaction sites because LiI has relative 
high ionic conductivity in the other lithium halides. It is note-
worthy that the transformation of Li2S-based solid solutions is 
reversible during charge–discharge process.
In the cell with 80Li2 S·20LiI, charge potential at an end 
of charging is gradually decreased with the cycling as shown 
in Figure 2e. The charge–discharge measurements are cut off 
with the initial charge capacities as mentioned above. At the 
51th cycle, the cell with 80Li2 S·20LiI is charged at the cut 
off voltage of 3.6 V versus Li+/Li without the limitation of the 
initial charge capacity. A reversible capacity is increased to 
1000 mAh g−1 at the 51th cycle. For the cell with 70Li2 S·30LiCl 
as shown in Figure 2b, overpotentials at the end of charge 
during steady cycles are lower than that of the initial cycle. At 
the 51th cycle, the cell with 70Li2 S·30LiCl is charged in the 
same way of the cell with 80Li2 S·20LiI. The cell capacity is 
increased, however the overpotential is still large. Charge–dis-
charge curves of the cell with 80Li2 S·20LiI charged at the cut 
off voltage of 3.6 V versus Li+/Li for each 10 cycles are shown 
in Figure 2f. The reversible capacity is gradually increased and 
then achieved to the almost theoretical value of 1100 mAh g−1. 
The reversible full utilization of Li2S has been first demon-
strated here in the reported Li2S cells with not only solid elec-
trolytes but also organic liquid electrolytes.[11]
Rate performance of the cells with Li2S and 80Li2 S·20LiI 
at different current densities is shown in Figure 3a. The cell 
with Li2S shows the capacity of 525, 480, and 380 mAh g−1 at 
0.5, 1, and 2 C, respectively. On the other hand, the cell with 
80Li2S·20LiI shows the capacity of 1130 mAh g−1 at 0.5 C 
and retains its capacity at 1 C. Moreover, the cell can deliver 
a capacity of 970 mAh g−1 even at a high current of 2 C. The 
capacities of the cell with 80Li2 S·20LiI are more than two 
times higher than that of the cell with Li2S in any rates. Average 
discharge potentials of the cell with 80Li2 S·20LiI at 0.5 and 
2 C are respectively 1.7 and 1.3 V versus Li+/Li as shown in 
Figure 3b. The difference of the average discharge potentials

solid electrolyte layer and the positive electrode determined by 
the impedance measurements is 240 Ω, which will give the 
overpotential difference of 360 mV between 2 and 0.5 C. The

The charge–discharge profiles of the cells with Li2S and 
80Li2 S·20LiI are compared as a function of capacity reten-
tion ratios and are shown in Figure 3c. The capacity reten-
tion ratios are calculated with the discharge capacity at 0.5 C 
as 100%. Interestingly, the discharge potential profiles are not 
significantly different between Li2S and 80Li2 S·20LiI at any 
discharge depth even though the utilization of 80Li2 S·20LiI 
is two times larger than that of Li2S. On the charging process, 
overpotential is also same degree although potential profiles are 
slightly different. These results suggest that the electrochemical 
resistances are almost same between Li2S and 80Li2 S·20LiI 
even though the utilization is significantly different. The differ-
ence of utilization would be attributable to simple increase of 
electrochemical reaction sites.
Discharge capacities and Coulombic efficiencies of the cells 
with Li2S and 80Li2 S·20LiI at 1 C for 300 cycles are shown 
in Figure 3d. The cells with Li2S and 80Li2 S·20LiI show ini-
tial capacity of 470 and 1090 mAh g−1, respectively, and then 
100% of the initial capacities are maintained after 300 cycle. 
Long-term cycle performance of the cell with 80Li2 S·20LiI at 
2 C is shown in Figure 3e. Remarkably, the cell with the 80Li2 
S·20LiI electrode shows a capacity retention of 100% for the 
initial capacity after the 2000th cycle and Coulumbic efficiency 
close to 100%. In the batteries with organic liquid electrolytes, 
long-term cycle tests for 400 to 1500 cycles have been investi-
gated and these cells have retained only 30–80% of their initial 
capacities after cycling.[12] Therefore, the all-solid-state cell with 
the 80Li2 S·20LiI electrode exhibits the best cycle performances 
in the reported cells with Li2S as an active material. As shown 
in Figure 3f, an operating voltage slightly changes of about 
50 mV from the 50th to 2000th cycles, indicating that an 
increase of resistances caused by side reactions during cycling 
is ignored and the all-solid-state Li/S batteries with sulfide-
based SEs have the excellent stability.
In conclusion, Li2S–LiX (X = Cl, Br, and I) solid solutions 
were prepared and then applied to the all-solid-state cells. The 
utilization of Li2S did not depend on the ionic conductivities of 
the Li2S-based solid solutions and the difference of utilization 
was based on the electrochemical reaction sites. It is expected 
that solid solutions were decomposed on the charge process, 
and LiI dispersed in Li2S matrix would provide electrochem-
ical reaction sites. The all-solid-state cells with 80Li2 S·20LiI 
exhibited the capacity of more than 1100 mAh g−1 at 0.5 C, 
corresponding to the utilization of 95%. Furthermore, the cell 
retained the capacity of 980 mAh g−1 at 2 C for 2000 cycles. 
The cells with 80Li2 S·20LiI showed the highest utilization 
and best cycling performances in not only all-solid-state cells 
but also liquid type Li/S cells reported so far. This work dem-
onstrates that the all-solid-state cell with Li2S-based solid solu-
tions is a promising high energy storage system and provides 
new insight to improve the Li2S utilization for high-peformance 
Li/S batteries.
Experimental Section
Synthesis of Li2S Solid Solutions: To prepare the Li2S–LiX (X = Cl, 
Br, and I) solid solutions, a mixture of reagent-grade Li2S (Mitsuwa 
Chemicals Co., Ltd.) and one of lithium halides of LiCl (Aldrich, 99.9%), 
LiBr (Aldrich, 99.9%), and LiI (Aldrich, 99.9%) powders was placed into 
a ZrO2 pot (45 mL) with 160 ZrO2 balls (diameter: 4 mm). It was then 
mechanically milled with a planetary ball mill (Fritsch Pulverisette 7) for 
10 h at 510 rpm in Ar atmosphere.

Characterizations of Li2S-Based Solid Solutions: X-ray diffraction 
measurements (Cu Kα) were performed for the obtained solid solutions 
using a diffractometer (UltimaIV, Rigaku) to identify crystalline phases 
and calculate lattice constants. Ionic conductivities of the solid solutions 
were measured for the pellets with 10 mm in diameter pressed under 
360 MPa sandwiched between stainless-steel disks with an impedance 
analyzer (Solartron, 1260) in dry Ar atmosphere. The frequency range is 
0.1 Hz to 8 MHz, and the applied voltage is 100 mV.
Preparation of the Composite Electrodes: The 75Li2S·25P2S5 (mol%) 
glass solid electrolyte prepared by mechanical milling was used as solid 
electrolyte layers for all-solid-state cells. Its lithium ion conductivity 
was over 10−4 S cm−1 at 25 °C. The composite electrodes consisted of 
Li2S–LiX (X = Cl, Br, and I), vaper grown carbon fiber (VGCF, Showa 
Denko), and solid electrolyte (SE) in a weight ratio of 50:10:40. The 
mixture of the 70Li2 S·30LiCl, 70Li2 S·30LiBr, or 80Li2 S·20LiI (mol%) and 
VGCF as a conductive additive were mechanically milled at 510 rpm for 
10 h in a ZrO2 pot (45 mL) with 160 ZrO2 balls (diameter: 4mm) to form 
electron conduction paths to the solid solutions. The prepared Li2S–LiX 
(X = Cl, Br, and I)-VGCF powder and SE powder were softly milled at 
150 rpm for 0.5 h to avoid a function of SE as an active material in a 
dry Ar-filled glove box. The obtained composite electrode powders were 
used as working electrodes in all-solid-state cells.
Construction of All-Solid-State Cells and Their Electrochemical 
Measurements: Electrode properties of the solid solutions were 
investigated in all-solid-state electrochemical cells. The all-solid-state 
cells of LiIn/70Li2 S·30LiCl, 70Li2 S·30LiBr, or 80Li2 S·20LiI composite 
electrodes were fabricated as follows. The prepared composite electrodes 
were directly used as a working electrode, and the SE was used as a solid 
electrolyte separate layer. The obtained working electrode (3 mg cm−2), 
corresponding to 0.9–1.1 mg cm−2 of Li2S mass, and the solid electrolyte 
layer (80 mg) were set in a polycarbonate tube (diameter: 10 mm) and 
pressed together under 360 MPa. An indium foil with a thickness of 
300 µm and a lithium foil with a thickness of 250 µm were then placed 
on the surface of the SE side of the bilayer pellet as a counter-reference 
electrode. The three-layered pellet was then sandwiched between two 
stainless-steel disks as a current collector. Finally, electrochemical solid-
state cells were obtained by pressing under 72 MPa. Electrochemical 
tests of the cells tightened constantly are conducted at various current 
densities at 25 °C in Ar atmosphere using a charge–discharge measuring 
device (BTS-2004, Nagano Co.). The voltage range was 1.2–3.6 V (vs Li). 
The SE in the composite electrodes did not act as an active material 
because only one plateau attributable to Li2S active material appeared in 
the charge–discharge profile as shown in Figure 2.
