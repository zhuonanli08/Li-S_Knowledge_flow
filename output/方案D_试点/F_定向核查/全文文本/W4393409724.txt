Nano-Scale Interface Engineering of Sulfur Cathode to
Enable High-Performance All-Solid-State Li–S Batteries
Haoyue Zhong, Yu Su, Ruqin Ma, Yu Luo, Hongxin Lin, Jiabao Gu, Zhengliang Gong,
and Yong Yang*
All-solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) are expected to be the next
generation of high-energy battery systems due to their long lifespan and high
safety. However, unstable interfaces between elemental sulfur, conductive
carbon, and solid electrolytes lead to slow charge transport and mechanical
failures, thereby limiting battery performance. Herein, atomic layer
deposition-derived lithium phosphorus oxide is applied to the surface of
carbon/sulfur particles to enhance the interfacial stability of the sulfur
cathode and improve the electrochemical performance of ASSLSBs. The
coating layer can inhibit electrolyte decomposition and improve interfacial
stability by blocking electron conduction between carbon and electrolyte.
Moreover, it not only serves as an ion-conducting layer to facilitate Li+
transport but also acts as a stress buﬀer layer to alleviate contact failure. The
assembled ASSLSBs with sulﬁde electrolyte exhibit an initial speciﬁc capacity
of 1322 mAh g−1 at 0.2 C and capacity retention of 86.4% after 300 cycles.
Furthermore, ASSLSBs maintain a reversible capacity of 645 mAh g−1 at
0.5 A g−1 after 1000 cycles, conﬁrming the long cycling stability of the coated
sulfur cathode. Even under high sulfur loading, ASSLSBs achieve high areal
capacities of 4.6 mAh cm−2 at 30 °C and 11.7 mAh cm−2 at 60 °C.
1. Introduction
The development of electric vehicles, smart grids and other
ﬁelds has put forward new requirements and challenges for elec-
trochemical energy storage systems.[1,2] Due to the advantages
of elemental sulfur such as high theoretical speciﬁc capacity,
low cost, and environmental friendliness, lithium–sulfur (Li–S)
batteries have attracted much attention as next-generation en-
ergy storage devices.[3–5] All-solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries

The key of preparing composite cathodes for ASSLSBs is to
achieve proper distribution of sulfur, carbon, and SSE, which
is essential for creating an eﬀective triple-phase interface to
promote stable electrochemical reactions. So far, researchers
have adopted multiple strategies to develop sulfur compos-
ite cathodes, such as carbon material design,[15–18] electrolyte
optimization,[19,20] and composite process development.[21,22] Al-
though a large amount of triple-phase interface is generated dur-
ing the material preparation process, the ion transport property
deteriorates rapidly under high-loading and thick cathode con-

which hinders the practical application of ASSLSBs.[23–25] Re-
cently, Matsuda et al. studied the redox chemistry of the Li2S/SSE

troscopy, conﬁrming that the electrochemical decomposition re-
actions of the sulﬁde SSE increase the interfacial impedance and
deteriorate battery cycling performance.[26] Although instability
of sulﬁde SSE at the interface with conductive carbon has been
veriﬁed, the strategy to solve the decomposition of electrolyte in
the sulfur cathode has not been studied.[27] Therefore, in-depth
study of the degradation mechanism of SSE and exploration
of eﬀective modiﬁcation technologies are the keys to designing

Surface modiﬁcation of cathode materials is an eﬀective way
to enhance the Li+ transfer eﬃciency and suppress the sul-
ﬁde SSE decomposition.[28,29] Atomic/molecular layer deposition
(ALD/MLD) are powerful surface-controlled technologies with a
unique growth mechanism that can achieve precise nanoscale
thickness and excellent consistency.[30,31] Recently, ALD has
been used to tailor artiﬁcial coatings on oxide cathode mate-
rials to improve the electrochemical performance of all-solid-
state lithium batteries (ASSLBs).[32,33] Various coating materi-
als such as ZrO2,[34] Al2O3,[35] LiAlO2,[36] LPON,[37] Li3PO4,[32,38]
LiNbO3,[39] and Al-GL[40] thin ﬁlms have been successfully syn-
thesized by ALD/MLD technology. Proper modiﬁcations made
by ALD technology have been proven to suppress intergranular
cracks, stabilize the cathode/SSE interface to avoid side reactions
and the formation of space-charge layers, and facilitate the rapid
transport of Li+ at the cathode/SSE interface. In addition, ALD-
Al2O3 or MLD-alucone ﬁlm have been studied as an eﬀective pro-
tecting layer for carbon/sulfur (C/S) composite materials to pre-
vent the direct contact with solvent molecules, thereby eliminat-
ing polysulﬁdes issues and improving the cycling performance
of Li‒S batteries.[41,42] Although various interfacial coatings have
been explored to control the degradation of oxide cathodes and
dissolution of sulfur cathodes, there is currently a lack of rele-
vant research on the use of ALD coatings for ASSLSB.
In this work, we report the surface modiﬁcation engineering
through nanoscale lithium phosphorus oxide (hereafter referred
to as LPO) coating on the surface of C/S particles using ALD
technology. Characterization techniques such as NMR, XPS, and
TOF-SIMS were used to study the modiﬁcation mechanism of
the LPO coating layer on the sulfur cathode. It can be specu-
lated that the LPO coating functions as an ion-conducting layer to
enhance the Li+ transport between the active material and elec-
trolyte. ALD-derived LPO coating with nanoscale thickness and
toughness can eﬀectively suppress the volume expansion of the
electrode, thereby maintaining the eﬀective contact between CS
particles and SSE. More importantly, the electrochemical stabil-
ity of sulﬁde SSE is improved due to the electronic insulation of
LPO. As a result, ASSLSBs using LPO coated C/S cathodes ex-
hibit stable cycling performance at high areal capacity and high
current density. These results demonstrate that ALD technique
is an eﬃcient, scalable, and controllable synthetic strategy for de-
signing sulfur cathode materials for ASSLSBs.
2. Results and Discussion
Figure 1a shows the deposition process of LPO layer by ALD tech-
nique. First, lithium tert-butoxide (LiOtBu) is introduced to the
material by interacting with the hydroxyl groups on the surface
of the C/S substrate. In the second step, LiOH is generated on the
surface of the substrate under the action of H2O and tert-butanol
is released. Then, methyl phosphate is generated on the surface
through the reaction of TMP and LiOH accompanied by the re-
lease of CH3OH. Finally, LiOtBu is introduced again to react with
methoxyl groups to obtain the product LPO. The ALD process can
be cycled to generate the desired thickness.
Two diﬀerent synthetic routes for sulfur cathodes are shown
in Figure 1b, in which ALD-derived LPO is deposited on the sur-
face of acetylene black (AB) and AB/sulfur (AB/S), respectively.
The obtained samples are marked as LPO@AB and LPO@AB/S,
respectively. The morphologies of the bare and coated materials
were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). As
shown in Figure S1a,b (Supporting Information), AB is a small
particle with a diameter of ≈100 nm. After ALD coating process,
a blurry coating layer is observed on the surface of LPO@AB,
and the morphology of AB particles is well maintained. In ad-
dition, the corresponding energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS)
mapping results (Figure S1c, Supporting Information) show that
P and O are evenly distributed on the surface of AB, indicating
that the LPO coating was successfully formed on the AB parti-
cles using the ALD technology. The obtained LPO@AB and sul-
fur powder were then subjected to high-energy ball milling to
prepare sulfur composite materials (denoted as LPO@AB-S).
The AB/S composite was prepared by ball milling, and then
LPO@AB/S was obtained by the ALD technology. The surface of
AB/S particles (Figure S2a–c, Supporting Information) is coarse,
with a diameter of ≈30 μm. SEM and corresponding EDS results
(Figure S2d–g, Supporting Information) show that the morphol-
ogy of AB/S particles is unchanged and the LPO layer is success-
fully coated on the surface of AB/S after the ALD process. X-ray
diﬀraction (XRD) patterns of AB/S and LPO@AB/S (Figure S3a,
Supporting Information) indicate that the crystallinity of sub-
limated sulfur is well maintained without noticeable impurity
phases. TG analysis (Figure S3b, Supporting Information) shows
that the sulfur content in AB/S and LPO@AB/S composites de-
crease from 64.3% to 62.1% after ALD treatment, which may be
attributed to the heating process. These results indicate that the
coating process by the ALD technique has little eﬀect on the mor-
phology and crystal structure of sulfur composites.
High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM)
was employed to conﬁrm the presence and morphology of the
LPO coating layer. Figure 2a shows the HRTEM image of AB/S
material. The highly ordered carbon stripes originating from
AB were observed in the HRTEM image. The HRTEM image
of LPO@AB/S shows that the LPO coating is an amorphous
structure with a thickness of ≈5 nm (Figure 2b). EDS mapping
(Figure 2c) exhibits an even distribution of C, S and P elements,
indicating that LPO has been introduced onto the AB/S particle.

To explore the eﬀect of the LPO coating layer on improv-
ing interfacial reaction kinetics, the carrier transport property of
AB/S, LPO@AB-S and LPO@AB/S cathode composites are mea-
sured using DC polarization technique. Sulfur cathode compos-
ites were synthesized by high-energy ball milling. The commer-
cial Li7P3S11 (LPS) SSE was mixed with AB/S, LPO@AB-S and
LPO@AB/S respectively to form small particles and evenly dis-
tributed on the surface of C/S particles (Figure S5, Supporting In-
formation). For electronic/ionic mixed conductors, ion-blocking
and electron-blocking structures (Figure S6a,b, Supporting In-
formation) are used to distinguish the electronic/ionic transport
properties.[46] Figure 3a,b presents the time-current response
curves of symmetric cells with diﬀerent composite materials un-
der a constant external voltage from −40 to 40 mV, respectively.
By applying a constant voltage to the sample, the electronic and
ionic resistance of cathode composites (Figure S6c,d, Supporting
Information) can be obtained according to Ohm’s law (Rk, k =
e or Li+). The ionic and electronic conductivity of cathode com-
posites are shown in Figure 3c and Table S1 (Supporting Infor-
mation). According to the literature, ALD LPO ﬁlms are in amor-
phous state with ionic conductivity of ≈10−8 S cm−1 at 25 °C.[30]
It is worth noting that the ionic conductivities of LPO@AB-S and
LPO@AB/S at 30 °C are 1.1 × 10−5 S cm−1 and 4.4 × 10−5 S cm−1,
respectively, which are both higher than that of AB/S (9.1×10−6).
That result is attributed to the enhanced ion transport capability
of LPO@AB-S and LPO@AB/S by the ion-conducting LPO coat-
ing compared to the uncoated material. However, it should be
mentioned that LPO coating reduces the electronic conductivity
of the cathode composites, but the electronic conductivity on the
order of 10−3 can guarantee the electronic conductivity require-
ments for battery performance.[22] It can be concluded that ionic
transport in the cathode composite is the major issue compared
to electron transport.

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy experiments
were carried out to further investigate the impact of the LPO coat-
ing. As shown in Figure 3e, the 1D 6Li magic angle spinning
(MAS) NMR spectra display the three resonances with chemical
shifts of 0.14, 0.64, and 2.35 ppm, representing Li in LPO coating,
LPS, and Li2S, respectively. After lithiation of LPO@AB/S-LPS
cathode, Li+ environment attributed to LPS and Li2S is observed
in the 1D spectra. Compared to the individual Li2S, the broader
resonance in discharge product Li2S indicates poor Li+ transport
capability in the cathode.[48] However, the LPO resonance cannot
be distinguished at the cathode due to the close chemical shifts
of LPO and LPS. The schematic diagram in Figure 3f illustrates
the role of the LPO coating between the C/S electrode and SSE,
which promotes Li+ exchange and blocks electronic transfer dur-
ing charge and discharge.
The electrochemical performance of diﬀerent sulfur cathodes
was tested to evaluate the eﬀect of LPO coating layer. ASSLSBs
were assembled in the homemade battery devices, using diﬀer-
ent sulfur composites as cathode, Li6P5SCl (LPSCl) as SSE layer
and Li–In as anode. Figure 4a shows the initial charge–discharge
curves of AB/S, LPO@AB-S, and LPO@AB/S cathodes at 0.2 C
and 30 °C. The initial discharge capacities of AB/S, LPO@AB-
S, and LPO@AB/S cathode are 1247, 1136, and 1322 mAh g−1,
respectively. Compared to AB/S and LPO@AB-S, LPO@AB/S
delivers higher discharge capacity, indicating the higher active
sulfur utilization. In addition, the LPO@AB/S cathode exhibits
minimal voltage polarization in dQ/dV curve during the initial
charge–discharge process (Figure S8, Supporting Information).
In contrast, LPO@AB-S exhibits the lowest discharge capacity
and largest overpotential, which is attributed to the fact that the
non-conductive LPO coating hinders the electronic conduction
between LPO@AB and S, resulting in reduced reaction kinet-
ics. These results indicate that LPO coating on the surface of
AB/S can increase the Li+ dynamics in the cathode. CV curves of
ASSLSBs with diﬀerent sulfur cathodes were recorded at a scan
rate of 0.05 mV s−1 over a voltage range of 0.9–2.4 V at 30 °C.
In the initial 15 cycles, the peak current of AB/S cathode gradu-
ally increases (Figure S9a, Supporting Information), which is at-
tributed to the activation of sulfur by the electrochemical decom-
position reactions of LPS during the initial charge process.[24]
However, the peak current decreased rapidly in subsequent cy-
cles, indicating poor electrochemical reversibility and stability. In
contrast, the peak current of LPO@AB/S cathode is higher than
that of AB/S and LPO@AB-S (Figure S9b,c, Supporting Infor-
mation), which is consistent with the charge/discharge process.
In addition, the LPO@AB/S cathode shows better curve coinci-
dence, indicating superior reversibility of the electrochemical re-
action process.
Figure 4b compares the cycling performance of ASSLSBs with
diﬀerent sulfur cathodes. After 300 cycles at 0.2 C (0.3 A g−1),
LPO@AB/S retains a high discharge capacity of 1070 mAh g−1
with a capacity retention of 86.4%. In contrast, AB/S and
LPO@AB-S cathodes exhibit severe capacity fading, leaving low
reversible capacities of 859 and 665 mAh g−1, corresponding
to 72.5% and 59.4% capacity retention. The electrochemical re-
versibility of AB/S, LPO@AB-S, and LPO@AB/S is further com-
pared through the charge–discharge curves during the 300 cycles.
Figure 4c–e shows the charging and discharging voltage curves of
the AB/S, LPO@AB-S, and LPO@AB/S cathodes. As the number
of cycling increases, the charge–discharge voltage polarization of
AB/S and LPO@AB-S cathodes increases signiﬁcantly, indicat-
ing severe electrochemical failure. In contrast, the LPO@AB/S
cathode exhibits an essentially unchanged charge–discharge volt-
age plateau after 300 cycles, which is attributed to the stable inter-
face and smooth Li+ migration conferred by the LPO coating.[49]
Figure S10 (Supporting Information) shows the comparison of
EIS proﬁles of AB/S and LPO@AB/S cells before and after cy-
cling. LPO@AB/S cell exhibits a much smaller increase in inter-
face resistance than that of AB/S, conﬁrming that LPO coating
improves the interface stability during cycling.
The rate performance of ASSLSBs with AB/S, LPO@AB-S,
and LPO@AB/S cathodes is evaluated at diﬀerent current den-
sity. As shown in Figure S11a (Supporting Information), the re-
versible discharge capacities of LPO@AB/S cathode are 1468,
1239, 855, and 480 mAh g−1 at 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, and 1 C, respec-
tively. The AB/S and LPO@AB-S cathodes exhibit very low ca-
pacities of 412 and 321 mAh g−1 at high rate of 1C, respec-
tively. As the current density increases, the LPO@AB/S cath-
ode exhibits smaller charge–discharge overpotential compared
to AB/S and LPO@AB-S electrodes (Figure S11b–d, Support-
ing Information). The excellent rate performance of LPO@AB/S
reveals enhanced electrochemical reaction kinetics, which is at-
tributed to the smooth Li+ transport achieved by LPO coating.
While the LPO coating layer increases the Li+ dynamics at in-
terface, the thickness and electronic insulation of the coating
layer have a signiﬁcant impact on reaction kinetics.[47] The thick-
ness of the LPO layer can be adjusted by changing the num-
ber of ALD coating cycles. Increasing the LPO coating thickness
to 24 cycles, the morphology of the C/S particles remains un-
changed (Figure S12, Supporting Information). However, com-
pared to the 12LPO@AB/S cathode, the 24LPO@AB/S cathode
exhibits reduced discharge capacity and poor rate performance
(Figure S13, Supporting Information). Overall, the electrochem-
ical performance of ASSLSBs is related to the LPO coating thick-
ness on the cathode, which corresponds to fact that LPO coat-
ing increases the ionic conductivity but decreases the electronic
conductivity.

contact failure caused by stress change during cycling.

3. Conclusion
In summary, we have successfully synthesized a thin and control-
lable LPO coating using ALD technology to enhance the stability
of sulfur cathode for ASSLSBs. The as-prepared LPO coating has
nanoscale thickness and ionic conductivity, which is beneﬁcial
to facilitate Li+ transport between the active sulfur and the elec-
trolyte. In addition, the robust LPO coating signiﬁcantly alleviates
the volume change of sulfur during charge/discharge process,
eﬀectively inhibiting the mechanical cracking failure of the sul-
fur cathode. In-depth analysis by Raman, XPS, and TOF-SIMS
demonstrates that the electronically inert LPO coating can eﬀec-
tively suppress the electrochemical decomposition of the sulﬁde
SSE. Therefore, ASSLSBs exhibit high speciﬁc capacity, long cy-
cling stability, and superior high-temperature performance. This
work indicates that rational cathode structure design has the syn-
ergistic eﬀect of improving charge transport capability and elim-
inating chemical mechanical failure, which is crucial to realize
high-performance ASSLSBs.
