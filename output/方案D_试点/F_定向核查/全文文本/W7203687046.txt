ABSTRACT 
Lithium argyrodites hold great promise as solid electrolytes for all-solid-state batteries (ASSBs) owing to their high room- 
temperature ionic conductivity and soft mechanical properties. However, their poor air stability hinders large-scale commercial 
applications. To overcome this, we demonstrate herein a high-entropy design strategy by substituting the P-site in Li6 PS5 I with Ge, 
Sb, Si, and As, yielding Li6.5 As0.25 Si0.25 Ge0.25 Sb0.25 S5 I with a configurational entropy of 2.04 R . Neutron powder diffraction (NPD) 
and various spectroscopic analyses indicate that the compositional complexity increases structural (occupational) disorder and 
facilitates lithium transport, resulting in room-temperature ionic conductivities of 8.0 mS cm− 1 in cold-pressed state and 11.4 mS 
cm− 1 for hot-pressed pellets, the latter being significantly higher compared to the glassy counterpart Li6 PS5 I (1.5 mS cm− 1 ). HEA- 
As0.25 also demonstrates much improved chemical stability against moist air and toluene. When integrated into full cells with 
a sulfur cathode and an In/InLi anode, the respective all-solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries exhibit excellent cycling stability. 
Overall, this work provides a new approach for designing advanced sulfide solid electrolytes that simultaneously possess high 
ionic conductivity and air stability. 
1 
Introduction 
Replacing flammable organic (liquid) electrolytes with inorganic 
(sulfide) solid electrolytes (SEs) is a pivotal route towards safer 
all-solid-state batteries (ASSBs) with higher energy density [ 1–
3 ]. Among various sulfides, lithium argyrodites are particularly 
attractive owing to their high ionic conductivity and good 
deformability [ 4 ]. Specifically, Li6 PS5 X (X = Cl− , Br− ) SEs possess 
high room-temperature ionic conductivities of 1–5 mS cm− 1 due to 
S2 − /X− disorder [ 5, 6 ]. Typically, the structural disorder facilitates 
Li+ migration through interconnected cages. Conversely, the 
large ionic radius of iodine drives a complete ordering of the anion 
sublattice in Li6 PS5 I. This ordered structure hinders Li+ hopping 
between the cages, resulting in a low ionic conductivity of 10− 3 
mS cm− 1 [ 7 ]. These results demonstrate that the degree of anion 
ordering has a strong influence on ionic conductivity.

Several strategies have been explored to enhance the ionic 
conductivity of Li6 PS5 I. Cationic substitution at the P-site with 
Ge4 + , Sb5 + , or Si4 + introduces beneficial S2 − /I− disorder, thereby 
increasing the ionic conductivity to 5.4, 2.0, and 0.1 mS cm− 1 , 
respectively [ 8 ]. Partial substitution of sulfur with chlorine 
enables the synthesis of the halogen-rich lithium argyrodite 
Li5.5 PS4.5 Cl1.5 , exhibiting an ionic conductivity of 9.4 mS cm− 1 [ 9 ]. 
Similarly, an iodine-rich glassy electrolyte, Li5.6 PS4.6 I1.4 , prepared 
by high-energy ball milling, shows an ionic conductivity of 
about 2 mS cm− 1 [ 10 ]. Notably, compositional disorder (entropy) 
introduced by Cl− /Br− co-doping yields Li5.5 PS4.5 Cl0.75 Br0.75 with 
an impressive ionic conductivity of 13 mS cm− 1 [ 11 ]. Despite 
achieving room-temperature ionic conductivities comparable to 
those of liquid electrolytes, these sulfide SEs suffer from poor air 
stability. They readily react with atmospheric moisture, releasing 
toxic H2 S while undergoing structural degradation that compro- 
mises ionic transport [ 12 ]. Furthermore, their susceptibility to 
nucleophilic attack in polar solvents poses additional challenges 
for solution-based processing [ 13 ]. Overcoming these stability 
problems while preserving high ionic conductivity remains a 
critical bottleneck for their practical application. 
Chemical doping/substitution is widely used to optimize the 
air stability of sulfide SEs. This often involves replacing a 
fraction of the hard-acid P5 + with soft-acid cations [ 14 ]. For 
example, by doping Li3 PS4 with ZnO, a new SE of composition 
Li3 + 3 x P1 − x Znx S4 − x Ox with an ionic conductivity of 1 mS cm− 1 was 
synthesized, which can significantly improve the air stability of 
Li3 PS4 [ 15 ]. Similarly, doping of Li3 PS4 with Bi2 O3 has been shown 
to increase the air stability by forming [BiS4 ]3 − (with higher 
hydrolysis energy than [PS4 ]3 − ) and [PS3 O]3 − (with stronger H2 O 
adsorption energy than [PS4 ]3 − ), though the ionic conductivity 
remains below 3 mS cm− 1 [ 16 ]. Strategies such as co-doping 
with Sb–O or Sn–O in lithium argyrodites (e.g., Li5.5 PS4.5 Cl1.5 ) 
can achieve higher conductivities, but the improvements in air 
stability are modest [ 17, 18 ]. Complete replacement of P5 + with 
soft-acid cations, like Sn4 + , As5 + , or Sb5 + , yields SEs such as 
Li3.85 Sn0.85 Sb0.15 S4 (0.85 mS cm− 1 ) [ 19 ] or Li3.875 Sn0.875 As0.125 S4 (2.45 
mS cm− 1 ) [ 20 ] with excellent stability against moisture. However, 
their conductivities are still insufficient for practical applications. 
Surface coating represents another effective strategy for increas- 
ing the air stability of sulfide SEs. For example, exposing Li6 PS5 Cl 
to an oxygen atmosphere enables the in situ formation of a 
∼ 50 nm oxygen-rich passivation layer on its free surface [ 12 ], 
which effectively suppresses hydrolysis. Similarly, the in situ 
decomposition of NH4 F on Li10 GeP2 S12 generates a LiF coating 
that significantly reduces H2 S release, albeit at the cost of 
decreased ionic conductivity (from 6.2 to 2.5 mS cm− 1 ) [ 21 ]. 
Therefore, achieving improved air stability without sacrificing 
lithium mobility remains a pivotal requirement for practical 
applications of ASSBs. 
In the present work, by substituting phosphorus with a qua- 
ternary combination of soft-acid cations (As5 + , Si4 + , Ge4 + , 
Sb5 + ), we synthesized a novel high-entropy lithium argyrodite, 
Li6.5 As0.25 Si0.25 Ge0.25 Sb0.25 S5 I (HEA-As0.25 ), with a configurational 
entropy of 2.04 R . This SE achieves room-temperature ionic 
conductivities of 8.0 and 11.4 mS cm− 1 upon cold and hot pressing, 
respectively, a fivefold enhancement over its unmodified (glassy) 
counterpart Li6 PS5 I. The underlying ion conduction mechanism

Results and Discussion 
2.1 
Structural and Morphological 
Characterization 
Following the principles of hard and soft acids and bases (HSAB) 
and the hydrolysis energy of elements, we designed a high- 
entropy sulf ide SE by co-substituting P5 + at the 4 b site in Li6 PS5 I 
with Si4 + , Ge4 + , As5 + , and Sb5 + . The target composition, HEA- 
As0.25 , was achieved by high-energy ball milling, followed by 
annealing at 550◦C. For comparison, a series of control samples 
was prepared, including Li6.5 P0.25 Si0.25 Ge0.25 Sb0.25 S5 I (HEA-P0.25 ), 
crystalline Li6 PS5 I (c-LPSI), crystalline Li5.6 PS4.6 I1.4 (c-LPSI1.4 ), 
glassy Li6 PS5 I (g-LPSI), and glassy Li5.6 PS4.6 I1.4 (g-LPSI1.4 ). Both 
g-LPSI and g-LPSI1.4 were obtained by high-energy ball milling, 
whereas c-LPSI and c-LPSI1.4 were produced via conventional 
solid-state synthesis. The compositions and corresponding abbre- 
viations of the above SEs are provided in Table 1 . 
As shown in the x-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns in Figure 1a , 
HEA-As0.25 , HEA-P0.25 , and c-LPSI form single-phase argyrodite 
phases, while g-LPSI and g-LPSI1.4 exhibit characteristics of 
amorphous materials. However, as evident from Figure S1 , the c- 
LPSI1.4 SE synthesized by dry mixing through gentle ball milling 
and subsequent annealing contains several impurity phases.

(see Figure S2 ). For c-LPSI, g-LPSI, and their iodine-rich coun- 
terparts, a characteristic peak at about 416 cm− 1 is observed, 
corresponding to the [PS4 ]3 − units [ 22 ]. In the glassy g-LPSI 
and g-LPSI1.4 samples, an additional weak signal at 378 cm− 1 
emerges, which can be attributed to [P2 S6 ]4 − [ 6 ], indicating local

Figure 1d shows the 29 Si MAS NMR spectra of HEA-As0.25 and 
HEA-P0.25 . A sharp resonance at ∼ 11.3 ppm is observed, corre-

The 7 Li MAS NMR spectra (see Figure S3 ) show a single broad 
resonance at ∼ 1.6 ppm for the crystalline samples (HEA-As0.25 , 
HEA-P0.25 , and c-LPSI). In contrast, the glassy SEs, g-LPSI1.4 and 
g-LPSI, exhibit two distinct resonances at ∼ 0.2 and ∼ 2.3 ppm,

enable ion transport in the amorphous phase. The 31 P MAS 
NMR spectra (see Figure S3 ) reveal that HEA-P0.25 exhibits 
a broader resonance compared to c-LPSI, reflecting increased 
chemical disorder at the P-site, as expected. Collectively, these 
results confirm the successful formation of high-entropy lithium 
argyrodites with substantial occupational disorder across both the 
cation and anion sublattices. 
The morphology of the HEA-As0.25 SE and the element distri- 
bution were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) 
coupled with energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDS), as 
shown in Figures 1e–j and S4 . The particles exhibit a diameter of 
about 5 µm. EDS mapping revealed that the constituent elements 
(As5 + , Si4 + , Ge4 + , Sb5 + , S2 − , and I− ) are evenly distributed 
throughout the particles without any localized enrichment or 
segregation. Similarly, the control samples, HEA-P0.25 , g-LPSI1.4 , 
and g-LPSI, were found to exhibit uniform element distributions 
(see Figure S4 ), further confirming their phase purity.

2.3 
Air and Solvent Stability 
The practical application of sulfide SEs is severely hindered 
by their intrinsic moisture sensitivity, which leads to structural 
degradation, H2 S release, and loss of ionic conductivity upon 
exposure to ambient air [ 14 ]. The moisture stability of the 
synthesized SEs was assessed by monitoring the evolution of 
H2 S with exposure to N2 atmosphere with 40% relative humidity 
(RH) for 30 min (see Figure 3a ) at room temperature. Among 
the samples, the glassy g-LPSI SE was found to exhibit the 
fastest and strongest H2 S release. Increasing the iodine content 
(g-LPSI1.4 ) somewhat decreases the initial release rate and final 
concentration of H2 S, although the improvement remains lim- 
ited. In contrast, HEA-P0.25 , designed by partially substituting P5 + 
with soft-acid cations (Si4 + , Ge4 + , Sb5 + ) according to the HSAB 
principle, demonstrates markedly reduced H2 S evolution ( ∼ 37% 
of that released by g-LPSI). Complete replacement of P5 + in HEA- 
P0.25 with As5 + yields the most stable SE (HEA-As0.25 ), with H2 S 
formation suppressed to ∼ 30% of the g-LPSI value. This increased 
stability stems from the stronger bonding between the soft- 
acid cations and S2 − anions, thereby lowering their susceptible 
to hydrolysis. These results provide evidence that the high- 
entropy strategy, through the incorporation of soft-acid elements, 
effectively stabilizes the sulfide framework against hydrolytic 
decomposition. 
The stability was further evaluated by monitoring the retention 
of ionic conductivity upon exposure to ambient air for 60 min at 
room temperature (see Figure 3b ). In line with the above results, 
HEA-As0.25 showed the highest retention, maintaining 82% of 
its initial conductivity, followed by HEA-P0.25 with 79%. In stark 
contrast, the control samples, g-LPSI and g-LPSI1.4 , suffered from 
severe degradation, retaining only 21% and 18% of their original 
conductivities, respectively. This agrees well with the significant 
H2 S release observed for both SEs. Raman spectra of the same 
SEs acquired after air exposure (see Figure S7 ) show no major 
changes compared to the pristine state, suggesting that the bulk 
structure remains largely unaffected. The observed decrease in 
ionic conductivity is likely due in part to surface amorphization 
and/or partial decomposition. This demonstrates that the high- 
entropy strategy applied in this work not only reduces H2 S 
evolution, but also mitigates the loss of ionic conductivity upon 
exposure to moisture. 
The compatibility of sulfide SEs with nonpolar solvents, such as 
toluene, is critical for their integration into scalable manufactur- 
ing processes. Therefore, the tolerance of the SEs described herein 
was assessed by immersing them into toluene for 60 min at room 
temperature (see Figure 3c ). The high-entropy HEA-As0.25 was 
found to retain a high ionic conductivity of 6.6 mS cm− 1 after 
toluene exposure, demonstrating excellent solvent resistance. In 
contrast, the room-temperature ionic conductivities of g-LPSI and 
g-LPSI1.4 deceased strongly to 0.6–0.75 mS cm− 1 , corresponding 
to losses of over 50% compared to the initial values. Raman 
spectra acquired before and after solvent exposure (see Figure 
S8 ) again show no bulk structural changes, suggesting that the 
conductivity loss may arise from surface amorphization and/or

2.4 
Battery Performance 
To demonstrate the practical applicability of HEA-As0.25 , all- 
solid- state lithium- sulfur batteries with a sulfur/MWCNT/HEA- 
As0.25 composite cathode and an In/InLi anode were assem- 
bled and tested electrochemically at room temperature in a 
potential range from 1.0 to 2.7 V versus In/InLi (correspond- 
ing to approx. 1.62 ‒3.32 V vs. Li+ /Li). Figure 4a shows the 
long-term cycling performance of a representative In/InLi|HEA- 
As0.25 |sulfur/MWCNTs/HEA-As0.25 cell with a sulfur loading of 
∼ 1.5 mg cm− 2 at a rate of 0.1C. The cell delivered an initial 
specific discharge specific capacity of 1302 mAh g− 1 , followed by 
1299 mAh g− 1 in the second cycle. Remarkably, even after 200 
cycles, the cell was still capable of maintaining a high specific 
discharge capacity of 931 mAh g− 1 . At a higher rate of 0.2C (see 
Figure 4b ), the cells also exhibited stable cycling, maintaining a 
specific discharge capacity of about 886 mAh g− 1 after 100 cycles. 
As can be seen from Figure 4c , the voltage profiles for the third 
and 100th cycles show good overlap, thus indicating minimal 
polarization and stable interfacial contact throughout cycling. 
These results validate the effectiveness of the high-entropy 
design in enabling both high capacity and long-term stability in 
ASSBs.

within the cathode occurs. Concurrently, minor decomposition 
products from the sulfide SE may contribute additional capacity 
[ 34 ]. These combined effects account for the gradual increase in 
specific discharge capacity observed over the first 20 cycles or so 
(see Figure 4a ). 
The Ge 3d spectra (Figure 5b ) show a peak at 31.8 eV correspond- 
ing to [GeS4 ]4 − , along with a peak at 29.6 eV that may be attributed 
to reduced Ge species [ 32, 33 ]. An additional component at 33.8 eV 
is assigned to Ge–O bonds [ 25 ], with the oxygen originating 
from the functional groups present on the MWCNTs. Upon 
discharge to 1.0 V versus In/InLi, the intensity of the peak indi- 
cating Ge–S–Ge bridging species decreases, while the [GeS4 ]4 − 
signal intensifies. This evolution reflects the reductive cleavage 
of Ge–S–Ge bonds to form [GeS4 ]4 − units. During subsequent 
charging to 2.7 V versus In/InLi, the reverse transformation 
occurs, indicating the reversible nature of this kind of structural 
rearrangement. 
The Si 2p spectra (see Figure 5c ) can be deconvoluted into 
contributions related to [SiS4 ]4 − and oxygenated silicon species 
[ 29, 35 ]. Upon discharge to 1.0 V versus In/InLi, the [SiS4 ]4 − 
signal diminishes slightly, accompanied by an increase in the oxy- 
genated silicon component. As cycling proceeds, the oxygenated 
silicon signal continues to grow, suggesting progressive formation 
of Si–O bonds during battery operation. 
From the Sb 3d spectra (see Figure S9 ), it can be seen that the 
relative intensity of the [SbS4 ]3 − peaks decreases, while the O 1s- 
related signal increases during charging [ 23 ]. Upon discharge, 
this trend is reversed. In contrast, the As 3d and I 3d spectra 
(see Figure S9 ) exhibit no significant changes during both charge 
and discharge, confirming the electrochemical stability of both 
As5 + and I− in the high-entropy structure. This stability likely 
contributes to the robust cycling performance of the HEA-As0.25 - 
based all-solid-state lithium-sulfur cells.

Conclusions 
In summary, a chemically complex sulfide solid electrolyte, HEA- 
As0.25 , with a configurational entropy of 2.04 R was successfully 
synthesized by substituting P5 + in LPSI with four soft-acid 
cations, namely, As5 + , Si4 + , Ge4 + , and Sb5 + . Comprehensive 
structural characterization by XRD, NPD, Raman spectroscopy, 
and solid-state NMR confirmed the lithium argyrodite structure 
with even element distribution at the 4 b site and no detectable 
impurities. Notably, HEA-As0.25 was found to possess a high 
ionic conductivity of 8.0 and 11.4 mS cm− 1 in cold- and hot- 
pressed states, respectively, representing a fivefold improvement 
over the glassy counterpart LPSI (1.5 mS cm− 1 ). The activation 
energy of HEA-As0.25 was also markedly reduced to 0.167 eV, 
compared to 0.35 eV for g-LPSI, underscoring that the high- 
entropy design effectively lowers the energy barrier for lithium
