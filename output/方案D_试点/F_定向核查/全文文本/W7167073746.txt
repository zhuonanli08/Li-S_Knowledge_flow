ABSTRACT
All-solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) promise high energy density and intrinsic safety, yet their performance
is fundamentally constrained by unstable triple-phase interfaces among sulfur, conductive carbon, and solid electrolytes.
Such instability leads to sluggish solid–solid sulfur redox kinetics, hindered charge transport, and severe chemo-mechanical
degradation. Herein, we demonstrate a biomolecular strategy using astaxanthin (AXT) as an electron-switching interfacial
regulator to simultaneously address these coupled challenges. Combined experimental and theoretical analysis reveal that
AXT modulates electrolyte decomposition pathways in a coverage-dependent manner via a localized “electron pocket” effect,
favoring the formation of electrochemically active Li2S over insulating LiCl. Meanwhile, polar oxygen functional groups in AXT
establish low-potential corridors that facilitate Li+ transport and stabilize key intermediates, thereby accelerating sulfur redox
kinetics. In addition, the chain-like molecular architecture of AXT acts as a flexible scaffold to buffer volume fluctuations and
preserve interfacial contact integrity during cycling. Consequently, AXT-modified ASSLSBs achieve exceptional electrochemical
performance under high sulfur loading conditions, delivering an areal capacity of 16.56 mAh cm−2 at 9.49 mg cm−2 sulfur loading.
This work establishes a biomolecule-driven electronic engineering paradigm for programmable interface chemistry, offering a
general strategy toward high-energy-density and durable solid-state batteries.

Introduction
The pursuit of high-energy-density, low cost-effectiveness, and
intrinsically safe storage systems has positioned all-solid-state
lithium–sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) as one of the most promising
next-generation technologies. By integrating ultrahigh theoretical
capacity of sulfur with nonflammable solid electrolytes (SEs),
ASSLSBs offer a compelling pathway toward energy densi-
ties beyond conventional lithium-ion batteries while mitigating
safety concerns associated with liquid electrolytes. Especially,
sulfide solid electrolytes (SSEs) stand out from various SEs
due to their outstanding ionic conductivity (>10−3 S cm−1),
favorable mechanical deformability and excellent compatibility
with sulfur cathode [1, 2]. Despite rapid progress, however,
their practical implementation has been fundamentally hindered
by the complex and unstable nature of triple-phase interface
between active materials, conductivity agent and SSEs, fea-
tured with sluggish solid–solid sulfur redox reaction (SRR)

failures [3–7].
At this buried interface, electrochemical, electronic, and mechan-
ical processes are strongly coupled, under which researchers
have developed various conductive carbon (e.g., doped carbon
[8], carbon nanocages [9], and 3D carbon framework [10] etc.)
into sulfur cathode to promote the conversion of active materials
(sulfur and Li2S, a fully lithiated form of sulfur), enhanc-
ing ionic/electronic transport and electrochemical-mechanical
stability [11–13]. Unfortunately, these issues cannot be simul-
taneously solved especially under high sulfur loading condi-
tions, as determined by the nature of intertwined interfaces,
as outlined below: (1) with the increment of sulfur loading,
cathode thickness inevitably magnifies long-range conducting
path, deteriorating rapid Li+ and electron conduction [14]; (2)
more conductive carbon will directly trigger the intense decom-
position of SSEs at charge/discharge processes due to intense
electron transfer from P─S bond to Li─Cl bond, which usually
generates inert substance LiCl accumulated at the cathode/SSEs
interface, ultimately affecting the interfacial mass transfer and
leading to poor surface reaction kinetics [15–19]; (3) the large
volumetric fluctuations of sulfur species during cycling induce
substantial chemo-mechanical stress, resulting in contact loss
and interfacial fracture [9–11, 20]. Clearly, existing strategies
have primarily focused on introducing conductive frameworks
or catalytic hosts to enhance charge transport and sulfur utiliza-
tion, which have typically addressed individual aspects of the
problem and often exacerbate the others. For instance, increasing
electronic conductivity can intensify electrolyte decomposition,
whereas rigid structural designs may fail to accommodate volume
changes. Under this context, a fundamental question arises:
how can one simultaneously regulate interfacial reaction path-
ways, transport properties, and mechanical stability in a unified
manner?
From a mechanistic perspective, the key lies in controlling
electron distribution and transfer at the interface, which governs
both electrolyte decomposition chemistry and sulfur conversion
pathways. If electron flow can be spatially confined or redi-
rected, it may become possible to selectively favor beneficial
reactions while suppressing detrimental ones. Such “electron-
level” regulation represents a largely unexplored but potentially
transformative strategy for interface engineering in ASSLSBs.
Following such understanding, we herein propose a biomolecule-
driven electronic regulation approach by introducing astaxanthin
(AXT), a naturally occurring conjugated molecule with rich
redox-active functional groups [21–23], into the sulfur cathode.
Distinct from conventional additives, AXT presents unique struc-
ture and modulation capacity, including: (1) its unsaturated
hydroxyl and carbonyl groups can attract unpaired electrons from
external molecules or provide electrons to outside, which will
endow it with a significant antioxidant activity for accelerated
SRR kinetics and play important roles in regulating the decom-
position of electrolytes [18, 24]; (2) the high sensitivity of excited
states to small changes in 13 conjugated double bonds of AXT
allows it to participate in electron/ion transfer and reduce energy
barriers, showing a good interface regulation capability of AXT;
(3) its rigid chain-like structure can enhance the flexibility and
interfacial adhesion of the system, which is expected to cushion
the volume changes of sulfur cathode [25].
By integrating these functionalities, this work establishes a uni-
fied biomolecular framework for regulating triple-phase interface
chemistry, in which electron transfer, Li+ transport, and mechan-
ical evolution are cogoverned at the molecular level. As a result,
AXT not only redirects electrolyte decomposition toward electro-
chemically active Li2S, but also accelerates sulfur redox kinetics
and alleviates chemo-mechanical degradation under high sulfur
loading. Consequently, the resulting ASSLSBs deliver high areal
capacities, robust rate capability, and durable cycling stability,
including 16.56 mAh cm−2 at 9.49 mg cm−2 sulfur loading.
These findings highlight biomolecular electronic regulation as a
promising general strategy for high-energy-density and durable
solid-state batteries.

Results and Discussion
2.1
AXT-Regulated Interfacial Decomposition
and Underly Mechanisms
Given that the cathode in ASSLSBs consists of sulfur active
materials, SSEs (e.g., Li6PS5Cl (LPSC)), and conductive carbon
additives (e.g., Ketjen black (KB)), severe SSEs degradation
induced by carbonaceous components usually occurs at the
electrolyte/cathode interface. To specifically understand the
effect of AXT addition on the interfacial chemistry, different
amounts of AXT were introduced into the cathode composites
and thoroughly mixed via high-energy ball milling, followed
by symmetric battery tests. In these experiments, symmetric
batteries containing 10 mg of cathode composite powders, namely
KB/S|LPSC|KB/S and KB/S-x%AXT|LPSC|KB/S-x%AXT, were
assembled and tested for four cycles within the voltage range from
–0.7 to 0.7 V [26]. As shown in Figure 1a, the KB/S|LPSC|KB/S
battery delivers an initial discharge capacity of 66.04 mAh
g−1 and charge capacity of 129.16 mAh g−1. In comparison,
the KB/S-0.5%AXT|LPSC|KB/S-0.5%AXT battery shows higher
initial discharge/charge capacities of 75.48 and 145.75 mAh g−1,
respectively, while maintaining stable discharge/charge perfor-
mance in the subsequent three cycles. Such enhanced capacities
upon 0.5% AXT-loading are likely associated with promoted SSE
decomposition, which generates additional electrochemically
active sulfur/Li2S species. With further increment of AXT addi-
tive, however, the capacities of the symmetric battery gradually
decrease. When the additive of AXT reaches up to 5%, the
symmetric battery exhibits negligible discharge/charge capacities
(7.08 and 12.23 mAh g−1, respectively), which may be attributed
to the reduced formation of sulfur/Li2S active species and the
increased generation of electrochemically inactive side products
due to overly suppressed electrolyte decomposition, as further
evidenced below. These results suggest that a moderate amount
of AXT can effectively regulate the electrolyte decomposition
pathway at the cathode interface, thereby balancing the formation
of active species and side products and ultimately improving cell
capacity.
To further verify the AXT-mediated regulation of electrolyte
decomposition, cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements were
performed on cells with LPSC/KB-AXT cathodes, as shown in
Figures S1 and S2. Notably, AXT itself exhibits no discernible
redox peaks within the operating voltage range of 1.5–3 V, whereas
LPSC typically undergoes reductive decomposition below 1.7 V

Theoretically, DFT calculations (Figures S10–S14) further elu-
cidate the coverage-dependent role of AXT in regulating the
decomposition pathway of the solid-state electrolyte. As AXT
coverage increases, the adsorption energy between SSEs and
AXT decreases from –2.97 to –6.25 eV (Figure S11). Notably, the
stabilization gain per AXT molecule increases in a sublinear man-
ner, suggesting the emergence of significant intermolecular π-π
interactions among adjacent AXT molecules at higher coverage.
Such intermolecular π-π stacking leads to a more compact and
spatially uniform Li–Cl coordination environment, narrowing
the Li–Cl distance range (2.40–2.52 Å, Figure S12). This structural
arrangement not only spatially constrains sulfur migration but
also electronically pre-stabilizes the Li–Cl-associated transition
state, thereby thermodynamically and kinetically favoring LiCl
formation. This interpretation is further supported by the Bader
charge analysis in Table S1, which shows that negative charges
is preferentially localized on Cl (−0.07 e), while the polarization
of S is weakened, consistent with the stabilization of the Li–
Cl reaction pathway. Under high AXT coverage, cooperative
polarization arising from multiple carbonyl and hydroxyl groups
generates a collective interfacial polarization, which drives Li+
toward Cl−and establishes a continuous electron-density channel
along the Li–Cl axis. At low AXT coverage, the Li–Cl coordination
shell in LPSC remains relatively loose and heterogeneous, with
Li–Cl distances distribution over a wider range (2.37–3.08 Å,
Figure S12). In this case, localized electron pockets are formed,
which hinders charge delocalization and delays Li–Cl bond
formation (Figure 2a). Meanwhile, Li+ is preferentially attracted
toward O/S sites of AXT and deviates from the original Li–S
coordination in LPSC, thereby polarizing and weakening the P–
S bond, which can promote the formation of active Li2S species.
This trend is also consistent with the Bader charge analysis
(see Table S1), where Li becomes more positive (+0.21∼+ 0.27
e), P undergoes electron depletion (–0.35 e) and S in LPSC
becomes more negative (–0.11 e), all of which indicate the
activation of P─S bond cleavage. Bond order analysis (Figure 2b)
and energy barrier (Figure 2c) calculations further confirm this
AXT-coverage-dependent evolution of electrolyte decomposition
products. At low AXT coverage, the P─S bond in LPSC is sharply
weakened in the transition state and nearly vanishes in the final
state, whereas significant Li─Cl bond strengthening occurs only
in the final state, consistent with a lower energy barrier for
Li2S formation. In contrast, Li─Cl bond at high AXT coverage
is already substantially developed in the transition state and
becomes dominant in the final state, while P─S bond retains
partial strength throughout the reaction process, leading to a
substantially lower energy barrier for LiCl formation. These
theoretical results collectively demonstrate that AXT coverage
determines the decomposition selectivity of LPSC: low AXT
coverage promotes Li2S generation, whereas high AXT coverage
shifts the decomposition pathway toward LiCl formation.
Based on the above investigation, it can be concluded that, at
a low doping concentration level of AXT, no appreciable P─O
bonding is formed to suppress the electrolyte decomposition
in the cathodes. Instead, small amount of AXT, owing to its
strong reducibility, can accelerate electrolyte decomposition and
redirect the decomposition pathways via an electron pocket effect.
In this scenario, electrons are locally trapped in the “pocket”
region around AXT, which suppresses electron transfer toward
the Li–Cl pathway while promoting preferential interaction with
O-containing sites, thereby polarizing and weakening the P─S
bonds. As a result, electrolyte decomposition is steered toward
the formation of more electrochemically active species (e.g., Li2S)
rather than electrochemically inert side products (e.g., LiCl),
leading to enhanced battery capacity. When the amount of insu-
lating AXT exceeds a certain threshold, however, a non-negligible
P─O coordination network is established within the cathode,
which suppresses electrolyte decomposition into active species
and instead favors the formation of more insulating LiCl side
products. Under this condition, intermolecular π-π stacking and
a continuous electron-density channel emerge within AXT-rich

2.2
Catalytic Ability of AXT Toward Sulfur Redox
Kinetics
To evaluate the effect of AXT on the sulfur redox kinetics at
the cathodes, scan rate-dependent CVs were conducted on fresh
cells. As illustrated in Figure S15, all cells exhibit a well-defined
reduction peak at ∼1.35 V and a distinct oxidation peak at ∼1.75 V,
indicating effective solid–solid conversion reactions between S8
molecules and Li2S at the cathodes. With respect to pristine
cathode, the 0.5%AXT-modified cathode delivers higher currents
together with earlier redox potentials, suggesting faster sulfur
redox kinetics. Further, the scan rate-dependent CV results were
plotted as contour maps in Figure 3a for a clear redox overpoten-
tial (Δη). Among all the cathodes, KB/S-0.5%AXT cathode gains
the lowest Δη of 784 mV. Both CV curves and capacity/differential
voltage (dQ/dV) curves (Figure S16) consistently indicate reduced
polarization, improved sulfur utilization and accelerated sulfur
redox kinetics upon the introduction of small amount of AXT
addition. The fast reaction dynamics and diminished polarization
at the 0.5%AXT-based cathode were further validated via galvano-
static intermittent titration technique (GITT) in Figure S17, in
which the open-circuit voltage (OCV) at the end of discharge is
1.78 V for KB/S-0.5%AXT cathodes, lower than that of pristine
KB/S cathodes (1.89 V) [30].
Considering that AXT itself is electrochemically inert and
exhibits poor electrical and ionic conductivity, systematic ex
situ and in situ electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS),
together with Li+ diffusion analysis, were conducted to clar-
ify whether the observed kinetic enhancement originates from
changes in electronic/ionic transport or from a genuine catalytic
effect on the sulfur reduction reaction. Figure S18 shows the
Nyquist plots of fresh ASSLSBs, in which the semicircular regions
sometimes become coupled due to specific electrochemical pro-
cesses in all-solid-state battery systems. Therefore, to resolve the
impedance contributions over different time scales and correlate
them with specific electrochemical processes, the ex situ EIS
results were analyzed using the distribution of relaxation times
(DRT) approach [31]. As shown in Figure S19, the peaks at 0.1–1
and 1–10 s can be assigned to the charge-transfer impedance at the
cathode/electrolyte interface (Rct) and the cathodic ion diffusion
impedance (W), respectively. Obviously, the presence of large
amounts of inert substances in the KB/S-2.5%AXT and KB/S-
5%AXT cathode significantly increases both cathodic ion diffu-
sion impedance and the cathode/electrolyte interface impedance,
thereby hindering charge/ion transport and deteriorating SRR
kinetics [12, 32–34]. In contrast, for 0.5%AXT-modified ASSLSB,
the incorporation of inert additives at trace levels induces only
negligible increases in both interfacial impedance and cathodic
ionic diffusion impedance. This result indicates that 0.5% AXT
has a negligible influence on the intrinsic electronic and ionic
conductivity of the system under initial states. Therefore, the

to an adjacent hollow through a bridge site. In the case of Gh-
AXT, however, Li+ migrates along a polarized channel defined by
O─containing sites of AXT (from ─OH toward C═O). As shown
in Figure 3d, the minimum migration energy barrier for Li+
diffusion on Gh-AXT is only 0.17 eV, which is 0.11 eV lower than
that on pristine Gh (0.28 eV). This barrier reduction corresponds
to an approximately 70-fold increase in the theoretical migration
rate at 300 K, indicating substantially facilitated Li+ transport on
the AXT-modified interface.
From the perspective of electronic structures, the partial density
of states (PDOS) in Figure S23 reveal an enhanced orbital
hybridization between O-2p states of AXT and Li-2s/S-3p states
of the anchored Li2Sx (x = 1, 2) within the range from −4 to
0 eV. This hybridization indicates that strong electron coupling
between AXT oxygen atoms and interfacial Li centers, which
induces charge delocalization from Li─S to Li─O bonds. Such
redistribution weakens the Li–S covalency and thereby facilitates
the subsequent conversion of Li2S2 into Li2S. Consistently, the
differential charge density maps in Figure S24 further reveal
directional Li─O charge accumulation and interfacial charge
transfer between Li2Sx (x = 1, 2) species and the carbonyl/hydroxyl
O atoms of AXT on the Gh-AXT interface, whereas pristine Gh
mainly exhibits image-charge polarization. Electrostatic potential
(ESP) maps in Figure 3e reveal continuous regions of negative
electrostatic potential (“negative potential pockets”) and low-
potential corridors surrounding the C═O and ─OH functional
groups of Gh-AXT system, which provides stronger adsorption
for the Li2S2 intermediate, thereby effectively accelerating the
reaction process. Collectively, these thermodynamic, kinetic and
electronic structure results demonstrate that AXT constructs a
low-energy interfacial reaction pathway and a favourable Li+
migration channel, while simultaneously stabilizing the Li2S2
intermediate via polar Li–O coordination, thus facilitating the
SRR kinetics and Li+ transport at solid–solid interfaces.
2.3
In Situ Probing Mechanical Behaviors of
AXT-Optimized ASSLSBs
As is well recognized, the large volume expansion of sulfur
cathodes has been the major constraint on the performance of
Li–S batteries in the past decades [37]. This issue is even more
critical in ASSLSBs, where repeated expansion and contraction of
sulfur during lithiation/delithiation can readily induce interfacial
detachment between the electrode and the SSE, as well as
the formation of voids and cracks. At high sulfur loadings,
the resulting stack-pressure fluctuations become even more
pronounced, which can severely deteriorate solid–solid interface
contact and compromise battery performance. Therefore, it is
highly essential to directly monitor the real-time stack-pressure
evolution during battery cycling. In our experiments, an operando
electrochemical-pressure device was utilized to track the stack-
pressure evolution during electrochemical process of ASSLSBs,
as illustrated in Figure 4a. Briefly, a force sensor coupled with
a signal conditioner was mounted on top of the stainless-steel
casing of the ASSLSB. During the charging/discharging process,
the force sensor converts mechanical changes within the cell
into electrical signals, thereby enabling real-time monitoring of
pressure evolution inside the battery.
Figure 4b,c shows the time-dependent potential-pressure profiles
for KB/S-0.5%AXT|LPSC|Li and KB/S|LPSC|Li batteries with a
high sulfur loading of 3.6 mg cm−2, cycled galvanostatically at
0.05 C. For both cells, the overall stack pressure decreases during
discharge and increases during charge, as essentially determined
by the (de)lithiation of Li anode and the corresponding volume
expansion/shrinkage of sulfur cathode. Notably, because KB/S-
0.5%AXT cathode exhibits higher sulfur conversion efficiency
(see Figure 3), the corresponding ASSLSB shows a pressure
decrease of 0.739 MPa during the first discharge, followed by
a pressure increase of 0.749 MPa during charge, resulting in
an exceptionally small net pressure variation of only 0.01 MPa
over one full cycle. On the contrary, the stack pressure of
KB/S|LPSC|Li battery decreases by 0.306 MPa in first cycle (0.574–
0.268 MPa). After 5 cycles, the pressure fluctuations in the pristine
KB/S|LPSC|Li battery become much larger than those of KB/S-
0.5%AXT|LPSC|Li battery. Such pronounced and accumulated
pressure fluctuations are unfavorable for maintaining intimate
solid–solid interfacial contact and significantly increase the risk
of electrochemo-mechanical failure, thereby undermining the
long-term stability of the battery [20].
To exclude the pressure contribution of Li anode, which pos-
sesses a much larger partial molar volume and can dominate
the overall pressure evolution in ASSLSBs, and thereby more
accurately resolve the mechanical behavior of the KB/S-0.5%AXT
cathode, Li4Ti5O12 (LTO) is paired with Li metal and cycled
within 1.5−2.5 V (vs. Li/Li+), taking advantage of the well-known
zero-strain property of LTO [10]. Figure 4d displays the time-
dependent potential-pressure profiles of the LTO|LPSC|Li battery.
Because the current densities during the charge/discharge pro-
cesses were kept constant at 0.3 mA cm−2, the pressure evolution
associated with Li plating/stripping exhibits a linear relation-
ship between pressure and capacity (Figure 4d). Based on this
calibration, the pressure variation rate of the Li metal anode
during lithiation/delithiation was fitted to be 0.262 MPa h−1. By
subtracting the Li-anode contribution from the original pressure
curves in Figure 4b,c, the net pressure evolution can be obtained
in Figure 4e. After this correction, the increase in stack pressure
during the discharge can be assigned to the volume expansion
associated with sulfur conversion into Li2S, where the drop-in
stack pressure during charge represents a reversible conversion
process from Li2S to sulfur along with volume shrinkage [9, 11].
Comparison of the corrected pressure profiles clearly shows that

Modified With AXT
Using Li metal as anode and LPSC as SE, the electrochemical
performances of KB/S-x%AXT (x = 0, 0.5, 2.5, and 5) cathodes in
ASSLSBs were assessed within the voltage window of 1.5–3.0 V

of the four cathodes at current densities ranging from 0.1 to

much lower capacities (<834.9 mAh g−1). This remarkable rate

and robust interface stability of the KB/S-0.5%AXT cathode.

3
Conclusion
In summary, we developed an unprecedented AXT/C/S cathode
composite through a simple high-energy ball-milling strategy to
enhance the interfacial stability of sulfur cathode in ASSLSBs.
Our results demonstrate that AXT, featuring continuous low-
potential corridor, plays a dual interfacial regulatory role. On the
one hand, it promotes Li+ transport and stabilizes Li2S2 inter-
mediates through polar Li–O coordination, thereby accelerating
sulfur redox kinetics. On the other hand, it redirects electrolyte
decomposition toward the generation of more electrochemically
active species through an electron pocket effect, leading to
improved capacity and the construction of a stable triple-phase
interface among sulfur, carbon and solid electrolyte. Moreover, in
situ stack pressure measurements and molecular dynamics sim-
ulations reveal that AXT effectively suppresses the large volume
variation of sulfur during cycling through a flexible molecular-
scaffold effect, eliminating electrochemical-mechanical failure.
As a result, AXT-regulated ASSLSBs deliver ultrahigh sulfur
utilization (>100% at 0.2 C), excellent rate capability (2 C) and
remarkable long-cycling stability with a low decay rate of 0.060%
per cycle over 300 cycles. Even at a high sulfur mass loading of
9.49 mg cm−2 and over a wide temperature range, such AXT-
containing cells still exhibit an impressive areal capacity of 16.56
mAh cm−2 with the Coulombic efficiency of 99.68% at 0.1 C,
representing a performance level rarely achieved in previously
reported ASSLSBs. Overall, this work enables fast and stable
solid-state sulfur conversion chemistry through a biomimetic
cathode design and provides a new strategy for constructing
high-performance ASSLSBs.
