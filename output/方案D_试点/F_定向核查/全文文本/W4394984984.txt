A B S T R A C T  
All-solid-state lithium-sulfur battery (ASLSB) is deemed a promising next-generation energy storage device owing to its combination of high theoretical specific 
energy (2600 Wh kg−1) derived from the sulfur active material, and exceptional safety characteristics and the ability to suppress the polysulfide shuttle effect through 
the use of solid electrolyte (SE). However, the low electronic and ionic conductivity of sulfur, coupled with the poor solid–solid interfacial contact inherent in all- 
solid-state configuration, has resulted in poor electrochemical performance. Herein, NiO electrocatalyst supported on N-doped sites of super-p was synthesized and 
incorporated as a carbon additive in the composite cathode of the ASLSB. The direct growth of nanosized NiO electrocatalyst on the N-doped sites of super-p ensures 
facile electron transfer to the electrocatalyst surface, while maximizing the electrocatalyst’s surface area. As a result, the overpotential during charging and dis­
charging of the ASLSB was significantly reduced, as assessed through galvanostatic intermittent titration technique and cyclic voltammetry, indicating enhanced 
redox conversion of sulfur. Furthermore, the Li6PS5Cl sulfide SE induces solid–solid conversion of sulfur, eliminating the dissolution issue of polysulfide intermediates 
and ensuring good cycle performance. The assembled ASLSB exhibits a high reversible capacity of 1845 mAh g−1 at 0.05C at room temperature and enhanced rate 
capability at high C-rates, operated at 60 ◦C. Even at a substantially high sulfur loading level of 6.65 mg cm−2, a maximum areal capacity of 9.48 mAh cm−2 was 
achieved at room temperature, highlighting the important role of electrocatalyst in realizing high performance ASLSBs.   
1. Introduction 
Lithium-sulfur batteries (LSBs) have been extensively studied as one 
of the most promising next-generation energy storage systems for a wide 
range of applications that necessitate lightweight power sources, such as 
portable electronics and unmanned aerial vehicles [1–3]. LSBs offer a 
high theoretical energy density of 2600 Wh kg−1 which is five time 
higher than that of conventional lithium-ion batteries (~500 Wh kg−1) 
and benefit from low battery production cost arising from the natural 
abundance of sulfur [4,5]. Despite their potential advantages, however, 
the commercialization of LSBs is hindered by the rapid capacity decay 
and poor Coulombic efficiency caused by polysulfide dissolution phe­
nomenon in liquid phase ether-based electrolytes, and the safety con­
cerns derived from high reactivity of Li metal [6–10]. 
The implementation of sulfide solid electrolyte (SE) holds great 
promise in addressing these challenges associated with liquid phase 
electrolyte-based LSBs. Sulfide SEs induces a solid–solid conversion re­
action from sulfur to lithium sulfide (Li2S), effectively mitigating the 
concerns related to polysulfide dissolution [11–13]. Furthermore, SEs 
contribute to enhanced safety in Li metal batteries due to their inherent 
nonflammability and nonleakage characteristics [14–16]. Nevertheless, 
employing SEs in all-solid-state LSBs (ASLSBs) presents a significant 
challenge due to the high contact resistance arising from the solid–solid 
interfacial contact between the active material and the electrolyte in 
composite electrodes [17–19]. This issue is more severe in ASLSBs than 
in other all-solid-state batteries (ASBs) due to the low electronic and 
ionic conductivity of sulfur, which necessitates the formation of both 
electronic and ionic conduction pathways [20]. More importantly, the 
inhibition of polysulfide formation results in significantly lowered 
mobility of active materials in ASLSBs, which results in kinetically 
limited sulfur conversion reaction [5,13,21]. These challenging issues 
lead to high polarization, poor sulfur utilization, and poor rate perfor­
mance in ASLSBs. Therefore, developing strategies to accelerate the 
kinetics of sulfur conversion reaction is crucial to approach high per­
formance ASLSBs.

In this work, we investigated the performance of nickel oxide (NiO) 
nanoparticle electrocatalyst supported on N-doped carbon host on the 
reaction kinetics of ASLSBs. The uniform integration and robust 
adsorption of nanosized NiO particles on N-doped sites of super-p en­

electrocatalyst surface. Moreover, the even dispersion of NiO nano­

modest amount (~7 wt%) of NiO incorporated in the composite cath­
ode. The NiO electrocatalyst distinctly diminishes the overpotential of 
the composite cathode, as demonstrated by various electrochemical 
assessments. Consequently, the final ASLSB incorporating the NiO 
electrocatalyst exhibits a remarkably high reversible capacity, even at a 
notably high loading level, operated at room temperature. 
2. Experimental Section 
2.1. Synthesis of NiO@NSP 
The NiO@NSP was prepared through soft nitriding of super-p (NSP) 
followed by the incorporation of NiO nanoparticles. 1.2 g of super-p was 
physically mixed with 1.8 g of urea by mortar grinding. The resulting 
mixture was heat-treated at 150 ℃ for 2 h, followed by second heat 
treatment at 300 ℃ for 2 h in a box furnace (heating rate: 5 ℃ min−1). 
The heat-treated powder was then dispersed in a solvent mixture con­
taining 200 mL of ethanol and 200 mL of deionized water to completely 
remove any unreacted urea residual. After stirring for 2 h, the product 
was collected by filtration and dried. Then, 100 mg of as-prepared sur­
face-modified super-p was homogeneously dispersed in 80 mL of 
dimethylformamide (DMF) by sonification and a 0.2 M of nickel acetate 
tetrahydrate solution was prepared separately. 5 mL of nickel acetate 
tetrahydrate solution was mixed with the prepared super-p solution. The 
resulting mixture was stirred at 90 ℃ for 4 h. After cooling to room 
temperature, the powder was collected by centrifugation with subse­
quent washing with deionized water, followed by drying in an oven. The 
final NiO@NSP product was obtained by heat treatment at 450 ℃ for 4 h 
under an inert Ar atmosphere (heating rate: 2 ℃ min−1). 
2.2. Preparation of composite cathodes 
The composite cathodes were prepared by ball milling sulfur 
(99.998 %, Sigma-Aldrich), super-p (or NiO@NSP), and Li6PS5Cl (LPSCl; 
Jeong Kwan Display Corp.) with a mass ratio of 1:1:2, respectively. The 
raw materials were enclosed in an air-tight zirconia pot in an argon- 
filled glovebox (H2O < 1 ppm) before milling. Ball milling was per­
formed using planetary micro mill (Pulverisette 7, Fritsch) at a rotating 
speed of 500 rpm for 10 h. The ball-milled powder was sieved with a 
mesh size of 32 μm before further use. 
2.3. ASLSB cell assembly 
Using a pressure cell mold, 150 mg of LPSCl powder was cold pressed 
using a hydraulic uniaxial press at 440 Mpa. On one side of the cold 
pressed LPSCl pellet, 12 mg of composite electrode powder 
(corresponding to areal sulfur loading of 2.26 mg/cm2) was spread and 
cold pressed at 440 Mpa. On the other side of the LPSCl pellet, Li foil 
(300 μm, Honjo Metal Co.) with a diameter of 12 mm was attached. The 
entire cell was subjected to constant stack pressure of 45 Mpa using a 
pressure jig throughout the electrochemical measurements. All cells 
were assembled in an argon-filled glovebox. 
2.4. Material characterization 
A transmission electron microscopy (TEM; G2 F30 S-Twin, Tencai) 
and a scanning electron microscopy (SEM; S-4800 field emission, Hita­
chi) analyses were used to characterize the size and morphology of the 
as-prepared catalyst composite materials. High annular dark-field 
scanning TEM (STEM) and high-resolution TEM (HR-TEM; Titan 
cubed G2 60–300) analyses with energy dispersive spectrometry (EDS) 
mapping were conducted for the in-depth characterization of as- 
synthesized catalyst materials. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were 
collected using a RIGAKU D/MAX-2500 V X-ray diffractometer (Cu Kα 
radiation, λ = 1.541 Å). Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) results were 
obtained under O2 atmosphere with a scan rate of 10 ◦C min−1. 
2.5. Electrochemical measurements 
Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was measured with a 
potentiostat (SP-150e, BioLogic) with an amplitude of 10 mV and fre­
quency range of 1 MHz to 50 mHz at room temperature. Galvanostatic 
cycling tests were conducted using a cycle tester (Toyo System) in a 
27 ◦C or 60 ◦C chamber in the potential range of 1.5 ~ 3.0 V. Constant 
current (CC) mode was used for both charge and discharge. Galvano­
static intermittent titration technique (GITT) was performed with cur­
rent pulses corresponding to 0.05C for 30 min, followed by 4 h resting. 
Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was measured between 1.5 to 3.0 V at a scan 
rate of 0.03 mV s−1 in a 27 ◦C or 60 ◦C chamber. 
2.6. Density function theory (DFT) calculation 
Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were conducted with 
the semi-local PBE (Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof) [30] functional as 
implemented in the Vienna ab Initio Simulation Package (VASP) [31]. 
Spin-polarized calculations were also performed. By using a plane-wave 
basis with a cutoff energy of 400 eV, the electrons of Ni(4 s,3d) was 
treated as valence states with the Projector Augmented Wave method 
(PAW) [32,33]. For Co, a value of U = 3.4 eV was used [34]. A vacuum 
gap was set to be 15 Å, and the Brillouin zone was sampled with Gama- 
centered 1x1x1 k-point meshes for surface calculations. All atoms were 
relaxed with the implemented conjugate-gradient algorithm until the 
convergence tolerance of Hellmann-Feynman forces and energy on each 
atom was less than 0.05 eV/Å and 10-4 eV/atom, respectively. Also, the 
van der Waals (vdW) interactions were described by the Grimme’s DFT- 
D3 scheme with the application of dispersion correction [35,36]. 
3. Results and discussion 
When using SEs for sulfur-based cathodes, physical properties of the 
electrocatalyst should be carefully considered to improve the overall 
reaction kinetics of sulfur conversion. Firstly, the use of electrocatalysts 
with small particle size and high surface-to-volume ratio is critical, as it 
maximizes the contact area between active sulfur species and the elec­
trocatalysts, thereby efficiently promoting the solid–solid phase con­
version of sulfur in ASLSBs. Secondly, the direct growth of 
electrocatalysts on electrically conductive agents is important in facili­
tating electron transfer to the electrocatalyst’s surface while minimizing 
the overall contact resistance of the composite cathode in ASLSBs. 
In this regard, we synthesized NiO nanoparticles directly grown on 
the surface of N-doped super-p (NSP) conductive carbon, hereafter 
referred to as NiO@NSP (Fig. 1). To achieve uniform incorporation of

between NiO nanoparticles and electrically conductive NSP is crucial for 
maximizing Li-S electrocatalysis in the cathode. Nanoparticles possess a 
higher surface-to-volume ratio than larger particles, providing 
numerous electrocatalytically active surfaces of NiO for the sulfur con­
version reaction. Furthermore, the direct growth of NiO on NSP facili­
tates electron transfer to NiO through conductive NSP, and this electron 
pathway is well-maintained during the cycling due to their intimate 
contacts. Conversely, physical mixing of NiO particles with NSP for 
cathode composite design can lead to poor electron transfer to NiO, and 
the large volume change of sulfur during charge–discharge can result in

These series of analyses confirm that NiO nanoparticles were uniformly 
distributed on the surface of NSP without significant agglomeration. We 
believe that NiO@NSP holds great potential for commercialization for 
the following reasons: i) its synthetic process is straightforward, 
involving simple solution mixing followed by calcination, which aligns 
closely with the general synthetic route used to fabricate commercial 
battery electrodes, thereby facilitating large-scale production; ii) SP, 
which was used as the carbon substrate, is already commercially 
employed in the battery industry, indicating an existing and established 
supply chain. 
The synthesized NiO@NSP was incorporated into the composite 
cathode by ball milling sulfur, NiO@NSP, and Li6PS5Cl (LPSCl) with a 
mass ratio of 1:1:2, respectively. The obtained well-mixed composite 
cathode after ball milling is presented in Fig. 3a. The LPSCl SE was 
chosen for this study due to its high ionic conductivity of 3.1 mS cm−1, as 
measured at 27 ◦C, and low activation energy of 0.18 eV, as calculated 
from the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) data in Fig. S4. 
To distinguish the C signal of super-p from the background, EDS 
measurement was conducted using Si wafer as the substrate. The EDS 
mapping images of a composite particle display uniform dispersion of P, 
S, and Cl signals originating from the SE composition and C signal 
resulting from the super-p composition. Given the poor electronic and 
ionic conductivity of sulfur, the uniform dispersion of both electronic 
and ionic conductive materials is of utmost importance for ASLSBs. As 
evidenced by the EDS mapping images, the well-dispersed composite 
cathode indicates well-established electronic and ionic pathways and 
intimate contact between different components of the cathode, which 
will lead to high sulfur utilization during charge–discharge. The rela­
tively small amount of electrocatalyst remaining in the composite 
cathode led to the absence of Ni signal at this stage. The constant sulfur 
content of ~25 % in the final composite was further confirmed by TGA 
in Fig. 3b. The XRD pattern of the composite cathode revealed crystal 
peaks corresponding to LPSCl SE and sulfur active material (Fig. S5). 
Despite the evident presence of NiO crystal peaks in NiO@NSP (Fig. 2a), 
NiO crystal peaks were not clearly detected in the XRD pattern of the 
composite cathode. This can be attributed to two factors: i) the total 
content of NiO in the composite cathode is considerably lower than that 
of other cathode components (i.e., LPSCl and sulfur), and ii) the crystal 
peaks of NiO nanoparticles are broader compared to those of highly 
crystalline LPSCl and sulfur. 
To investigate the electrochemical performances of ASLSB cells 
employing NiO electrocatalyst, cells were fabricated with a composite 
cathode featuring a sulfur loading level of 2.26 mg cm−2 and a Li anode 
with a thickness of 300 μm. To provide a practical evaluation of our 
system, all electrochemical evaluations were conducted at 27 ◦C except 
for high rate test performed at 60 ◦C. Hereafter, cells assembled with 
NiO@NSP will be referred to as “NiO@NSP cells,” while reference cells 
assembled with pristine SP will be termed “SP cells.” SP cells were 
assembled with an equivalent sulfur loading level of 2.26 mg cm−2 and 
equivalent composition (i.e., S/SP/LPSCl = 1/1/2, w/w/w). 
Prior to the charge–discharge test, electrochemical impedance (EIS) 
analysis was performed at open-circuit voltage to assess the interfacial 
resistance of the NiO@NSP and SP cells. The Nyquist plots of both 
composite cathodes in Fig. 4a showed negligible difference, suggesting 
that the interfacial stability between the NiO@NSP electrocatalyst and 
other composite cathode components (i.e., sulfur and LPSCl) is

For the charge–discharge tests, all cells were initially charged- 
discharged at 0.05C for five cycles to activate the cells, followed by 
cycling at higher C-rates. As illustrated in the voltage profiles of both 
cells in Fig. S6, all profiles display a single plateau, signifying the 
absence of liquid-phase polysulfide formation and solid–solid conver­
sion of S to Li2S in our all-solid-state system. As shown in Fig. 4b, the NiO 
cell consistently displays higher capacities at all C-rates compared to the 
SP cell. This enhancement is attributed to the improved sulfur conver­
sion reaction facilitated by the incorporation of the NiO electrocatalyst, 
leading to higher sulfur utilization. The fifth discharge capacities at C- 
rates of 0.05C, 0.1C, 0.2C, and 0.3C for the SP cell are 1601, 1502, 1329, 
and 1175 mAh g−1, respectively. On the other hand, the fifth discharge 
capacities at equivalent C-rates for the NiO@NSP cell are 1845, 1731, 
1547, and 1386 mAh g−1, respectively. The reversible capacity sur­
passing the theoretical capacity (1673 mAh g−1) of sulfur is attributed to 
the partial capacity contribution of LPSCl due to its reversible electro­
chemical reaction, as reported in the literature [41,42]. Arising from the 
high sulfur utilization, however, tradeoff between capacity and cycle 
stability was witnessed. As shown in Fig. 4b, NiO@NSP cell showed 
slightly faster capacity decay than SP cell at 0.1C. We can infer that the 
increased sulfur utilization ratio attained with the electrocatalytic effect 
of NiO@NSP electrocatalysts results in more substantial volume 
expansion during the lithiation of sulfur in the NiO@NSP cell, leading to 
more severe mechanically induced contact loss between the active ma­
terial and the conductive matrix, thereby accelerating the rate of ca­
pacity decay [43]. Nevertheless, the NiO@NSP cell still exhibited 
acceptable capacity retention, which will be discussed in detail later. 
Due to the high sulfur loading in our cell configuration, char­
ge–discharge performance at C-rates higher than 0.3C could not be 
tested at 27 ◦C. This limitation is attributed to the critical current density 
(CCD) of the LPSCl SE at the Li anode interface, as reported in the 
literature [44–46]. To further investigate the advantageous effect of NiO 
electrocatalyst within the sulfur composite, additional electrochemical 
assessments were conducted at an elevated temperature of 60 ◦C. 
The discharge capacities and corresponding coulombic efficiencies of 
the SP and NiO@NSP cells at various C-rates, tested at 60 ◦C, are shown 
in Fig. 4c. The elevated temperature of 60 ◦C allows for higher ionic 
conductivity of 6.9 mS cm−1, permitting testing at C-rates as high as 1C 
without compromising the CCD at the anode side. Unlike the trend 
observed at 27 ◦C, at which both cells displayed different capacities at 
the fifth cycle at 0.05C, both of the SP cell and NiO@NSP cell exhibited 
similar capacities. The SP cell exhibited a discharge capacity of 1744 
mAh g−1, while the NiO@NSP cell exhibited 1778 mAh g−1. We hy­
pothesize that at the lower temperature of 27 ◦C, the limited ionic 
conductivity of the SE becomes the primary limiting factor, and as a 
result, the NiO electrocatalyst effectively enhances the rate performance 
even at a low current of 0.05C. However, at 60 ◦C, the ionic conductivity 
of the SE is significantly enhanced, rendering the electrocatalytic effect 
of the NiO electrocatalyst negligible during cycling at a low current of 
0.05C. Nonetheless, the catalytic effect becomes evident at higher C- 
rates at 60 ◦C. The fifth discharge capacities at various C-rates of 0.1C, 
0.3C, 0.5C, and 1C for the SP cell are 1676, 1538, 1380, and 1001 mAh 
g−1, respectively. The corresponding capacity retentions in relation to 
the discharge capacity at 0.05C are 96.1, 88.2, 79.1, and 57.4 %. On the 
other hand, the fifth discharge capacities at the same C-rates for the 
NiO@NSP cell are 1735, 1667, 1536, and 1237 mAh g−1, exhibiting 
capacity retention of 97.5, 93.7, 86.4, and 69.6 %, respectively. Fig. 4d 
provides a comparative assessment of the capacity retention of both 
cathodes at higher C-rates. The enhanced rate capability resulting from 
the utilization of the NiO electrocatalyst is also evident in the voltage 
profiles presented in Fig. 4e and f. Notably, the overpotential between 
the charge–discharge voltage profiles of the NiO@NSP cell is reduced 
compared to that of the SP cell, resulting in higher capacities. 
To comprehensively assess the influence of NiO@NSP’s physical 
properties on the electrochemical performance of ASLSBs, we also 
fabricated a solid-state sulfur composite cathode by blending commer­

weight ratio as NiO@NSP. The ASLSB cell incorporating bulk NiO + SP

and 60 ◦C, respectively, at 0.05C (Fig. S7), which are considerably lower

NiO + SP cell experienced cell failure (i.e., zero capacity) at 0.1, 0.2, and

materials (without well-controlled nanostructures) into the solid-state

To verify the enhanced redox kinetics of the composite cathode 
incorporating NiO electrocatalyst, galvanostatic intermittent titration 
technique (GITT) was conducted on both cells at 27 ◦C. The GITT ex­
periments were initiated following the initial discharge process using 
freshly assembled cells. As illustrated in Fig. 5a and b, the overpotential 
observed during both the charge and discharge processes in the 
NiO@NSP cell exhibits a notable reduction when compared to the SP 
cell. For visual comparison, the overpotential was calculated as the 
difference between the voltage during galvanostatic loading and the 
quasi-open-circuit voltage (QOCV) after relaxation, as depicted in 
Fig. 5c and d. Both cells initially exhibit a slight decrease in over­
potential for both charge and discharge processes, most likely due to the 
cell activation process associated with the redistribution of components 
within the composite cathode, leading to lower charge transfer resis­
tance [47,48]. Upon subsequent reaction, the overpotential increases for 
both charge–discharge processes, attributed to complex factors 
including the increase in internal resistance arising from the loss of 
physical contact at the solid–solid interface upon further reaction and 
resulting volume change, and change in diffusivity of Li within sulfur 
particles upon (de)lithiation, as reported in the literature [49,50]. 
Nonetheless, for both charge and discharge processes, the overpotential 
of the SP cell increases at a relatively faster rate than that of the 
NiO@NSP cell. This behavior can be attributed to the accelerated redox 
reaction kinetics within the NiO@NSP cell, facilitated by the presence of 
the NiO nanoparticle electrocatalyst. 
The electrocatalytic effect of NiO was also evident in the cyclic 
voltammetry (CV) analysis performed at both 27 ◦C and 60 ◦C with a 
scan rate of 0.03 mV s−1, as depicted in Fig. 5e and f. The CV profiles of 
NiO@NSP and SP cells show a distinctive cathodic peak (corresponding 
to the reduction reaction of sulfur to Li2S) and anodic peak (corre­
sponding to the oxidation reaction of Li2S to sulfur). This behavior 
contrasts with that of liquid-phase electrolyte-based LSBs, which typi­
cally exhibit two cathodic and anodic peaks. This observation corrobo­
rates that formation of liquid-phase polysulfide intermediates is 
suppressed in ASLSBs due to solid–solid conversion reaction. It is note­
worthy that these two CV profiles exhibit a significant difference in 
polarization. In the case of SP cell at 27 ◦C, the cathodic and anodic 
peaks are positioned at 1.68 and 2.83 V, respectively, indicating a po­
larization of 1.15 V. Conversely, the CV profile of NiO@NSP cell at 
27 ◦C, show cathodic and anodic peaks at 1.68 and 2.68 V, respectively, 
resulting in a reduced polarization of 1.00 V. The polarization measured 
at 60 ◦C are reduced in both SP and NiO@NSP cells, relative to those 
measured at 27 ◦C, due to the enhanced ionic conductivity of SE at 
elevated temperature. Nonetheless, the polarization reduction from NiO 
electrocatalyst is more distinct at 60 ◦C. The calculated polarization of 
SP and NiO@NSP cell at 60 ◦C are 0.77 V and 0.53 V, respectively. The 
notable decrease in overall polarization and the downshift of the anodic 
potential observed in NiO@NSP cell corroborate that the NiO nano­
particle electrocatalyst homogeneously incorporated on the surface of 
NSP is favorable for accelerating the reaction kinetics of solid–solid 
conversion in ASLSBs.

observation was further confirmed by crystal orbital Hamilton popula­

Benefiting from the improved reaction kinetics catalyzed by NiO, the 
NSP@NiO cell, even with a notably high sulfur loading, demonstrated 
exceptional performance, as depicted in Fig. 7. Even at a substantial 
sulfur loading of 4.46 mg cm−2, the NiO@NSP cell exhibited a high 
initial discharge capacity of 6.95 mAh cm−2. This equates to 93 % of the 
theoretical capacity (7.48 mAh cm−2) at this loading level, signifying 
high sulfur utilization. The cell demonstrated reasonable cyclability 
concerning its high loading level of active material (4.46 mg cm−2), 
exhibiting 4.61 mA h cm−2 and 2.73 mA h cm−2 of areal capacity even 
after 100 and 200 cycles, respectively. As the sulfur loading was further 
increased to 6.65 mg cm−2, the NiO@NSP cell displayed an astounding

using state-of-the-art layered cathode materials (~4 mAh cm−2) and is 
among the highest reported values for ASLSBs reported to date (Table 1)

electronic and ionic conduction pathways and rapid redox kinetics 
within the NiO@NSP cell synthesized in this study, highlighting the 
substantial role of electrocatalyst in ASLSBs. However, the high utili­

cycle stability in high sulfur loading ASLSBs with NiO nanoparticle 
electrocatalyst. While the enhanced sulfur utilization facilitated by the 
NiO electrocatalyst results in superior areal capacity, it also induces a 
significant volume change of active species during cell cycling, which 
deteriorates the stability of solid–solid contact within the cathode.

To examine the development of interfacial impedance in cells after 
cycling, EIS was conducted before and after multiple charge–discharge 
cycles. Fig. S9a-d displays the Nyquist plots of the SP and NiO@NSP cells 
before and after the 5th and 16th cycles of charge–discharge at 27 ◦C and 
60 ◦C. All cells underwent an initial charge–discharge at 0.05C for five 
cycles, followed by cycling at 0.1C. The equivalent circuit model of the 
ASLSB cells is depicted in Fig. S9e. Constant phase elements (CPEs) were 
utilized to account for the non-ideal behavior of the electrodes, resulting 
from their rough and/or porous surfaces. Rb corresponds to the bulk 
resistance of the solid electrolyte and the current collector of the battery. 
The high-frequency semicircle, characterized by Ran and CPEan in par­
allel, corresponds to the resistance and capacitance related to the elec­
trolyte/anode interface [28,62]. The mid-frequency semicircle, 
characterized by Rct and CPEct in parallel, corresponds to the charge 
transfer resistance within the cathode and the capacitance formed at the 
cathode interface. Warburg impedance (W) corresponds to Li ion 
diffusion in the battery. The fitted results of the Nyquist plots using the 
equivalent circuit model are listed in Tables S2 and S3. 
As demonstrated by the Nyquist plots and the fitting results, the Rct of 
NiO@NSP cells is distinctively lower than that of SP cells at both 
operating temperatures. Interestingly, the Rct of the SP cell cycled at 
60 ◦C (996 Ω) was higher than that of the same cell cycled at 27 ◦C (94.3 
Ω). We attribute this behavior to the larger volume change and internal 
stress experienced by the cell due to higher sulfur utilization when 
operated at 60 ◦C. Although the same degradation mechanism is ex­
pected in the NiO@NSP cell, the Rct remains relatively lower in the 
NiO@NSP cell even at 60 ◦C (65.4 Ω) due to the enhanced reaction ki­
netics resulting from the introduction of electrocatalysts in the 
NiO@NSP cell. A similar improvement in the Rct upon incorporating 
NiO@NSP is observed in cells operated at 27 ◦C. The higher values of Ran 
and Rct after the 5th cycle compared to those after the 16th cycle for 
both cells operated at 27 ◦C may be attributed to improved interfacial 
contact after slower activation during cycling at 27 ◦C 
The evolution of the interface after cycling was further examined 
using SEM by visualizing the morphology of the cells before and after 
cycling. The cell used for this SEM analysis underwent 300 cycles of 
charge–discharge before disassembly. As shown in Fig. S10a, the as- 
assembled NiO@NSP cathode exhibits a rough surface morphology 
similar to that of the SE pellet layer, when viewed from the cross- 
sectional perspective. In contrast, after cycling, the composite cathode 
displays a very smooth and dense morphology in Fig. S10b, indicating 
improved homogeneity of the different components within the com­
posite. After multiple cycles of lithiation/delithiation, the sulfur parti­
cles are pulverized and more uniformly distributed, explaining the 
initial increase in discharge capacity and decrease in impedance during 
the initial activation cycles. From the top view, despite utilizing me­
chanically ductile sulfide SE and pressing the composite cathode at a 
high pressure of 440 Mpa, the existence of some voids within the com­
posite cathode pellet is unavoidable (Fig. S10c). However, after multiple 
cycling, the sulfur particles are pulverized from repeated lithiation/ 
delithiation, resulting in a seamless interface as shown in Fig. S10d. 
Moreover, even after 300 cycles of charge–discharge, no significant 
cracks were observed in the NiO@NSP cathode and uniform dispersion 
of different components within the composite cathode was maintained, 
as confirmed by the EDS mapping images in Fig. S10e. 
4. Conclusion 
In conclusion, an ASLSB with a remarkable areal capacity was suc­
cessfully demonstrated through the incorporation of NiO electrocatalyst 
into the sulfur composite cathode. From galvanostatic intermittent 
titration technique (GITT) analysis, it was evident that the NiO@NSP 
cell exhibited a reduction in overpotential, for both charge and 
discharge processes. This observation is a strong indicator of improved 
sulfur conversion kinetics which was further validated through CV 
analysis, as evident from earlier onset of redox peaks. The enhanced 
electrochemical performance of the NiO@NSP cell translated into su­
perior rate capability at elevated C-rates, when sufficient ionic con­
ductivity of SE was achieved. Notably, the NiO@NSP cell exhibited 
exceptional areal capacity, achieving an unprecedented 9.48 mAh cm−2 
after cell activation at a high sulfur loading of 6.65 mg cm−2. This 
achievement significantly surpasses the reported capacities for 
contemporary layered cathode materials, emphasizing the potential of 
this system for addressing the energy storage challenges of the future.
