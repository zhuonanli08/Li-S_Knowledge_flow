Towards high performance inorganic all-solid-state lithium-sulfur
batteries: strategies for enhancing reaction kinetics and solid-solid
contact
Zewei Huang1†, Liying Deng2†, Wangyang Li1, Jie Zhang1, Shuyu Liao1, Hong Zhang1* and
Xinghui Wang1,3*
ABSTRACT
Inorganic solid electrolyte-based all-solid-state
lithium-sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) have garnered significant
attention due to their inherent safety and higher energy den-
sity, making them a promising candidate for the upcoming
lithium batteries. However, employing sulfur as the active
material in all-solid-state composite cathodes introduces two
critical challenges: sluggish electrochemical reaction kinetics
and insufficient solid-solid contact between the sulfur, con-
ductive additive, and solid electrolyte phases. These issues
directly impact battery performance and hinder the commer-
cialization of ASSLSBs. In this comprehensive review, the
underlying causes of these issues are first discussed to gain a
fundamental understanding of potential improvement direc-
tions. Subsequently, we summarize the recent progress in
enhancing sulfur reaction kinetics and optimizing solid-solid
contact. The fundamental principles, fabrication techniques,
and resultant performance enhancement of diverse strategies
are systematically categorized, summarized, and evaluated.
Finally, the challenges and future outlook of advanced ASSLSB
cathode research are discussed at the end of this review.
Keywords: all-solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries, sulfur cath-
odes, cathode modifications, inorganic solid-state electrolytes
INTRODUCTION
Innovations in lithium-ion batteries (LIB) have revolutionized
the fields of electric vehicles and consumer electronics, with
insertion-type cathodes playing a dominant role in commercially
available LIBs [1–5]. However, the energy density of traditional
insertion-type cathodes coupled with graphite is gradually
approaching their theoretical limit (170 W h kg−1 for LiFePO4
and 300 W h kg−1 for ternary cathode), which is not sufficient to
meet market demands [6–8]. The two-electron redox reaction
between sulfur and lithium provides high theoretical capacity,
making lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries one of the most promising
candidates for upcoming energy storage devices [9–12]. Speci-
fically,
the
theoretical
capacity
of
the
lithium
anode
is
3860 mA h g−1, while that of the sulfur cathode is 1675 mA h g−1
[13,14]. Therefore, a maximum theoretical energy density of
2600 W h kg−1 is provided, and a fully packaged Li-S battery can
achieve a high energy density up to 600 W h kg−1 [15–17].
Nevertheless, in commonly used ether-based liquid-state elec-
trolytes (LEs), sulfur undergoes multistep electrochemical reac-
tions, accompanied by the notorious drawback of the shuttle
effect [18,19]. This phenomenon leads to a series of side reac-
tions and a significant loss of active materials. Furthermore, the
thermal instabilities and leakage-prone nature of LEs in the
system pose potential safety risks [20–22]. Although there have
been many attempts to suppress the shuttle effect by trapping
soluble lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) and to improve the safety of
LEs by adding flame retardants, fundamental solutions to the
aforementioned challenges have yet to be achieved [23–28].
The introduction of inorganic solid-state electrolytes (ISEs)
offers a promising solution to overcome the limitations. Cur-
rently, the ISEs utilized in all-solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries
(ASSLSBs) primarily include sulfide-based, oxide-based, and
halide-based electrolytes. Unlike gel electrolytes, some polymer
electrolytes, or electrolytes that require interfacial wetting, the
use of ISEs in place of separators and LEs typically results in the
discharge curve of Li-S batteries transitioning from a dual pla-
teau to a single plateau around 2.0 V (vs. Li/Li+) [29–34]. It is
attributed to the absence of highly soluble long-chain LiPSs
(Li2Sn, 4 ≤n ≤8), which prevents the shuttle effect [35–37].
Moreover, ISEs exhibit superior thermal stability compared to
LEs containing organic solvents, making them less susceptible to
volatilization and leakage [38]. When ISEs replace porous elec-
trode structures and separators, their relatively dense nature
potentially facilitates a higher energy density [39,40].
Despite considerable progress in the development of ASSLSBs,
there remain substantial challenges in the design and synthesis
of high performance cathode, ISE, and anode, as well as the
stabilization of their interfacial chemistry [41–43]. Particularly in
the cathodes, the poor conductivity of sulfur and its significant
volume change have become major concerns due to the absence
of wetting by LEs [44,45]. In the solid-state system, the insu-
lating properties of sulfur and its poor contact with ISEs are
unfavorable for rapid electron and ion transport, leading to

CHALLENGES OF ASSLSB CATHODES
Although
sulfur
boasts
a
high
theoretical
capacity
of
1675 mA h g−1, its intrinsic electrical conductivity (10−18 S cm−1)
is substantially lower than that of commonly utilized LiFePO4
(10−9 S cm−1) and ternary cathode (10−5 S cm−1). The markedly
low conductivity results in slow redox kinetics for sulfur mate-
rials [46]. In ASSLSBs, the ISEs are incapable of penetrating the
sulfur cathode as the LEs do, resulting in the active material
depth within the cathode being difficult to participate in the
conversion reaction due to its inherent insulating properties and
tortuous electron and ion pathways. Additionally, the complex
interface between the cathodes and ISE further exacerbates the
challenges in charge transport. As a result, the observed low
sulfur utilization hinders the development of high-loading, thick
cathodes [47–49]. Therefore, it is necessary to enhance the
transport capabilities of the solid-state sulfur cathode to meet the
requirements of fast reaction kinetics.
Aside from the adverse effects caused by poor conductivity,
the side reactions of ISEs also hinder rapid reaction kinetics.
Several studies have reported that sulfide-based ASSLSBs exhibit
discharge capacities surpassing theoretical values during cycling
[50–52], a phenomenon attributed to the decomposition of ISEs,
which contributes additional capacity. Due to the limited elec-
trochemical windows, approximately 1.5–2.5 V vs. Li+/Li, sul-
fide-based ISEs are prone to oxidative decomposition at the
cathode interface [53–55]. Although the capacity is partially
reversible, this undesirable side reaction inevitably deteriorates
the triple-phase (the phase between sulfur, conductive carbon,
and ISEs) interface of the composite cathode, leading to battery
capacity degradation and severe polarization [55–59]. The by-
products of sulfide-based ISEs obstruct electron and ion trans-
port. As a result, the active material is deprived of an adequate
supply of ions and electrons, which is detrimental to the reaction
kinetics. Hence, it is essential to implement effective strategies to
decrease side reactions.
In addition to improving the conductivity of cathodes to
provide favorable conditions for rapid reaction kinetics, it is also
essential to clarify the reaction mechanisms of ASSLSB cathodes
to provide guidance. Conventionally, the single charge/discharge
plateau observed in ASSLSBs has been attributed to a conversion
between S8 and Li2S/Li2S2, in contrast to the two plateaus
representing soluble long-chain LiPSs in liquid-state Li-S bat-
teries [55,60,61]. Recent advanced characterization studies have
unveiled the electrochemical reaction mechanism of sulfur,
demonstrating the formation of an intermediate Li2S2 phase
[62]. Specially designed operando Raman spectroscopy demon-
strated that the reaction of S8 is incomplete during discharge,
while a new peak representing Li2S2 emerges at the half-charged
state and gradually disappears as charging proceeds. Com-
plementary X-ray absorption spectroscopy further confirmed the
presence of various species in the cathode at different discharge/
charge depths, with Li2S2 accumulating after multiple cycles due
to sluggish reaction kinetics. Another study employed time-of-
flight secondary ion mass spectrometry to quantitatively analyze
the relative proportions of Li2S2 and Li2S [51]. In addition, it is
noteworthy that the formation of LiPSs during cycling in
ASSLSBs has also been observed in S9.3I-based cathodes as well
as in Li2S cathodes co-doped with Cu+ and I−, highlighting the
complexity of underlying reaction mechanisms in all-solid-state
configurations [63,64]. Based on the above studies, the slow
charge transfer kinetics of S8, Li2S, and Li2S2 result in sluggish
reaction kinetics of the solid-solid conversion, which in turn
affects the sulfur utilization, capacity retention, and electro-
chemical reversibility of ASSLSBs. Therefore, strategies should
be sought to catalyze sulfur conversion for improved perfor-
mance.
The full lithiation of sulfur is accompanied by an approxi-
mately 80% volume expansion. During cycling, the repeated
volume changes of sulfur can damage the electrode structure and
disrupt the solid-solid contact within the sulfur cathode. As a
result, more and more sulfur is lost from the reaction, leading to
rapid capacity fade. Previous research has demonstrated that
applying external pressure during charge/discharge can assist
batteries in accommodating volume changes and effectively
enhance capacity output and cycling stability [65,66]. The

PROMOTION OF REACTION KINETICS
Construction of conductive networks
To achieve rapid reaction kinetics, it is essential to ensure that
the cathodes exhibit efficient electron and ion transfer cap-
abilities. The importance of minimizing particle size and
achieving uniform distribution in cathode active materials is
widely recognized. Smaller particles generally exhibit a larger
surface area, leading to improved electrochemical performance
by facilitating the formation of conductive networks. Various
methods such as mechanical ball milling and vapor deposition to
obtain well-dispersed sulfur have been extensively reported
[50,70–74]. From a microscopic perspective, each sulfur particle
should be in contact with ISEs to facilitate electron and ion
transport. On a macroscopic level, the cathode interior should be
structured with uniform and continuous pathways for electron
and ion transmission. Thus, it is necessary to explore feasible
strategies to achieve a cathode with an ideal conductive network.
Various conductive carbons, such as carbon black, graphene,
carbon nanotubes (CNTs), and vapor-grown carbon fibers
(VGCF), are commonly incorporated into solid-state sulfur
cathodes to enhance electronic conductivity. Among them,
fibrous carbon with high aspect ratios not only effectively
reduces
detrimental
contact
with
sulfide-based
ISEs
and
strengthens the mechanical properties of the cathode but also
participates in the formation of a three-dimensional conductive
network. Modifying the carbon in the sulfur cathode has been
demonstrated to significantly improve the performance of
ASSLSBs. Zhao et al. [75] proposed a novel hybrid sulfur cath-
ode whose host is composed of lithium-ion/electron conductive
Nb18W16O93 (NWO) and CNTs. After constructing conductive
frameworks with NWO and CNTs, the electronic conductivity
was further enhanced. The S/NWO/CNT composite exhibited
an electronic conductivity of 5.14 × 10−6 S cm−1. Electrochemical
tests demonstrated that S/NWO/CNT possessed superior cycling
stability (1262 mA h g−1 after 1000 cycles) and better rate cap-
ability compared to S/CNT and S/NWO. Another composite
material with polypyrrole (PPy) coated on nitrogen-doped CNTs
(NCNTs), named PPy@NCNT, has been reported to possess
superior mechanical properties and electrical conductivity [44].
The coating of PPy on NCNTs was realized through a liquid-
phase polymerization process. The inclusion of 5% PPy@NCNT
within the composite cathode resulted in the formation of a
continuous, conductive pathway (Fig. 2a), reducing the tortu-
osity of electron transport and achieving a high electronic con-

Benefiting from the well-designed conductive pathway, thick

the voids and provided outstanding interfacial stability (Fig. 2b).
The VGs-Li2S thin-film cathode-matched lithium-phosphorous-

to achieve high areal capacity for TFLSBs. It is also worth
mentioning that besides incorporating conductive additives to
enhance the electronic conductivity, the intrinsic electrical
conductivity of sulfur-based active materials is also critical.
Beyond Li2S and S, metal sulfides have been extensively utilized
as cathode materials in ASSLSBs due to their superior electrical
conductivity [77]. Furthermore, their conversion products,
including metals and Li2S, can enhance the electrical con-
ductivity of the cathode after discharge. Wan and colleagues [78]
prepared Co-doped FeS2 nanospheres by a simple solvothermal
method with improved electronic conductivity, which exhibited
excellent rate performance and reduced polarization.
In addition to increasing the electronic conductivity, effective
ion transport within the cathode is crucial. Traditionally, ISEs
have been incorporated into sulfur cathodes to enhance ionic
conductivity [79–81]. However, conventional ISEs often face
challenges such as poor contact with active materials and
insufficient
ion
pathways.
Recently,
several
studies
have
employed novel ionic conductors, such as lithium salts, to
enhance the performance of ASSLSBs, demonstrating enhanced
compatibility. Fujita et al. [82] proposed a novel strategy of
utilizing the amorphous Li2O-LiI electrolyte to construct
nanoscale lithium-ion conductive pathways. The Li2O-LiI elec-
trolyte possesses a relatively high ionic conductivity (3.1 × 10−5
S cm−1 for 67Li2O·33LiI (mol%) at 25°C) to activate Li2S and
deliver high capacities. By combining the nanoscale Li2O-LiI ion
conductive network with microscale ion transport channels from
sulfide-based ISEs, a battery with a high Li2S loading of
10 mg cm−2 exhibited outstanding capacities and cyclability over
50 cycles at room temperature. In lithium metal batteries,
additives containing F, N, and S in the electrolyte have been
shown to facilitate the formation of a solid electrolyte interface
with high ionic conductivity and low migration barriers.
Inspired by this, another effort involved introducing LiTFSI into
the cathode to construct ion pathways (Fig. 2c) [52]. After
adding LiTFSI inside the sulfur cathode, a large amount of LiF
and Li3N are generated during cycling. Density functional theory
calculations find that LiF and Li3N have lower lithium-ion
migration barriers than the Li6PS5Cl electrolyte, which can
effectively widen the ion pathways in the cathode and improve
the interface between sulfur and electrolyte. The S-Ketjen Black
(KB)-LiTFSI cathode material exhibits outstanding initial capa-
city and long cycling performance, proving the effectiveness of
this strategy.
Benefiting from the suitable ionic conductivity and relatively
low melting point of the commercial LiFSI, another study
employed it as a solid-state catholyte in the cathode, achieving
promising results (Fig. 2d) [83]. After brief heat treatment at
150°C, LiFSI can fill the pores in the cathode, and upon cooling,
it can maintain satisfactory interfacial contact and ionic con-
duction. Concurrently, the incorporation of nanographene wires
(NGWs) provides electronic conductivity and structural rein-
forcement within the cathode, allowing the cathode to remain
intact during high-temperature processing. Finally, by using
sulfurized polyacrylonitrile with better thermal stability as the
cathode active material and preparing a three-phase composite
cathode matched with a bilayer Li7La3Zr2O12 (LLZO) ISE,
ASSLSBs with stable cycling capability were successfully fabri-
cated.
Alleviation of ISE decomposition
The undesirable side reaction of sulfide-based ISEs inevitably
deteriorates the triple-phase (the phase between sulfur, con-
ductive carbon, and ISEs) interface of the composite cathode,
leading to battery capacity degradation and severe polarization,
the challenge that necessitates further resolution [55,56]. To
promote the reaction kinetics, it is essential to prevent the for-
mation of detrimental inert by-products, reducing the occur-
rence
of
electrolyte
decomposition,
and
maintaining
unobstructed electron and ion transport within the cathode. As
calculated by Zhu et al. [53], sulfide-based ISEs exhibit relatively
poor stability compared to other typical solid electrolytes,
characterized by a narrower stability window (Fig. 3a). Recent
operando photoelectron spectroscopy analysis of Li6PS5Cl indi-
cates that the reduction begins at potentials of EWE = 1.75 V vs.
Li+/Li [59]. Furthermore, the contact between carbon and ISEs
exacerbates this side reaction. Tan et al. [54] suggested that
reducing the surface area of sulfide-based electrolytes could limit
their exposure to electronically conductive surfaces, thereby
minimizing electron pathways and effectively slowing the
kinetics of ISE decomposition. Therefore, the primary con-
sideration should be to regulate the conductive carbon content
and reduce the contact area between them. Recent X-ray photo-
electron spectroscopy analysis was conducted on the cycled
sulfur-containing composite cathode surfaces [84]. The signal
intensity corresponding to the irreversible decomposition pro-
duct P2S5 is notably weaker for the designed soybean flour
carbon cathode compared to the conventional flour carbon
cathode. This phenomenon can be attributed to the carbon
framework’s ability to inhibit electron aggregation on the elec-
trolyte surface. Additionally, the uniform distribution of sulfur
on the framework surface reduces direct contact between the
electrolyte and carbon, thereby demonstrating that the mesh-like
carbon structure could effectively mitigate the irreversible
decomposition of the Li6PS5Cl ISEs. To extend this approach, Jin
et al. [85] replaced the carbon host with platelet-ordered
mesoporous silica, effectively reducing the decomposition.
The application of conductive carbon in the cathode is
essential for the intrinsically insulating sulfur, and it is unrea-
listic to prohibit the use of conductive carbon in the cathode
[17]. Considering the dual character of carbon, efforts should be
made to improve the electrochemical stability of the electrolyte.
Strategies to widen the electrochemical window of sulfide-based
ISEs include electrolyte-cathode interface engineering, micro-

Catalytic strategy
Due to the sluggish reaction kinetics of the active material sulfur,
catalysts are widely used in liquid-state Li-S batteries to accel-
erate the conversion process of sulfur cathodes [90,91]. In the
ASSLSB system, cathode design faces even greater challenges due
to the limited solid-solid contact and slower reaction activity,
which can similarly be mitigated by employing catalytic strate-
gies [92]. Catalysts play a critical role in regulating the discharge
products of sulfur. For instance, Kim et al. [51] set the lower
voltage limit to 0.6 V (vs. Li-In/Li+) and introduced LiI as a
catalyst to induce Li2S2 as the dominant discharge product. The
battery without LiI suffered an approximately 18% loss in initial
discharge capacity after charging, whereas the cell incorporating
6 wt% LiI was fully reversible and exhibited reduced electrode
polarization. This is attributed to the catalytic role of LiI in
facilitating the electrochemical oxidation of Li2S and Li2S2,
thereby enhancing reversibility and cycling stability, albeit at the
expense of a slight sacrifice in initial discharge capacity.
Single-atom catalysts have also been utilized to accelerate the
rate of sulfur reactions by lowering the activation energy. Zhong
et al. [93] employed a simple ball-milling method to synthesize a
Co-N4 single-atom catalyst embedded in an acetylene black
matrix (Co@AB) as a sulfur host. The assembled batteries
exhibit excellent performance, which can be attributed to the
catalytic mechanism induced by Co-N4. Due to the d-p orbital
hybridization between Co-N4 and sulfur, a Co–S bond is formed
after sulfur loading. During the discharge process, the intense
electron transfer effectively weakens the S–S bonds, promoting
the conversion of sulfur to Li2S. Conversely, during the charging
process, electrons are transferred from Co-N4 to sulfur, which
helps to weaken the strength of the Li–S bonds and reduce the
energy barrier for the oxidation of Li2S back to sulfur (Fig. 4a).
The reversible electron transfer and bond modulation, facilitated
by the strong chemical affinity between Co-N4 and sulfur spe-
cies, enable efficient electrocatalysis of the sulfur redox reactions
(Fig. 4b).
The contact between the catalyst and the active material is also
crucial. Kang et al. [94] integrated nanosized NiO particles as an
electrocatalyst, with N-doped super-p (NSP) host. This config-
uration facilitates rapid electron transfer to the electrocatalyst
surface, simultaneously optimizing the available surface area for
catalytic processes. Electrochemical analysis, including galva-
nostatic intermittent titration technique and cyclic voltammetry,
demonstrated that the NiO@NSP cathode exhibited a significant

MAINTENANCE OF INTIMATE SOLID-SOLID
CONTACT
Mitigation of volume change
Physical confinement is an intuitive approach. Restricting sulfur
within a host can effectively alleviate the degradation of the
cathode caused by volume changes. A two-dimensional ordered
mesoporous carbon (OMC) framework has been developed as
an advanced host for Li2S and Li6PS5Cl (Fig. 5a), significantly
enhancing the electronic conductivity of the cathode [95]. After
200 cycles, the battery based on the OMC framework maintains
a reversible capacity of 1083.8 mA h g−1, with a high capacity
retention rate of 96.8%. Post-cycling scanning electron micro-
scopy (SEM) images show no significant fracturing, demon-
strating the strong structural stability of the Li2S/OMC/Li6PS5Cl
cathode, highlighting the effectiveness of physical confinement.
The effect of physical confinement is closely related to the
temperature of the loading process, which can significantly
influence the binding efficacy between sulfur and its host. In
contrast to the conventional method of impregnating sulfur into
the carbon host by rendering it molten at approximately 155°C,
elevating the sulfur processing temperature facilitates the for-
mation of smaller sulfur particles. This assists sulfur in pene-
trating the pores more effectively, thereby achieving superior
physical confinement effects. The study by Alzahrani and col-
leagues [50] employs 300°C vapor deposition to encapsulate
sulfur and its discharge product, Li2S, within porous carbon.
This method effectively minimizes the presence of sulfur outside
the porous matrix, while ensuring a uniform distribution of
finely sized sulfur particles and their deep physical confinement
within the carbon structure. Compared to conventional methods
such as liquid deposition and solid deposition, the vapor
deposition technique exhibits enhanced electrochemical perfor-
mance. Confining both sulfur and Li2S within the porous carbon
host during cycling is crucial for achieving optimal performance.
In another work, sulfur nanocrystals were grown on both sides
of graphene via vapor deposition technique to form a sandwich-
structured sulfur/carbon composite (Fig. 5b) [74]. The nanoscale
sulfur crystals, when confined within this unique architecture
design, can relieve mechanical stress and maintain stable triple-
phase contact in the cathode. On the other hand, the strong C–S
bonds on the graphene particles are also favorable for accel-
erating reaction kinetics. The effect of structural engineering
enabled the assembled ASSLSB to achieve a stable cycling of
2400 cycles at room temperature. Therefore, high-temperature
vapor deposition can be used to prepare sulfur/carbon compo-
sites, physically confining sulfur, in the hope of achieving better
triple-phase contact and more stable cycling performance in
sulfur composite cathodes.
However, using carbon-based sulfur hosts to limit volume
expansion easily leads to the electrolyte decomposition issue
mentioned earlier. Another promising strategy is to introduce a
redox mediator to actively adapt the cathode’s volume change in
ASSLSBs. As the fully lithiated product of sulfur, Li2S, possesses
the maximum volume during the reaction process, offering
potential advantages when employed as the cathode active
material. The structural design of the Li2S cathode does not need
to account for expansion-induced damage, only contraction-
induced degradation, which can be accommodated by using a
redox mediator. Recently, a novel Li2S/LiVS2 core-shell structure
has been reported (Fig. 5c), where the 30 nm shell functions
dually as a charge carrier transport vehicle and an electron
transfer mediator for Li2S [96]. The two-phase interface estab-
lished by this core-shell cathode structure provides well-con-
nected electron and ion pathways surrounding the active
material, maintaining stable contact even during volume chan-
ges. Assembling this core-shell cathode with an argyrodite-type
Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 ISE, the ASSLSB exhibits excellent rate capability
(3 mA cm−2) and cycling stability (over 1000 cycles). Yu et al.
[97] employed high-energy ball milling to design a novel Li2S-

While the strategies discussed previously have proven effective

warrants consideration is the accumulation of stress. This stress
buildup within the composite cathode can lead to mechanical
degradation, which is detrimental to reversibility and stability.
Therefore, enhancing mechanical stability by addressing other
components of the composite cathode is crucial. Elastic poly-
mers are favorable candidates for mitigating stress accumulation,
but their preparation typically involves the use of polar organic
solvents to achieve acceptable homogeneity, which is incompa-

necessary to explore processing techniques and enhancement

facilitating electronic conduction. Subsequently, the Li2S@C
composite was infiltrated with a sulfide electrolyte, forming an
ionically conductive network surrounding the Li2S (Fig. 6a). This
design maximizes the tri-phase interface while maintaining high
active loadings.
Precoating active materials with ISEs is a viable strategy. Kim
et al. [103] induced an interfacial chemical reaction during
electrode fabrication by employing a weakly polar solvent,
forming polysulfide-intermediate compounds that bridged S8 to
the Li6PS5Cl electrolyte, followed by solvent removal via drying.
The resulting cathode exhibited superior solid-solid contact, a
compact morphology, and high ionic conductivity. Electro-
chemical analysis revealed that the modulated discharge-charge
behavior stemmed from the reversible cleavage and formation of
S–S bridges by the intermediate phase. The design strategy,
judiciously utilizing the weakly polar isopropyl acetate, is
applicable for tailoring solid-solid interfaces in high-energy
ASSLSBs and holds the promising potential for slurry-coating
solid-state sulfur cathodes. Atomic layer deposition (ALD) is
also used in engineering the cathode-electrolyte interface for
ASSLSBs [104]. The nanoscale lithium phosphorus oxide (LPO)
coating by ALD facilitated effective contact between the C/S
particles and ISEs, with its ion-conducting nature promoting Li+
transport across the interface. Notably, its electronic insulation
mitigated the detrimental electrolyte decomposition mentioned
previously (Fig. 6b), while the robust LPO layer suppressed the
mechanical degradation arising from sulfur’s volumetric chan-
ges, maintaining intimate electrode-electrolyte contact.
Recently, the construction of thin-film cathodes has been
regarded as an effective strategy to achieve tight solid-solid
interfaces in ASSLSBs. By leveraging advanced deposition
techniques, thin-film cathodes can be fabricated with compact
structures, smooth surfaces, and uniform thickness, enabling
intimate contact with the electrolyte [105,106]. This tight
interface minimizes interfacial resistance, facilitating seamless
ion and electron transport between the cathode and electrolyte.
Moreover, thin-film cathodes have advantages in volumetric
energy density due to their dense and compact feature. He et al.
[107] prepared FeOxSy thin-film cathode by radio frequency
magnetron sputtering, maintaining a tight interface contact with
ISE. Fan et al. [108] developed a low-temperature sintered thin-
film Li2S cathode that was densified without applying pressure,
forming a tight solid-solid interface. The tight contact enables
the battery to operate under low or no voltage conditions and
exhibit flexibility.
Instead of merely constructing interfaces during battery
manufacturing, the intimate solid-solid contact at the cathode
can be actively reconstructed during the battery lifetime.
Recently, a novel healable active material S9.3I has been applied

repair of the microstructure in the cathode composite (Fig. 6c).

SUMMARY AND OUTLOOKS
The issues related to the sulfur cathode have significantly hin-
dered the further development and application of ASSLSBs. On
this basis, we first summarize the main challenges faced by solid-
state sulfur composite cathodes, including slow reaction kinetics
and poor solid-solid contact. Then, to address these issues, the
latest cathode modification strategies and corresponding per-
formances are summarized in Table 1. While significant pro-
gress has been made, further enhancements are essential for
achieving optimal performance. The following outlook is pro-
vided.
(1) Establishing an effective conductive network within solid-
state sulfur cathodes is a key issue for the practical application of
high-energy-density ASSLSBs, as it helps reduce charge transfer
resistance and accelerate electrochemical reaction kinetics. The
introduction of fibrous carbon and solid-state electrolytes with
better ionic conductivity is an effective approach.
(2) The inactive products resulting from the decomposition of
ISEs negatively impact the sulfur conversion process. While
carbon offers numerous advantages, its dual character must be
carefully considered when used with sulfide-based ISEs. To
address it, it is essential to design carbon structures thoughtfully
and determine suitable proportions. Currently, strategies such as
coatings or mesh-like carbon are commonly employed to
minimize their contact. Additionally, electrolyte doping is used
to enhance electrochemical stability. Moving forward, further
efforts should focus on optimizing the compatibility of various
ISEs with sulfur cathodes to achieve superior performance.
(3) Catalytic conversion has become a hot topic in the field of
liquid-state Li-S batteries, and in recent years, there have been
some studies applying it to ASSLSBs, showing interesting per-
formance. However, the role of catalysts in solid-solid conver-
sion is still not clear enough, and efforts should be made to
explore how to further enhance the effect of catalysts and
achieve significant improvements with small amounts of addi-
tives.
(4) The large volume change of sulfur has always been an
ongoing challenge, as it is unfavorable for maintaining close
solid-solid contact during the cycling process. To address this
issue, in current research, researchers commonly employ phy-
sical confinement of sulfur to protect the cathode structure from
being destroyed. The addition of elastic polymer components in
the cathode can effectively alleviate stress accumulation, and
directly using Li2S with the largest volume as the cathode active
component is also a common strategy. Future research should
consider combining multiple strategies to synergistically miti-
gate the volume change of sulfur and maintain the integrity of
the cathode structure and interface.
(5) Solid-solid interface enhancement within all-solid-state
systems remains a significant challenge in research. Current
efforts are primarily concentrated on designing effective solid-
solid interfaces, including the in-situ synthesis of electrolyte
interfaces and applying coatings to the surfaces of cathode
materials. Interestingly, in recent research, researchers have
explored the periodic restoration of interface within the battery’s
lifetime. Given the inherent volume changes associated with
sulfur, it is essential to explore strategies for enhancing solid-
solid interfaces both during the battery manufacturing process
and throughout its operational lifecycle.
Finally, given the diverse and formidable challenges that sulfur
cathodes still face, we believe that by combining different stra-
tegies and leveraging their respective strengths, multi-pronged
optimization can be achieved. Employing Li2S, sulfurized poly-
acrylonitrile, or other sulfide materials as the cathode active
materials, rather than conventional S/C composites, may
potentially facilitate the commercialization of ASSLSBs. Mod-
ifying the ISEs to improve their chemical stability and opti-
mizing the fabrication process (such as wet-process) to achieve

无机全固态锂硫电池: 增强反应动力学和固-固接触
策略综述
黄泽伟1†, 邓俐颖2†, 李王阳1, 张颉1, 廖书裕1, 张红1*, 王星辉1,3*
摘要
基于无机固态电解质的全固态锂硫电池具有高安全性和高能量
密度的优点, 成为未来锂电池的有力竞争者. 然而, 全固态复合正极因
使用硫基活性材料而存在两个关键难题: 电化学反应动力学缓慢以及
硫、导电剂和固体电解质相之间的固-固接触不足. 这直接影响了其电
化学性能, 阻碍了商业化进程. 在本综述中, 我们首先讨论了引起这些
挑战的内因, 以便从根本上理解潜在的改进方向. 随后, 深入探讨了在
提高硫反应动力学和优化固-固接触方面的最新进展, 并对各种策略的
基本原理、制造技术和由此带来的性能提升进行了系统的分类、总结
和评估. 最后, 展望了全固态硫基复合正极所面临的挑战及未来研究
方向.
