A B S T R A C T
The increasing demand for high-performance energy storage has intensified the pursuit of alternatives to con­
ventional lithium-ion batteries. Lithium-sulfur (Li–S) batteries has been extensively used due to their high 
theoretical energy density (2600 Wh kg−1), low cost, and sulfur’s environmental benefits. However, traditional 
Li–S systems face challenges including polysulfide shuttle effects, lithium dendrite formation, and limited cycle 
life. Incorporating solid-state electrolytes (SSEs) have enhanced the safety and stability by replacing flammable 
liquids. Recent progress in solid-state Li–S (SSLS) batteries includes development of high-conductivity SSEs 
(sulfide, halide, polymer-ceramic composites), electrodes provided with the volume changes and minimize 
interfacial resistance, and improved cathode architectures for optimized ion/electron transport. This review 
comprehensively analyzes the development in solid-state lithium-sulfur (SSLS) batteries over the past decade. 
SSLS development is driven by the potential for higher energy density and enhanced safety that have been 
essential for next-generation energy storage.This review also focuses on solid electrolytes as the key enabler for 
solid-state lithium-sulfur (SSLS) battery performance, addressing the challenges associated with liquid electro­
lytes such as flammability, polysulfide shuttle, and lithium dendrite formation. Finally, the review highlights the 
importance of integrated cell design, where optimized electrode architectures and advanced solid electrolytes 
work synergistically to maximize performance..
1. Introduction
Lithium-sulfur (Li–S) batteries have been exhibiting the remarkable 
theoretical capacities of both lithium (3,860mAh g−1) and sulfur 
(1,675mAh g−1) electrodes [1–3]. However, a major challenge for liquid 
Li–S batteries is the "shuttle effect.” This phenomenon occurs during the 
reaction of sulfur dissolve into the liquid electrolyte and migrate to the 
anode by the formation of polysulfides. Such migration has led to a loss 
of active materials and reduces battery efficiency [4]. Although the 
design of carbon scaffolds to trap sulfur can help in the mitigation of this 
issue, a more promising approach is required to eliminate the shuttle 
effect by replacing liquid electrolytes with solid-state electrolytes. 
Various solid-state options are available, including polymers, oxides, 
halides [5], and sulfides [6,7]. However, polymer electrolytes often 
suffer from low ionic conductivity at room temperature and do not fully 
suppress the shuttle effect of lithium polysulfides [8]. Oxide electrolytes 
are mechanically strong, and hence difficult to integrate into the sulfur 
cathodes [9]. Halide electrolytes, despite their high ionic conductivity 
and voltage stability, it has an undesirable electrochemical reactions 
with lithium metal anodes [10–13]. All-solid-state lithium batteries 
(ASSLBs) offer advantages over conventional batteries, including 
enhanced safety, higher energy density, and improved cycling stability. 
These benefits are obtained due to the replacement of volatile liquid 
electrolytes with solid-state electrolytes. However, challenges in mate­
rials design, interfacial stability, and scalable manufacturing continue to 
hinder 
their 
widespread 
commercialization

2. Fabrication methods of solid-state electrolytes
Solid-state electrolytes (SSEs) play a crucial role in advancing next- 
generation energy storage technologies because of their safety, high 
thermal stability, and compatibility with high-energy electrode mate­
rials. Wang et al., [39] reported that Li6PS5Cl solid-state electrolytes 
were prepared through a solid-state sintering method. Stoichiometric 
amounts of Li2S, LiCl, and P2S5 were combined in a molar ratio of 5:2:1 
and subjected to ball milling at 100 rpm for 1 h in an argon-protected 
environment to ensure uniform mixing and prevent contamination. 
The homogenized precursor mixture was then encapsulated in a quartz 
tube and thermally treated within the temperature range of 350–620 ◦C 
for 10 h using a heating rate of 1.5 ◦C min−1. After sintering, the product 
was gradually cooled to ambient temperature. The optimal crystalline 
phase was achieved near 550 ◦C, and the duration of the sintering 
process was further adjusted between 10 min and 20 h to enhance the 
phase homogeneity and ionic transport properties. Seino et al., [40] 
through their investigations on the melt-quenching technique, coupled 
with subsequent high-temperature processing, demonstrated as an 
effective strategy to enhance the ionic conductivity of solid electrolytes. 
In this approach, the precursor materials are sealed and heated to their 
melting temperature, followed by either rapid quenching or controlled 
cooling to ambient conditions. This process facilitates the formation of 
an amorphous or partially crystalline structure favorable for lithium-ion 
transport. Notably, Li2S–P2S5 glass-ceramic electrolytes fabricated via 
the melt-quenching followed by cold-pressing method have exhibited an 
impressive ionic conductivity of approximately 1.7 × 10−2 S cm−1, 
highlighting the effectiveness of this synthesis route in improving elec­
trochemical performance. Kuhn et al., [41] explored an another series of 
LGPS-based electrolytes with the general formula Li11−xM2−xP1+xS12 
with M = Ge, Sn, Si which were prepared by high-pressure synthesis at 
723 K with pressures in the range 3–5 GPa. The β-Li3PS4 (LPS) phase was 
synthesized using a liquid-phase approach reported by Wang et al., [37] 
in which Li2S and P2S5 were mixed in a 3:1 M ratio and dispersed in 
anhydrous THF, followed by continuous stirring for 24 h at 25 ◦C. 
Subsequently, a LiBH4 solution was introduced into the suspension using 
a high-precision pipette, maintaining with a molar ratio of LiBH4:Li2S: 
P2S5 = 4:3:1. The resulting mixture was stirred for an additional 48 h, 
and transferred to a Schlenk flask and subjected to vacuum drying at 
100 ◦C for 2 h to eliminate residual solvent. The dried powder was then 
annealed at 160 ◦C for 3 h under an argon atmosphere, yielding the LPB 
glass-ceramic argyrodite solid electrolyte. Zhang et al., [42] reports that 
the fiber-network-based elastomeric solid electrolyte (FESE) was fabri­
cated through in situ polymerization of methoxy polyethylene glycol 
(mPEG), which infiltrated and filled the inter-fiber voids of the fiber 
electrolyte structural membrane (FESM). The precursor solution was 
formulated by dissolving lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide 
(LiTFSI) and the thermal initiator azobisisobutyronitrile in mPEG with a 
molar ratio of LiTFSI:AIBN:mPEG = 3:0.01:8. Phuc et al. [43] employed 
a liquid-phase shaking method to prepare Li3PS4, offering a milder

3. Polymer solid-state electrolytes (PSSEs)
3.1. Polymer electrolytes
The development of poly (ethylene oxide) (PEO)-based solid elec­
trolytes faces a primary challenge due to their inherent crystallinity, 
which restricts efficient ion transport. To address this, the studies were 
focused to increase the amorphous of PEO while simultaneously tackling 
critical issues such as the polysulfide shuttle effect and lithium dendrite 
growth. Various structural modification strategies have been explored to 
achieve this goal. For example, the incorporation of functional fillers 
like fullerene (C60) [49] and magnesium fluoride (MgF2) [50] disrupts 
the ordered arrangement of PEO chains, reduces the crystalline regions 
and thereby promoting amorphous domains. Fullerene enhances PEO 
segmental mobility, leading to significantly improved ionic conductiv­
ity. Magnesium fluoride suppresses polysulfide migration through 
strong Lewis’s acid-base interactions and promotes the formation of a 
stable solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) rich in LiF and LiMg, that are 
crucial for inhibiting lithium dendrite growth and extending cycling life. 
Additionally, molecular-level techniques such as chemical cross-linking 
[51] and copolymerization allow precise control over PEO chain 
conformation, for reducing crystallinity and enhancing ion mobility and 
conductivity [52]. The addition of inorganic nanoparticles like kaolin 
nanoclay increases PEO chain flexibility [53], that extends the electro­
lyte’s structure and electrochemical performance. Collectively, these 
strategies have greatly advanced PEO-based electrolytes by expanding 
their amorphous regions and enhancing their electrochemical proper­
ties. Fig. 3A shows the unique cyclic structure of β-cyclodextrin (β-CD) 
that plays a vital role in improving PEO-based electrolytes. β-CD disrupts 
the ordered PEO chains via non-covalent interactions, altering the 
dihedral and torsional angles of the chains. This reduces crystallinity 
and expands amorphous regions, creating additional pathways for Li+
ion migration and increasing polymer segment flexibility. β-CD also 
strengthens the anchoring of TFSI−anions, facilitating Li+ dissociation 
and mobility. These structural modifications have helped to suppress the 
polysulfide shuttle by anchoring polysulfides, thereby limiting their 
diffusion and mitigating their negative impact on the electrochemical 
performance of all-solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) [54]. 
Shao et al. [55] illustrates the fabrication and structural advantages of 
the CPETM-MES composite polymer electrolyte (Fig. 3B). A key feature 
is the formation of chemical bridges linking LGPS nanoparticles to PEO 
chains through strong S–Si bonds, which enhance interfacial compati­
bility, prevent LGPS degradation at both lithium anode and sulfur 
cathode, and facilitate rapid Li+ transport. The CPETM-MES electrolyte 
exhibits superior mechanical strength (maximum stress of 0.96 MPa) 
and enhanced flexibility compared to other reference electrolytes. 
Fig. 3C presents electrochemical characterization data demonstrating 
the exceptional performance of CPETM-MES. This composite exhibits an 
improved electrochemical stability window of 5.54 V and maintains 
long-term stability in symmetric cells for over 1800 h at a current den­
sity of 1.05 mA/cm2. When it is applied in all-solid-state Li–S cells, it 
achieves an initial discharge capacity of 1227 mAh g−1, retaining 904 
mAh g−1 after 100 cycles. These results have highlighted the electro­
lyte’s high energy density and excellent cycling stability. Li et al. [56] 
summarizes the key challenges faced by composite polymer electrolytes 
(CPEs) in solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries (SSLSBs) (Fig. 3D). Poly­
sulfide diffusion has a significant issue, with prompting the need for 
LiPS-insoluble solid polymer electrolyte (SPE) matrices.

3.2. Polymer-in-salt electrolytes
Polymer-in-salt electrolytes (PISEs) are an emerging class of mate­
rials notable for their superior ionic conductivity at room temperature 
compared to conventional solid polymer electrolytes (SPEs) [57]. Liu 
et al. [58] developed an advanced polymer-in-salt solid-state electrolyte 
(PISSE) based on PVDF-HFP, which was incorporated into an 
all-solid-state lithium-sulfur battery (ASSLSB) featuring a fully 
PISSE-penetrated 3D cathode. This design offers two main advantages 
over traditional solid-state Li–S batteries: (1) the PVDF-HFP-based PISSE 
has exhibited strong mechanical properties due to its crystalline struc­
ture and a high density of fluorine groups that allow dissolution of large 
amounts of lithium salt, achieving a high ionic conductivity of 1.24 ×
10−4 S cm−1 at room temperature; (2) complete infiltration of PISSE into 
the 3D electrode enhances interfacial contact and stabilizes the electrode 
structure. In a related study, Chen et al. [59] have fabricated a 
polysiloxane-based polymer-in-salt electrolyte using BPSO, LiTFSI, and 
PVDF, achieving an high ionic conductivity of 7.8 × 10−4 S cm−1, effi­
cient sulfur utilization, prolonged cycle life, and effective suppression of 
lithium dendrite formation. Both studies have emphasized that 
increasing the lithium salt content and optimizing the salt-to-polymer 
ratio significantly improve ionic conductivity and lithium-ion trans­
port, thereby enhancing the safety and long-term stability of ASSLSBs. 
Polyacrylonitrile (PAN) and its copolymers have been extensively 
investigated as base polymers for PISEs in recent years [60–64]. These 
PAN-based PISEs have demonstrated an impressive ionic conductivity 
values up to 10−4 S cm−1 and high lithium-ion transference numbers, 
often exceeding 0.4 at room temperature [63–65]. The enhanced ionic 
conductivity of PISEs arises from a distinct conduction mechanism 
related to the high lithium salt concentration, which promotes the 
formation of numerous ionic aggregates [66].
Yang et al. [67] investigated by involving the incorporation of 
(3-Aminopropyl) trimethoxysilane-modified manganese dioxide (MnO2) 
nanowires (AMW) into PISEs, addressing the challenge of balancing 
strong mechanical properties with efficient ion transport as shown in 
Fig. 4. By embedding ultra-long MnO2 nanowires functionalized with 
AMW into a PAN-LiTFSI-based polymer-in-salt electrolyte, they formed 
the PIS-AMW composite. This additive has enhanced the mechanical 
strength and facilitates lithium-ion conduction through a unique 
"hand-to-hand" hopping mechanism between ion clusters. The resulting 
electrolyte exhibits a tensile strength of 2.77 MPa, an ionic conductivity 
of 4.44 × 10−4 S cm−1, and a lithium-ion transference number of 0.61 at 
25 ◦C. Lithium symmetric cells using PIS-AMW demonstrate excellent 
cycling stability for over 600 h. Moreover, all-solid-state lithium-sulfur 
cells assembled with this electrolyte deliver a discharge capacity of 643 
mAh g−1 at 0.05C and 25 ◦C after 80 cycles.

4. Inorganic solid-state electrolytes (ISSEs)
4.1. Sulfide-based electrolytes
Sulfonated solid-state electrolytes (SSEs) hold significant promise for 
all-solid-state batteries (ASSBs) due to their high ionic conductivity, 
processability, strong mechanical properties, and excellent electro­
chemical stability. Capitalizing on these advantages, major companies 
such as Toyota, Samsung SDI, CATL, and Solid Energy are actively 
investing in the development of sulfide-based SSEs to accelerate ASSB 
commercialization, targeting improved safety and higher energy density 
[73,74]. Despite these benefits, sulfide SSEs face critical challenges, 
including narrow electrochemical stability windows, sensitivity of the 
moisture, and susceptibility to detrimental interfacial side reactions, all 
of which undermine the electrochemical performance and longevity of 
ASSBs. Consequently, achieving both high ionic conductivity and stable 
solid–solid interfaces remain essential for the successful widespread 
application of ASSB technology. Fig. 6 presents a concise timeline 
highlighting the recent advancements in sulfide-based composite elec­
trolyte films for ASSBs.
Ionic conductivity is a critical parameter for evaluating the perfor­
mance of solid-state electrolytes (SSEs). Significant advances have been 
made for enhancing the ionic conductivity of sulfide-based SSEs over 
recent decades. These sulfide SSEs are generally classified into glasses, 
glass-ceramics, and crystalline materials based on their structural 
characteristics. Among these, sulfide glass SSEs typically exhibit higher 
ionic conductivity than their crystalline counterparts, primarily due to 
their more open structure and larger free volume [75]. These materials 
are recognized for their high ionic conductivity and excellent 
chemo-mechanophysical properties [74,76,77]. While sulfide-based 
all-solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) show great promise 
for improved energy density and safety, the sensitivity of sulfide SSEs to 
air poses a significant challenge for practical application. These 
electrolytes are highly reactive with atmospheric moisture, leading to 
structural degradation and the release of toxic hydrogen sulfide (H2S) 
gas.
To address these limitations, multiple approaches have been devel­
oped to improve the stability of sulfide SSEs in air. These include 
incorporating H2S absorbents, elemental substitution, surface modifi­
cation, and designing novel material compositions to enhance the sta­
bility and processability [78]. Gu et al. [79] investigated asymmetrical 
sulfur redox pathways in sulfide-based ASSLSBs. Fig. 6A illustrates 
operando pressure changes in the sulfur cathode during cycling at 60 ◦C, 
recorded by an integrated force sensor. The pressure decreases non­
linearly during discharge and increases linearly during charge, corre­
sponding to volume changes linked to the partial molar volumes of the 
sulfur cathode and LiIn anode. Fig. 6B(a) shows the electrochemical

4.2. Oxide-based electrolytes
Umair et al. [80] reported an extensive research that continues to 
deepen our understanding of oxide solid electrolytes. These inorganic 
solid-state electrolytes (SSEs) are broadly classified into sulfide- and 
oxide-based types. Oxide SSEs offer several advantages over sulfide 
counterparts for many applications. Specifically, oxide-based SSEs 
generally exhibit good chemical stability, enhanced resistance to 
dendrite formation, and improved thermal stability. Additionally, oxide 
SSEs has maintained reliable performance over a broader temperature 
range, making them particularly suitable for demanding sectors such as 
aerospace and automotive industries [81,82]. The study has focused on 
oxide SSEs, NaSICON-type, LiSICON-type, and perovskite-type mate­
rials. Their distinct crystalline structures are illustrated in Fig. 7A, while 
Fig. 7B provides a chronological overview of key developments in oxide 
solid electrolyte research.
Oxide solid electrolytes have been facing several challenges in all- 
solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries (SSLSBs). Their inherent hardness 
and high grain boundary impedance has hindered the formation of an 
effective ion-conductive network within the solid sulfur cathode, even 
when mixed grinding is applied. Moreover, employing porous electrode 
designs helps to maintain battery capacity at high sulfur loadings by 
increasing the contact area between the electrode and electrolyte [83]. 
Solid-state electrolytes (SSEs) offer several design advantages, including 
enhanced mechanical strength that allows them to serve as structural 
supports, simplifying battery construction while improving safety and 
durability. Unlike traditional organic liquid electrolytes, SSEs possess

6.2. Electrochemical performance
6.2.1. High initial discharge capacity and coulombic efficiency
All-solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) are gaining signif­
icant attention due to their potential for high energy density and 
improved safety. However, a major challenge remains: achieving both a 
high initial discharge capacity and high Coulombic efficiency simulta­
neously. Yi et al. [125] evaluated the performance of ASSLSBs using a 
Li10SnP2S12 electrolyte. Their study demonstrated the influence of the 
annealing temperature on the electrolyte’s lithium-ion conductivity and 
activation energy. These batteries have exhibited an excellent perfor­
mance at room temperature, delivering a high initial capacity of 1601.7 
mAh g−1 at a current density of 40 mA g−1. They also showed impressive 
capacity retention, good rate capability, and stable Coulombic efficiency 
close to 100 % during extended cycling. Wang et al. [39] developed a 
lithium-sulfur solid-state battery using a Li6PS5Cl solid-state electrolyte

contact between the lithium metal anode and LATP, and enhancing the

over 200 h. Moreover, all-solid-state Lithium-SPAN batteries built with

long-term stability. Gao et al. [127] successfully applied redox chemis­

7. Challenges and future perspectives
7.1. Next-generation solid-state electrolytes
Recent advances in all-solid-state lithium batteries are largely driven 
by next-generation solid-state electrolytes (SSEs) [151], which replace 
liquid electrolytes with solid ionic conductors, offering improved safety, 
higher energy density, and longer cycle life. For example, Li10GeP2S12 
SSEs achieved high ionic conductivities up to 10−2 S cm−1 [152], 
comparable to liquid electrolytes, due to their soft sulfur-based lattices 
that facilitate lithium-ion diffusion. However, these sulfide electrolytes 
face issues such as moisture sensitivity and limited electrochemical 
stability. In contrast, more chemically stable oxide electrolytes, such as 
garnet-type Li7La3Zr2O12, generally have lower ionic conductivities 
below 10−4 S cm−1 [153]. Emerging halide and composite electrolytes 
offer promising balances between conductivity, mechanical flexibility, 
and interface compatibility [154,155]. The design of solid-state elec­
trolytes (SSEs) increasingly benefits from computational approaches 
such as density functional theory (DFT) and molecular dynamics (MD) 
simulations, which deepen the understanding of ion transport mecha­
nisms and structural stability. Addressing structural stability, interfacial 
resistance, and scalable synthesis challenges remains essential for 
commercializing SSEs in practical batteries [156–159].

7.2. Smart cathode and anode integration
Sulfur-based cathodes offer high theoretical energy density and 
abundant resources, making them as promising for future battery tech­
nologies [160]. However, their practical application is limited by sul­
fur’s low conductivity and volumetric changes during cycling. Reducing 
cathode particle size to below 10 μm improves capacity retention by 
enhancing electrical connectivity and ion transport through grain 
boundaries [161]. Moreover, the sulfur-iodide S9.3I molecular crystal, 
formed by doping sulfur with iodine, exhibits semiconductor-level 
conductivity (~5.9 × 10−7 S cm−1 at 25 ◦C) and a low melting point 
(~65 ◦C), enabling interface self-healing during cycling. Li–S9.3I cells 
maintain 400 stable cycles with 87 % capacity retention, representing a 
significant advancement towards practical solid-state lithium-sulfur 
battery technologies [34].
7.3. Cell manufacturing, cost, and scalability for EVs
A critical barrier to widespread EV battery adoption is the cost and 
complexity of manufacturing solid-state cells at scale. Previous sections 
have highlighted the materials used in SSEs, whose costs vary signifi­
cantly. For example, sulfide electrolytes such as lithium thiophosphate 
(Li3PS4) require significant amounts of costly raw materials like lithium 
sulfide (Li2S), which is approximately 1.4 times more expensive than 
battery-grade lithium hexafluorophosphate (LiPF6) commonly used in 
liquid electrolytes [162]. Producing 1 L of Li3PS4 electrolyte demands 
about 15.6 mol of Li2S versus 1 mol of LiPF6 per liter for liquid systems, 
amplifying cost differences. Additionally, manufacturing challenges 
such as high-temperature sintering for oxide electrolytes (e.g., LLZO 
sintered at ~1100 ◦C) to reduce porosity and impurities has further 
increased the production expenses. Protective coatings on cathodes to 
enhance compatibility add to the overall costs of solid-state electrolyte 
(SSE) systems. These cost factors, combined with the scaling demands of 
EV markets and emerging sectors like electric vertical take-off and 
landing (eVTOL) aircraft emphasize the critical importance of devel­
oping more cost-effective and scalable manufacturing processes. Such 
advancements are essential to make SSEs commercially viable and 
supporting their widespread adoption in diverse applications [163,164].
7.4. Sustainability and recycling across the EV lifecycle
Evaluating the sustainability of SSLS batteries requires full life cycle 
assessment (LCA), considering material impacts, component recovery 
feasibility (e.g., sulfur and lithium), and environmental footprints [165]. 
For example, Zhang et al. [166] combined LCA and entropy-weight 
approaches to assess environmental impacts of various battery tech­
nologies, finding Li–S batteries have the lowest usage-phase environ­
mental impact. However, local power infrastructure strongly influences 
outcomes; batteries deployed in China has exhibited higher carbon 
footprints and ecological impacts due to the current energy mix, indi­
cating the need for targeted regional infrastructure improvements to 
support greener EV adoption.
7.5. Integrated battery pack design for EVs
Effective thermal management remains paramount for EV safety and 
performance. Modern battery management systems (BMS) and innova­
tive pack-level cost reduction strategies are critical to maintain cell 
temperatures within optimal ranges (25–40 ◦C) and minimizing tem­
perature gradients, despite varied external conditions [167]. Advanced 
BMS helps to reduce maintenance costs by extending battery lifespan 
through precise performance optimization, mitigating premature fail­
ures and system downtime [168].
7.6. Policy, regulation, and market adoption
The EV market’s rapid growth, with a 55 % sales increase in 2022 to 
10 million units, stems from supportive policies, technological advances, 
and environmental awareness [169]. Developing regions face significant 
challenges in electric vehicle (EV) adoption, including limited and 
inadequate charging infrastructure, unstable electricity grids, and the 
high upfront costs of EVs. Current battery recycling rates remain low 
(5–7 %), despite regulatory efforts like the EU 2023 Battery Regulation 
promoting recycling and extended producer responsibility (EPR) [170]. 
Strengthening stakeholder collaboration, data-driven policies, and 
consumer education is essential to enhance battery recycling efficiency 
and foster a circular economy for lithium-ion batteries [171]. Fig. 20
summarizes the key challenges and strategic solutions for advancing 
solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries in energy storage applications.
8. Conclusion
Over the past decade, solid-state lithium-sulfur (SSLS) batteries have 
overcoming key limitations of traditional lithium-ion and liquid elec­
trolyte Li–S systems. Recent advancements feature high-ionic- 
conductivity solid electrolytes, especially fluoride-, sulfide-, and 
halide-based ones, which bolster safety via enhanced thermal stability, 
lithium dendrite suppression, and efficient ion transport. Improved 
cathode designs featuring sulfur-host architectures, functional in­
terlayers, and buffer coatings have mitigated polysulfide shuttle and 
accommodated volume changes during cycling. Stabilizing lithium 
metal anodes via engineered artificial interphases and interfaces has 
extended cycle life and electrochemical stability. These achievements 
have highlighted the strong potential of SSLS batteries’ for high-energy, 
safe, and durable energy storage, especially in electric vehicles.
However, challenges remain in scaling production, reducing costs, 
and ensuring long-term stability. Interfacial degradation, mechanical 
stress, and limited ionic conductivity at room temperature continue to 
impede commercialization. Overcoming these obstacles can be achieved 
by integrating fundamental and applied research. The future success of 
solid-state lithium-sulfur (SSLS) batteries depends critically on collab­
orative efforts across academia, industry, and policymakers to advance 
material innovations, optimize cell designs, and improve interface 
stability.
