A B S T R A C T  
Sulfide-based all-solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) have recently attracted great attention. The 
“shuttle effect” caused by the migration of polysulfides in conventional liquid lithium-sulfur batteries could be 
eliminated. Therefore, the utilization of active materials and cycling stability, as well as battery safety, can be 
significantly improved. Impressive progress has been made in the research of materials for sulfide-based 
ASSLSBs, including sulfide solid-state electrolytes (SSEs), sulfur-based cathode materials, and anode materials. 
However, challenges such as scalable fabrication of large-scale, high-areal capacity composite electrodes and 
robust, high-conductivity thin SSE films, as well as the serious electrochemo-mechanical issues in sulfur-based 
electrodes, still hinder the practical application and development of sulfide-based ASSLSBs. In this review, 
recent research progress on sulfide-based ASSLSBs materials is summarized and discussed. In addition, the 
challenges in the practical application of sulfide-based ASSLSBs, such as scalable composite electrodes/SSE 
membranes fabrication and complicated electrochemo-mechanical coupling effects, are also systematically 
overviewed.   
1. Introduction 
With high theoretical energy density (2600 Wh kg−1) and the low 
cost brought by the abundance of sulfur, lithium-sulfur batteries are 
considered one of the most promising candidates for next-generation 
energy storage devices [1–10]. In conventional liquid lithium-sulfur 
batteries, the sulfur electrode undergoes a “solid-liquid-solid” reaction. 
Taking the discharging process as an example, the solid S8 ring is con­
verted into liquid lithium polysulfides (LPSs) Li2S8, long-chain LPSs 
(Li2Sn, 4 < n < 7), short-chain LPSs (Li2Sn, 2 < n ≤4), and solid Li2S2/ 
Li2S in sequence [11,12]. In particular, driven by the concentration 
gradient, the soluble long-chain LPSs migrate from the sulfur cathode to 
the Li metal anode, where they are (electro)chemically reduced to short- 
chain LPSs. These short-chain LPSs move back to the cathode driven by 
the electric field, which is called the “shuttle effect.” This parasitic re­
action of dissolved LPSs will lead to severe overcharge during the 
charging process, resulting in low Coulombic efficiency and poor cycling 
life [13–18]. In addition, other challenges, such as the huge volume 
change (~80 %) caused by the conversion reaction between S to Li2S 
[19], the low electronic/ionic conductivity of sulfur and Li2S [20], and 
the safety issues caused by Li metal anodes, severely hinder the practical 
application of conventional liquid lithium-sulfur batteries [21,22]. 
These problems are expected to be alleviated or eliminated by replacing 
liquid electrolytes with SSEs [23–28]. Nowadays, SSEs mainly fall into 
three classes: polymer-based electrolytes (including gel polymer elec­
trolytes (GPEs) and solid polymer electrolytes (SPEs))[29–31], ceramic 
electrolytes (mainly oxide/sulfide-based electrolytes)[32–35], and 
composite polymer electrolytes (CPEs)[31,36–40]. Among them, the 
sulfide SSE has advantages in terms of high ionic conductivity, complete 
elimination of the “shuttle effect,” and good chemical compatibility with 
sulphur [41–50]. It is regarded as the most promising SSE candidate for 
application in ASSLSBs. 
Recently, the applications of various sulfide SSEs, such as glasses 
[51–55], glass ceramics [56–58], and crystals [59–63] in ASSLSBs, have 
been reported. These studies mostly focus on modifying the sulfide SSEs 
synthesis method [64–67] and improving the ionic conductivity and 
(electro)chemical stability [68–74]. Intensive efforts have also been 
made to develop high-performance sulfide-based ASSLSBs cathode

Up to now, most of the reported research on ASSLSB is on a lab-level

electrodes films in ASSLSBs cannot be simply replacing the layered oxide

go for the practical fabrication of ASSLSBs composite electrodes/SSE 
films. 
Due to the lack of liquid electrolytes to buffer the internal stress 
caused by the volume changes of the active materials during the battery 
cycling, the mechanical failure problems such as active material cracks, 
contact loss/voids formation, and SSE layer fracture caused by the 
electrochemo-mechanical effect in the ASSBs are more serious 
[98–101]. At present, studies on the electrochemo-mechanical effects 
are mainly focused on layered oxide ASSBs and are often overlooked in 
sulfide-based ASSLSBs [102–106]. Since the volume changes caused by 
the conversion-type sulfur active materials are much larger than those of 
the Li-intercalation layered oxide materials, the electrochemo- 
mechanical effect will be more severe in ASSLSBs [107,108]. Thus, 
more investigations should be carried out to suppress the electrochemo- 
mechanical effects in ASSLSBs, which will help to improve their elec­
trochemical performance. 
In this review, the latest research progress on sulfide-based ASSLSBs 
is firstly described from the materials perspective of SSEs, sulfur-based 
cathode materials, and anode materials. Subsequently, for practical 
purposes, the preparation methods (wet or dry method) of large-scale, 
high-areal capacity composite electrodes and robust, high-conductivity 
thin SSE films are summarized and discussed. In the last part, the 
overlooked electrochemo-mechanical coupling effects in sulfide-based 
ASSLSBs are discussed (Fig. 1).

Energy, etc., are committed to the development of sulfide SSEs to realize

2.2. Chemical and electrochemical stability 
Although sulfide SSEs possess high ionic conductivity and easy 
processing, their chemical and electrochemical instability, including 
moisture sensitivity, solvent reactivity, narrow electrochemical window, 
and redox-active degradation, hinder the practical application of 
sulfide-based ASSBs. 
2.2.1. Chemical stability 
2.2.1.1. Air instability. Most sulfide SSEs are prone to spontaneous hy­
drolysis reactions with moisture in the air, generating toxic H2S gas, 
which causes a structural collapse and decreases ionic conductivity 
[112,139]. Therefore, the operation process must be carried out in an 
inert environment, which increases the manufacturing costs and further 
impedes commercial application. Many researchers are committed to 
explaining the mechanism of H2S gas evolution and improving chemical 
stability. Sahu et al. proposed the Hard and Soft Acid and Bases theory 
(HSAB) to explain the hydrolysis reaction, which is attributed to the fact 
that S ions in sulfide are easily replaced by hard base O in H2O by 
reacting preferentially with the hard acid P [140]. That means the key to 
improving air stability is to avoid contact between S and O as much as 
possible. Based on HSAB theory, hard acids are prone to react with hard 
bases, while soft acids react more readily with soft bases. Hence, 
elemental doping is an effective strategy to suppress the hydrolysis re­
action by adding soft acids Sn, Ge, and Sb to form stable compounds with 
soft base S, thereby preventing the substitution of S from O [141–144]. 
In addition, adding metal oxides with large negative Gibbs free energy as 
the adsorbent for inhibiting H2S release is another strategy to improve 
air stability. Hayashi et al. demonstrated that the metal oxides (Fe2O3, 
ZnO, and Bi2O3) are easy to react with H2S (MxOy + H2S → MxSy + H2O), 
thereby reducing the H2S generation in sulfide SSE [145,146]. Xu et al. 
reported that sulfide SSE membranes with F-POS@LATP coating layer 
can withstand water exposure conditions and maintain structural and 
functional stability, which provides a new approach to achieve the 
water-stable sulfide SSEs for large-scale production of ASSBs [147]. In 
conclusion, addressing the acute degradation of sulfides in an open 
environment helps to improve and broaden the way for the industrial 
manufacture of SSEs. 
2.2.1.2. Solvent instability. Besides the air stability, the solvent stability 
of sulfide SSEs is another important property when SSEs are prepared by 
the liquid method (details in section 2.4). However, the chemical 
sensitivity of sulfide SSEs to common organic solvents results in the 
destruction of structure and a dramatic decrease in ionic conductivity 
[45]. Tan et al. investigated the chemical compatibility between 
Li7P3S11 and common solvents [148]. According to their research, as the 
polarity indexes and dielectric constants of solvents increased, stronger 
chemical interactions between sulfide SSE and solvent molecules were 
observed. The chemical structure and intrinsic ionic conductivity of the 
sulfide SSEs are preserved due to the suppressed hydrolysis of PSx spe­
cies when using low dielectric constant non-polar solvents such as 
toluene and p-xylene. Although significant progress has been made in 
the stability of sulfide electrodes in organic solvents, for instance, 
acceptable chemical stability can be achieved between sulfide SSEs and 
non-polar solvents, a better understanding of the stabilization mecha­
nism of sulfide SSEs against solvents is still essential [149]. 
2.2.2. Electrochemical stability 
2.2.2.1. Electrochemical window. The electrochemical window (EW) of 
SSEs is a stabilized voltage range beyond which SSEs will decompose to 
form an interfacial layer that deteriorates battery performance. Through 
the density functional theory (DFT) simulation and experimental mea­
surement for decomposition composition, it is determined that sulfide 
SSEs have a narrower EW than other materials, as shown in Fig. 4a 
[150]. For example, the EW of LGPS is 1.7–2.1 V (vs Li+/Li), which will 
be oxidized to Li3PS4, S, and GeS2 over 2.1 V and reduced to Li4GeS4, P, 
and Li2S below 1.7 V [151]. Other sulfide materials, such as 
Li3.25Ge0.25P0.75S4, Li3PS4, Li6PS5Cl, and Li7P2S8I, are reduced at a 
similar voltage of ~ 1.6–1.7 V [152]. In addition, the Li7P3S11 is first 
reduced to Li3PS4 at a higher voltage of 2.28 V, and then it is further 
reduced at 1.71 V [153]. Furthermore, the intrinsic EWs of the solid 
electrolytes are measured by cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurement 
utilizing a Li/electrolyte/electrolyte-carbon cell that increases the con­
tact area and enhances the reaction kinetics [154]. However, the actual 
stable EWs of sulfide SSEs are much wider than that speculated from 
stable decomposition products because they are based only on thermo­
dynamic evaluation and ignore the kinetic barriers. Recently, Schwietert 
et al. proposed an indirect decomposition mechanism in which the 
preferential decomposition pathway of the Li6PS5Cl solid SSEs is that 
reduction of phosphorus and oxidation of sulfur form metastable inter­
mediate, not directly into stable decomposition products [155]. As 
shown in Fig. 4b, they demonstrate the redox behaviors of solid SSE 
before decomposing into more stable products, indicating the redox 
activity of SSEs, that is, delithiated potentials, determines the electro­
chemical stability window rather than the stable decomposition prod­
ucts. This indirect decomposition pathway indicates that the EW of SSE 
determined by redox activity is winder than the most stable SSE 
decomposition products. Consequently, the key to further developing 
stable sulfide SSEs is to expand the delithiation oxidation and reduction 
potentials of SSEs. 
2.2.2.2. Electrolyte degradation. Electrochemical decomposition prod­
ucts related to the electrochemical stability window will cause contact 
loss and increase the internal interface resistance, resulting in capacity 
degradation and cyclic performance decay [49,79,156,157]. The ionic 
and electronic conductivity of composite cathodes is vital for the elec­
trochemical performance of ASSBs. The mixture of highly conductive 
carbon and SSEs constructs the essential electronic and ionic conductive 
pathway in the cathode for ASSBs. However, the intimate contact be­
tween conductive carbon and sulfide SSE promotes the decomposition of 
SSE and forms less-conductive interphase. As shown in Fig. 4c, Swamy 
et al. found that sulfide SSEs undergo irreversible reactions to form 
interphase at high potentials, while the reduction reactions at low po­
tentials are partially reversible [154]. The available capacity from the 
reversible redox reaction may increase the battery capacity, but the 
degradation behavior also deteriorates Li-ion transport and cyclic per­
formance [158]. Meng’s group identified the reversible decomposition 
redox of Li6PS5Cl in Li-In | Li6PS5Cl | Li6PS5Cl-C cell at various voltages 
[159]. As shown in Fig. 4d, the redox reaction of sulfide electrolyte 
decomposition will undergo an intermediate transition process during 
charging and discharging. However, the utilization of the decomposition 
product of the SSE as cathode material is a promising approach to 
improving the available capacity of the cathode [160]. Recently, Wang

2.3. Synthesis method and processing 
The main synthesis methods for sulfide SSEs are divided into three 
types in Fig. 5, including solid-state reaction, mechanochemical method, 
and liquid-phase synthesis [118]. The solid-state reaction is a conven­
tional synthesis method for sulfide SSEs. Due to the high vapor pressure 
of common sulfide precursors such as P2S5, the solid-state reaction is 
carried out via sealing techniques which involve compressing the pre­
cursor material mixture into pellets in a stoichiometric ratio and filling 
them into a quartz ampoule sealed under vacuum. The next step is 
followed by melt quenching, which involves heat treating to the melting 
point (≈500-900 ◦C) and cooling to room temperature, either slow 
cooling (to ensure crystallization) or quenching in ice water (to obtain 
amorphous material). These requirements for high temperature and 
vacuum conditions have become critical issues that hinder the scale-up 
of solid-state reaction methods. The LGPS-based and Li6PS5X SSEs are 
synthesized by solid-state reaction from stoichiometric precursors 
[60,162]. 
Mechanochemical is a common method to synthesize sulfide SSEs, 
which are only involved in mechanical ball-milling processes at room 
temperature. The products of ball milling are affected by various oper­
ating parameters, such as the size and material of the milling media, the 
powder filling ratio, and the milling cycling program [163]. The suitable 
selection of operation parameters for setting defined conditions enables 
control of the process and helps reach the desired yield. In addition, the 
mechanochemical method enables the formation of unique compounds 
that cannot be obtained by classic solid-state synthesis. More impor­
tantly, amorphous SSEs powders can be prepared by mechanical ball- 
milling at low reaction temperatures [65]. Since the mixing and pul­
verization processes occur simultaneously during the ball-milling pro­
cess, it is convenient to prepare well-mixed cathode materials for 
ASSLSBs [118,164]. 
By combining mechanical milling with annealing, the region of sul­
fide SSEs is expanded to glass–ceramic and crystals, which possess su­
perior ionic conductivity. Liu et al. reported a Li5.6PS4.6I1.4 
glass–ceramic SSEs synthesized by the ultimate-energy mechanical 
alloying method, which exhibited a faster synthesis process than earlier 
reported methods [165]. Yu et al. investigated the effects of mechanical 
milling conditions and subsequent heat treatment processes on SSE

Recently, liquid-phase syntheses have received extensive attention 
for the fabrication of sulfide SSEs due to cost-effective procedures, 
shorted-synthesis time, and large-scale production. Sulfide SSEs with the 
Li2S-P2S5 system have been synthesized by a liquid-phase method in 
which Li2S and P2S5 are suspended in stable organic liquid solvents with 
low boiling points to form complexes, and the products are obtained 
through heating and evaporating the solvents [46]. In 2013, Liu et al. 
reported a liquid-phase reaction between Li2S and P2S5 using tetrahy­
drofuran (THF) as the solvent to form nanoporous β-Li3PS4[64]. Since 
then, a series of sulfide SSEs, such as Li7P3S11 and Li4SnS4, have been 
prepared by this liquid-phase chemical reaction with Li2S-P2S5 pre­
cursors or other substitutive components [167,168]. The argyrodite- 
type SSEs have been prepared by the liquid-phase method to improve 
dispersion uniformity. Park et al. developed a novel pathway to prepare 
Li6PS5Cl SSE with high ionic conductivity of ≈1.84 mS cm−1 [169]. In 
the liquid phase preparation process, the insoluble Li2S is converted into 
lithium polysulfide by adding elemental sulfur, making it easy to react 
with P2S5 to form Li6PS5Cl. Due to the formation of the impurity phase in 
solvents, the conductivity of SSEs synthesized through the liquid-phase 
method is usually lower than that obtained from a high-energy me­
chanical ball-milling method. 
In addition to the suspension synthesis of sulfide SSEs from Li2S and 
P2S5 in organic solvents, the dissolution-precipitation method in the 
solvent is a suitable method for preparing SSEs with regular particle size 
distribution [118]. Navarro et al. reported the synthesis of Li6PS5Cl SSE 
particles with a regular distribution on a nanometric scale through a 
dissolution-precipitation method [170]. In their report, Li6PS5Cl SSE 
was firstly prepared by mechanical milling process. These synthesized 
Li6PS5Cl SSE powders were then dissolved in a mixture of ethanol and 
acetonitrile solution containing dispersant. After a heating process at 
180 ℃, regular distribution of the SSE particles on nanometric scale with 
spherical shape was obtained. After the dissolution-precipitation pro­
cess, Li6PS5Cl SSE exhibited a higher ionic conductivity of 6 × 10-4 S 
cm−1 than that prepared by the mechanical milling process (4 × 10-5 S 
cm−1). Besides, a combined approach of suspension synthesis and sub­
sequent dissolution precipitation was proposed for the SSE synthesis 
[46]. Despite considerable progress in the liquid-phase synthesis 
method, the relationship between the reaction mechanism of precursors 
and the properties of sulfide SSEs needs to be further elucidated. 
In summary, it is difficult to conclude which synthesis method is 
more advantageous than the others. A high temperature is necessary to 
enhance the reaction dynamics of solid-state reactions. Furthermore, 
solid-state synthesis is not a convenient method for large-scale produc­
tion, given the requirement for sealed containers and additional 
quenching processes [118]. For the mechanochemical method, the 
movement of the jar and balls transfers the kinetic energy to precursor 
particles, leading to collisions, grinding, and mechanochemical re­
actions [163]. Due to its reproducibility and homogeneity, the me­
chanical ball-milling method is considered reliable for scalable 
production. Generally, the sulfide electrolytes prepared by liquid-phase 
processes show low ionic conductivity due to residual solvents and 
amorphous phases precipitating at the interface [171]. Therefore, a 
fundamental understanding of the reaction mechanism of sulfide SSEs in 
liquid media is imperative and essential for further optimizing prepa­
rations of sulfide SSEs with high ionic conductivity. Hence, the utiliza­
tion of different synthesis methods needs to be determined by the 
property and application of the material. 
3. Sulfur-based cathodes 
Similar to the traditional liquid lithium-sulfur batteries, the cathode 
active materials of sulfide-based ASSLSBs mainly fall into four classes: 
sulfur materials, metal sulfide materials, organic sulfide materials, and 
lithium sulfide materials. 
3.1. Sulfur materials 
Due to its high theoretical specific capacity (1675 mAh g−1) and low 
cost, elemental sulfur is considered an ideal active material for lithium- 
sulfur batteries. In particular, the interface between sulfur and sulfide 
SSEs shows good chemical compatibility in sulfide-based ASSLSBs. 
Interestingly, sulfur materials were not used as the cathode active ma­
terial alone in the early research on sulfide-based ASSLSBs. In order to 
improve the electronic conductivity, metallic copper was added to the 
composite cathode. Hayashi and co-workers found that the performance 
of the ASSLSBs varies greatly when S/Cu composite cathodes with 
different molar ratios are mechanically ball-milled for different times 
[172]. The battery with the cathode material of S/Cu = 3 prepared by 
milling for 15 min exhibited the best electrochemical performance, with 
a discharge capacity over 650 mAh g−1 for 20 cycles (Fig. 6a). XRD 
results showed that CuS is formed during the ball milling process and 
could also be utilized as active material. 
Subsequently, the work of ASSLSBs with the element sulfur as 
cathode active materials has been reported [58,77,78,166,173–188]. A 
breakthrough work was reported by Yao et al. in 2017[189]. In this 
work, the amorphous sulfur was firstly coated on electronic conductive 
rGO. Then the rGO@S composites were uniformly distributed into LGPS 
to achieve high ionic/electronic conductivities and reduced Li-ion 
diffusion length and stress/strain in the composite cathode (Fig. 6b). 
The reported ASSLSBs exhibited a high initial discharge capacity of 1629 
mAh g−1 at a current density of 0.05C at 60 ◦C. With increasing current 
densities to 0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, and 5.0C, the battery could deliver 
discharge capacities of 1384.5, 1336.3, 903.2, 502.6, and 204.7 mA g−1 
(Fig. 6c). A high and reversible capacity of 830 mAh g−1 at 1.0C could be 
maintained for 750cycles at 60 ◦C (Fig. 6d). 
Despite the high theoretical specific capacity, the element sulfur and 
its discharge-type product, lithium sulfide, exhibit poor electronic (5 ×
10-30 S cm−1 and ca. 10-13 S cm−1, respectively) and ionic conductivities 
[190–192]. Therefore, to improve the electrochemical performance of 
cathode active materials in ASSLSBs, a large number of conductive 
carbon materials and SSEs are usually added into the composite cathode, 
resulting in relatively low sulfur content in reported work. This will 
significantly reduce the energy density of the ASSLSBs. Over the past 
decade, researchers have made great efforts to improve the utilization 
efficiency of sulfur materials, which will be discussed in detail in the 
following sections. 
3.2. Metal sulfide materials 
Metal sulfide materials exhibit higher electronic conductivity and 
lithium-ion diffusion rates than sulfur materials. Based on intercalation 
or conversion mechanisms, metal sulfides can also be used as cathode 
active materials for ASSLSBs [77,107,191,193–202]. However, due to 
the molecular weight of metal sulfide being higher than that of 
elemental sulfur, the mass-energy density of a battery entirely composed 
of metal sulfide does not have an advantage, so researchers have put 
forward mixing the metal sulfide with S elemental in order to achieve a 
balance between the mass-energy density and electrochemical 
performance. 
Hosseini et al. investigated three different copper sulfide-sulfur- 
carbon (CuSS) as composite cathodes in sulfide-based ASSLSBs [194]. 
They found that the CuS/C ratio greatly influenced the redox properties 
of the composite cathode. According to their study, the best trade-off 
could be found with the CuS-rich cathode CuSS (2–1). The battery 
exhibited 1200 mAh g−1 and 1100 mAh g−1 at 20 and 40 mA g−1, 
respectively. Part of the intercalation-type metal sulfides can also be 
used as cathode active materials. Xu et al. reported an intercalation- 
conversion hybrid cathode that combines intercalation-type VS2 with 
conversion-type sulfur to construct a high-performance sulfide-based

Many metal sulfide materials with poor performance in liquid bat­

batteries as the “shuttle effect” is eliminated. Kim et al. compared the 
electrochemical reaction differences between SnS materials in solid and 
liquid batteries (Fig. 6e)[200]. As shown in Fig. 6f, the SnS-based solid-

the solid-state cell, although crystallinity is not preserved in the con­

prevent the degradation of the SnS active material and thus realize a 
reversible conversion reaction. 
3.3. Organic sulfur materials 
Organic sulfur materials are mainly composed of organic units and 
sulfur chains, and the sulfur atoms are covalently bonded with the 
organic skeleton. This structure contains uniformly dispersed sulfur, 
which can avoid sulfur agglomeration and improve the utilization effi­
ciency of sulfur. Besides, the organic skeleton can effectively alleviate 
the volume expansion effect during the charging and discharging pro­
cess [76,203–205]. 
Sun et al. prepared a dense composite sulfur carbon (S/C) cathodes 
featuring sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) supported by a macro­
porous carbon (MaPC) conductive matrix (SPAN@MaPC) (Fig. 6g) 
[206]. The assembled cells with ~ 1 mg cm−2 sulfur loading delivered a 
high reversible capacity of 1396.2 mAh g−1 at 0.1C and maintained a 
capacity of 715.5 mAh g−1 after 200 cycles (Fig. 6h). Besides, the author 
detected the peak of the C-Li bond through X-ray photoelectron spec­
troscopy (XPS) analysis of the discharge product and found that the 
irreversible C-Li bonding contributes partially to the super-capacity that 
exceeds the theoretical values. 
In addition to SPAN materials, more and more organic sulfur cathode 
materials have been developed and applied to sulfide-based ASSLSBs. 
Takeuchi et al. reported on sulfurized alcohol composite (SAC) material 
and its application to ASSLSBs [207]. The battery exhibited a high 
specific capacity (600–800 mAh g−1) and improved initial coulombic 
efficiency (ICE). Their further research showed that the improvement in 
ICE is attributed to partial lithiation of the SAC cathode during the ball- 
milling process with sulfide SSE, which is confirmed by S K-edge X-ray 
absorption fine structure measurements. Yang et al. reported the first 
organodisulfide cathode, poly (trithiocyanuric acid) (PTTCA), for 
ASSLSBs [208]. In their work, PTTCA was composited with carbon 
nanotubes and sulfide electrolyte (Li7P3S11) to enhance the electronic 
and ionic conductivity. The battery with PTTCA demonstrated a 
reversible capacity of 410 mAh g−1, an energy density of 767 Wh kg−1, 
and a capacity retention of 83 % after 100 cycles.

3.4. Lithium sulfide material 
Compared with other cathode active materials, lithium sulfide has 
many irreplaceable advantages as the fully discharged product of sulfur. 
First of all, the volume change effect could be alleviated while lithium 
sulfide is used as the cathode material since Li2S is already the least 
dense phase with the lithium incorporated and will not expand during 
cell operation [192,209–212]. In addition, lithium sulfide material can 
be matched with the lithium-free anode to improve the energy density of 
the battery [192,211,213,214]. However, the low electrical conductiv­
ity and high activation energy barrier of lithium sulfide still remain the 
challenges for its application in sulfide-based ASSLSBs. To address the 
above technique challenges, novel composite cathodes comprised of 
nanosized Li2S uniformly dispersed in an electronic/ionic conductive 
network (carbon/sulfide electrolyte) have been explored. Han et al. 
synthesized a mixed conducting Li2S nanocomposite with nanosized Li2S 
and Li6PS5Cl uniformly distributed in the carbon matrix via a copreci­
pitation and high-temperature carbonization process (Fig. 6i)[215]. The 
obtained nanocomposite exhibited superior electrochemical perfor­
mance due to the nanoscale and homogeneous distribution of the Li2S, 
Li6PS5Cl, and carbon. The assembled battery delivers a high reversible 
capacity of 830 mAh g−1 with a Li2S loading of ~3.6 mg cm−2 at 0.18 
mA cm−2 for 60 cycles at RT. 
Lithium sulfide cathode materials can also achieve high surface 
loading, specific surface capacity, and good capacity retention. Yan et al. 
reported an in-situ generated Li2S@C nanocomposite by combusting 
lithium metal and CS2 (Fig. 6j)[216]. Through transmission electron 
microscope (TEM) characterization, it was found that Li2S nanocrystals 
were uniformly embedded in the conductive carbon matrix. Benefiting 
from its unique architecture, the Li2S@C nanocomposite cathode 
exhibited excellent electrochemical performance with high capacity, as 
well as outstanding rate capability and cycling stability. The battery 
delivered a high reversible capacity of 1186 mAh g−1 at 0.2 mA cm−2 
with a Li2S loading of 1.75 mg cm−2. It also shows a high reversible 
capacity of 692 mAh g−1 and superior long-term cycling performance 
with a high capacity retention of 93 % after 700 cycles, even at a high 
current density of 2 mA cm−2. Moreover, ultrahigh areal Li2S loading (7 
mg cm−2) and high Li2S utilization of 91 % (corresponding to a 
reversible capacity of 1067 mAh g−1) can be achieved simultaneously. 
Wang et al. reported nitrogen-doped-carbon (NC) coated Li2S (Li2S@NC 
composite) by pyrolysis deposition approach (Fig. 6k)[217]. The as- 
obtained Li2S@NC composite exhibited enhanced electrochemical per­
formance with high reversible capacity, high coulombic efficiency of ~ 
100 %, good rate capability, and cycling stability. In this work, 
outstanding electrochemical performance with a high capacity of 1052 
mAh g−1 and 91 % capacity retention after 50 cycles can be obtained at 
both high Li2S content (43 %) and high areal Li2S loading (~8.2 mg 
cm−2). Besides, it was found that the Li2S@NC battery exhibited a much 
lower activation barrier (only about 0.15 V) through the CV test, proving 
that the N-doped carbon coating could promote Li+ transmission and 
increase the redox reaction. 
3.5. Optimization of composite cathode 
3.5.1. Optimization of composite cathode components 
The technical data on cathode active materials content, loading, and 
areal capacity in the current study are summarized in Table 1. It can be 
seen that most of the studies only used low active materials loading and 
areal capacity. Taking sulfur active materials as an example, to make the 
ASSLSBs practical, the sulfur loading and areal capacity need to be>4.5 
mg cm−2 and 5 mAh cm−2 if the specific energy of battery is set at about 
450 Wh kg−1, respectively [2]. Considering that sulfur itself is elec­
tronic/ionic insulated, as mentioned earlier, a large amount of SSE and 
conductive carbon materials are often added to the composite cathode. 
Therefore, rationally optimizing the composite cathode components, 
especially the ratio of conductive carbon and solid electrolytes in the 
composite electrode, is necessary to achieve high sulfur content and high 
areal capacity. 
Quantitative analysis of the ionic and electronic conductivity of the 
composite cathode and figuring out how it affects the electrochemical 
performance of the battery is the most critical step in the rational design 
of composite cathodes. Dewald et al. quantified the impact on the 
transport within a composite cathode and analyzed the effective trans­
port properties as a function of the tortuosity factor and the corre­
sponding volume fraction by systematically altering volume fractions of 
SSE and carbon in the composite cathode (Fig. 7a)[218]. By relating the 
effective conductivities of sulfur-based cathodes with their cycling per­
formance, they identify ionic transport as the rate-limiting transport 
process (Fig. 7b), which has important guiding significance for 
designing and optimizing composite cathode components. 
3.5.2. Optimization of mixing method 
Due to the lack of liquid electrolytes which could keep flexible 
contact between electrode and electrolyte and in order to ensure the 
formation of good ion and electron pathways, the composite cathode 
materials of sulfide-based ASSLSBs are usually formed by mixing active 
materials, SSEs, and conductive carbon materials. Constructing superior 
contact between active materials/SSEs/conductive carbon materials is 
the key to achieving satisfactory electrochemical performance of active 
materials [163,219–221]. 
Similar to the liquid lithium-sulfur batteries, achieving uniform 
sulfur distribution in sulfur-carbon composites also significantly affects 
the performance of sulfide-based ASSLSBs. Hou et al. compared the ef­
fects of routine point-to-point (CNT/S) interfaces and unique surface-to- 
surface (CNT@S) interfaces on the performance of ASSLSBs [78]. It is 
found that the battery with CNT@S cathode demonstrates a higher 
sulfur utilization, delivering a high initial discharge capacity of 1138.7 
mAh g−1 at 0.1C and large capacity retention of 87.7 % after 200 cycles. 
Alzahrani et al. synthesized sulfur-carbon composites by sulfur vapor 
deposition (SVD) method [179]. In this method, sulfur is first evapo­
rated under low pressure and then condensed and deposited inside 
porous carbon (Fig. 7c). Thus, a complete impregnation of sulfur within 
porous carbon was achieved. Compared with conventional sulfur liquid 
deposition (SLD) and sulfur solid deposition (SSD) approaches, a higher 
discharge capacity of 1792.6 mAh g−1 at 0.1C and 60 ◦C with a capacity 
retention of 92 % after 100 cycles has been achieved (Fig. 7d). 
Besides, the mixing method of composite cathodes (mechanical ball 
milling or hand-grinding) also significantly impacts the electrochemical 
performance of ASSLSBs. Zeier and co-workers studied the composite 
cathode materials prepared by two methods: hand grinding and ball 
milling [222]. It is found that the initial discharge capacity of the battery 
with the hand-ground composite cathode material is only 220 mAh gsulfur
- 
1, and only half capacity (99 mAh gsulfur
-1
) is recovered in the subsequent 
charging process, representing a significant capacity loss (Fig. 7e). In 
contrast, the battery with the ball-milling composite cathode material 
exhibited > 1000 mAh gsulfur
-1 
and recovered the same capacity in the 
following charging process (Fig. 7f). 
3.5.3. Optimization of conductive carbon materials 
In traditional liquid lithium-sulfur batteries, the conductive carbon 
material provides the electron transport path, and the sulfur material is 
often confined in the carbon material to alleviate the volume change 
effect in the charging and discharging process [223–226]. In sulfide- 
based ASSLSBs, due to the lack of infiltrating liquid electrolytes, 
which results in difficult ion transport within the composite cathode, the 
traditional selection principle of carbon materials in the liquid lithium- 
sulfur batteries may not be fully applicable. Therefore, it is necessary to 
consider the selection of carbon materials under “solid–solid” contact 
conditions in ASSLSBs. In addition, conductive carbon has been proven

4. Anode 
In order to meet the demand for high energy–density ASSLSBs, the 
anode side needs to satisfy the following requirements: (i) chemical/ 
electrochemical stability with the sulfide SSEs; (ii) high specific capac­
ity; (iii) low volume change during cycling. It should be noted that the 
anode must be Li-contained when using the S-based cathode, which 
implies that conventional graphite, silicon, and other Li-free materials 
are not suitable in delithiated-type cathodes-based ASSLSBs. Lithium is 
the ideal anode material for ASSLSBs due to the ultra-high specific ca­
pacity of over 3800 mAh/g. However, the chemical/electrochemical 
instability, mechanical instability of Li/SSEs interfaces, and dendritic 
problems haunt all the researchers. The practical application of Li metal 
still needs great efforts to solve the scientific and engineering challenges. 
Herein, we focus on the Li and Li-based anode materials, the challenges 
of Li/SSEs interfaces, and strategies of interfacial modification, with the 
purpose of providing guidance for the development of high energy 
density ASSLSBs. 
4.1. Lithium metal anode 
Lithium metal is considered the “holy grail” of lithium batteries’ 
anode materials due to its low density (0.59 g/cm3), lowest potential 
(-3.04 V vs standard hydrogen electrode (SHE)), and ultra-high theo­
retical specific capacity (3860 mAh/g). However, the high reactivity of 
lithium metal and the “host-less” properties severely hinder its appli­
cation in all-solid-state lithium metal batteries (ASSLMBs)[235]. It is 
well known that the electrochemical stability of an electrolyte is usually 
measured by the electrochemical stability window. When the chemical 
potential μ of the Li anode is higher than its lowest unoccupied molec­
ular orbital (LUMO) or conducting band of the solid electrolytes, the 
interface is thermodynamically unstable and will react spontaneously 
with the Li metal once the electrolyte contacts the Li metal. For most 
sulfide and thiophosphate electrolytes, the electrochemical stability 
window is between 1.7 ~ 2.3 V [117,236]. Due to their weak P–S and 
M−S bonds (M = Si, Sn, Ge, Al), reactions with Li metal to form Li2S, 
Li3P, Li-M alloys (M = Si, Sn, Ge, Al) and LiX (X = Cl, Br, I)[237,238] 
occurred, resulting in dramatic increases in polarization and interfacial 
impedance. The “host-less” nature of Li allows it to be deposited at 
arbitrary sites on the surface of electrode, and the relative volume 
change of Li anode is virtually infinite [235]. The initial defects, voids, 
pores, and cracks and protrusions on the surface of the Li/SSEs cause 
uneven current distribution. This resulted local deposition of Li leads to 
a protrusion formation and “tip effect”, which suggests that the more 
projecting the part of the surface, the more the surface field intensity 
distributed, which can easily absorb Li-ions [239]. The “tip effect” ex­
acerbates the uneven electric field distribution and further accelerates 
dendrite formation. It is hoped that solid electrolytes with high Young’s 
modulus would effectively inhibit the growth and penetration of lithium 
dendrites. However, Li dendrites are found more easily formed at the 
defects of the Li/SSEs interface and grow in the direction of the pores 
and grain boundaries even at low current density, rupturing the SSEs in 
ASSLMBs [240,241]. Moreover, the dendrites formation is closely 
related to the mechanical and electrochemical properties of Li metal. 
The “creep” property of Li metal is one of the governing factors of 
lithium deposition morphology in ASSLMBs, which depends on the 
stress condition, current density, temperature, and grain size [242]. 
4.1.1. Sulfide electrolyte/lithium metal interface 
The chemical/electrochemical and chemical–mechanical coupling 
instability of the interface between the sulfide SSEs and the highly 
reactive Li metal lead to problems, such as parasitic reactions and 
dendrite formation [149,155,159,243], resulting in rapid capacity 
degradation and cell failure. These problems severely limit the appli­
cation of sulfide electrolytes in ASSLMBs. 
The parasitic reactions mainly originated from the thermodynamic 
mismatch between Li metal and sulfide SSEs, manifested by a contin­
uous rise in interfacial impedance and polarization voltage. Based on the 
interfacial properties, solid electrolyte interfaces (SEI) between elec­
trolyte and electrode are usually classified into three types: thermody­
namically stable interfaces, mixed conductor interfaces, and self- 
limiting SEI [244]. As shown in Fig. 8a, In the case of a perfectly ther­
modynamically stable interface, the Li metal does not react with the 
electrolyte at all, allowing lithium ions to pass through but completely 
isolating electrons. However, no such interface has been reported to 
date. In the case of a mixed conductor interface shown in Fig. 8b, the 
sulfide SSEs react continuously once in contact with the Li metal due to

polymer composite SEI was constructed, significantly increasing the 
critical current density of LGPS-based lithium symmetric cell.

4.2. Lithium-based alloy anode 
It has been widely reported that lithium-based alloys serves as the 
protective layer to enhance the interfacial adhesion and guide uniform 
lithium plating/stripping during cycling [261–268]. However, despite 
the high specific capacity, there are still scientific and engineering 
challenges when lithium-based alloys are used as anode materials for 
ASSLSBs. 
Since the Li-In alloy has a high potential (~0.62 V vs Li/Li+) over a 
wide stoichiometry range, which is more compatible with sulfide SSEs, it 
is widely used as anode material in ASSLMBs with sulfide SSEs [86]. 
Park et al. reported that the In layer is free from dendritic growth and 
facilitates the formation of a stable interphase with sulfide SE, enabling 
long-term cycling without cell failure [275]. However, Luo et al. re­
ported the growth of Li-In dendrites in sulfide electrolytes at high cur­
rent density and high area capacity [88]. It was found that the 
accompanying volume change and slight interfacial reaction induced 
the growth of Li-In dendrites enclosing electrolyte particles, eventually 
leading to short circuits and cell failure after a long cycle. Moreover, the 
high molar mass of the Li-In alloy greatly increases the weight of the 
anode, limiting its application in high-energy–density ASSLMBs. 
The moderate potential (0.3 ~ 0.4 V vs Li/Li+) and low molar mass of 
Li-Al alloy can effectively inhibit the growth of lithium dendrite, 
improve the stability of the Li/SEs interfaces, and be capable of realizing 
high energy density batteries [276]. Compared with Li-Si alloy (320 %) 
and Li-Sn alloy (260 %), the volume change of Li-Al alloy is much 
smaller (about 96 %) during the lithiated/delithiated process [277]. 
Thus, the Li-Al alloy is considered a promising candidate anode for 
sulfide-based ASSLMBs [89,278–280]. Pan et al. designed an ASSLSB 
with the application of Li0.8Al alloy anode, S@CNTs cathode, and LGPS 
electrolyte [85]. As shown in Fig. 10a, the Li0.8Al alloy anode exhibited a 
specific capacity of 793 mAh/g and an operating potential of 0.35 V vs 
Li/Li+. Fig. 10b shows the electrochemical performance of Li0.8Al-LGPS-

Silicon, another well-known anode material with a high theoretical

4.3. Li2S-based anode-free Li-S batteries 
Anode-free batteries (AFBs), which are assembled with the cathode 
in the lithiated state with a bare current collector (CC), have attracted 
widespread attention in recent years due to several advantages in both 
cell structure and production process [285]. First, the absence of the 
anode can reduce the weight and thickness, which can increase the 
energy density of a full cell. Second, anode-free batteries can cut off the 
unnecessary anode production processes, such as the slurry preparation 
and coating of the conventional graphite anode or the challenging 
fabricating requirements of lithium thin film anode. AFBs with liquid 
electrolytes have been reported in several review articles [285–288]. To 
improve the cycling performance of AFBs, different strategies such as

5.1. Wet-coating techniques 
The main advantage of the wet-coating techniques is that it can 
obtain a uniformly mixed slurry with the assistance of solvent disper­
sion. Besides, this technique is similar to the current LIBs sheet-type film 
manufacturing process, making the production line easier to design and 
adjust [50,92,295–297]. At present, many research works have been 
reported on the preparation of electrode and SSE films in sulfide-based 
ASSBs using wet-coating techniques [96,97,110,298–310]. 
5.1.1. Electrode film 
The ASSLSB electrode wet-coating technique involves the mixing of 
the active materials, SSEs, and binders in a solvent to form a uniformly 
dispersed slurry [92]. Therefore, the primary premise is to ensure the 
structural and functional integrity of the active materials and the SSEs 
during the solution process [298]. While the chemical stability between 
sulfide SSEs and solvents of different polarities has been discussed in 
section 2.2.1.2, additional factors need to be considered in the sulfur 
electrode wet-coating process. Firstly, it is necessary to ensure that the 
sulfur can be uniformly dispersed in the solvent instead of dissolved. 
Besides, it is more important to ensure that the sulfide SSEs and sulfur do 
not react with each other to form polysulfides in the selected solvent. 
Therefore, the solvent selection criteria in the sulfur electrode wet- 
coating technique are more stringent than those in the liquid-phase 
synthesis of sulfide SSEs. 
Yuan et al. studied the effects of solvents with different polarities 
(tetrahydrofuran, chloroform, xylene, and n-hexane) and various sili­
cone rubber (SR) binder contents (2, 5, 10 wt%) on the electrochemical 
performance of sulfide-based ASSLSBs [298]. Although LGPS exhibits 
good chemical compatibility with all four solvents (undissolved into the 
above solvents), it is found that sulfur is dissolved into three other sol­
vents except for n-hexane. Furthermore, due to the relatively high 
dielectric constant, severe side reactions between LGPS and sulfur can be 
found in tetrahydrofuran solvent. Therefore, n-hexane is regarded as a 
target solvent for sulfur cathode slurry process. Since binders play a 
decisive role in the viscosity of the slurry and dispersion of the particles, 
it is necessary to ensure uniform dispersion and functional integrity of 
binders in the solvent which satisfies the above criteria. The authors 
chose SR binder in this work due to its excellent compatibility with n- 
hexane solvent. Then the sulfur cathode slurry was blade-coated on 
commercial aluminum foil to achieve a smooth and flexible electrode 
film (Fig. 11a). In addition, the authors further discuss the effect of the 
binder content on the electrochemical performance. When the binder 
content was 2 wt%, the assembled sulfide-based ASSLSB pouch battery 
exhibited an initial discharge capacity of 1169 mAh g−1 at 0.01C and 
high capacity retention of 950 mAh g−1 after 10 cycles (Fig. 11b). 
Although better battery performance can be obtained by rational 
selection of binder types and optimization of their content, the presence 
of non-conductive binders will affect the Li ions transport at the inter­
face. To address this problem, Jung et al. reported a novel Li+-conduc­
tive polymeric binder (NBR-Li(G3)TFSI)[97]. In this Li+-conductive 
polymeric binder, nitrile-butadiene rubber (NBR) is the polymer binder, 
Li(G3)TFSI (G3 is triethylene glycol dimethyl ether, and LiTFSI is 
lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide) is the solvent-salt complex 
to enhance Li ion transport at the interface. NBR-Li(G3)TFSI exhibits 
excellent chemical compatibility with sulfide SSEs and can be uniformly 
dispersed in low-polarity solvents with maintained structural stability. 
Therefore, it can facilitate Li+ transport at interfaces and, at the same 
time, ensure the mechanical strength of the electrode film (Fig. 11c). It 
can facilitate Li+ transport at interfaces and, at the same time, ensure the 
mechanical strength of the electrode film. 
Another method to reduce the contact resistance caused by the non- 
conductive binders and improve the charge transfer is to remove the 
binders through subsequent heat treatment after the slurry is coated on 
the current collector. Yamamoto et al. reported a binder-free electrode 
film by removing the poly (propylene carbonate) (PPC) binder with a 
vacuum heat treatment (225 ◦C) after slurry coating (Fig. 11d)[299]. 
Binder-free batteries exhibited higher capacity and better rate perfor­
mance than those with binders due to reduced internal resistance 
(Fig. 11e). 
5.1.2. SSE film 
In order to achieve high energy density and enable large-scale pro­
cessing in the practical application of ASSLSBs, a thin and flexible SSE 
film with high ionic conductivity must be prepared. The basic process of 
preparing SSE film by wet-coating techniques includes uniformly mixing 
SSEs and binders in solvent to form a slurry, then coating the slurry on a 
polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) release sheet, and obtaining free- 
standing SSE film after removing the solvent. 
Tan et al. reported the chemical compatibility between Li7P3S11 SSE 
and various solvents and polymers (in order to improve the mechanical 
flexibility)[148]. The chemical compatibility of Li7P3S11 SSE with 
different polar solvents is shown in Fig. 11f (top), indicated by the color 
change of the mixed solution. Their experiments showed that by using a 
low dielectric constant non-polar solvent (such as p-xylene) together 
with a polymer without electronegative functional groups, such as 
polystyrene-block-poly(ethylene-ran-butylene)-block-polystyrene 
(SEBS), a highly conductive (~0.7 mS cm−1) thin SSE film (~50 μm) 
with good mechanical properties can be fabricated (Fig. 11f bottom) 
[132]. Similar to the electrode film, binders in the free-standing SSE film 
greatly influence its performance. Riphaus et al. systematically investi­
gated various binders with regard to their impact on the processability, 
flexibility, density, and resistivity of the resulting SSE film [304]. Ac­
cording to their research, hydrogenated nitrile butadiene rubber 
(HNBR) showed the best results. Emley et al. reported a strong corre­
lation between the solid loading of SSE dispersions and the quality of 
sulfide SSE film [310]. As shown in Fig. 11g, the author discussed the 
quality of tape-cast films with different solid contents of SSE slurry by 
analyzing the tonal contrast of digital photos and quantifying them in 
histogram analysis (the larger the reciprocal of the standard deviation of 
the histogram, the fewer defects and the more uniform dispersion of the 
SSE films). The results showed that the SSE film exhibited the highest 
quality with a solid loading of 54 wt%. The fabricated LPSCl thin films 
are ~50 μm thick and highly flexible (Fig. 11h). 
Another challenge for SSE films is to reduce the thickness while 
maintaining good mechanical properties. Using a scaffold can alleviate 
this problem to some extent. Nam et al. report bendable and thin sulfide 
solid electrolyte films reinforced with a poly (paraphenylene tereph­
thalamide) nonwoven (NW) scaffold [96]. The battery using a thin (~70 
μm) NW-reinforced SE film exhibited a 3-fold increase in energy density 
compared to conventional batteries without NW (Fig. 11i). 
The preparation of electrodes and SSE membranes by wet-coating 
techniques provides the possibility for large-scale industrial produc­
tion of ASSLSBs. Inada et al. coated Li3.25Ge0.25P0.75S4 and 5 vol% sili­
cone binder slurry on Mo6S8 composite cathode (also prepared by wet- 
coating techniques) to construct an all-solid-state sheet battery with a 
dimension of 30 mm × 30 mm (Fig. 11j)[307]. The assembled battery 
showed good capacity retention at a current density of 0.1 mA. Although 
the direct coating of SSE slurry on the electrode is convenient and can 
obtain superior interfacial contact, it still suffers from precise thickness 
control and poor mechanical properties. 
5.2. Dry-film techniques 
Recently, dry-film techniques have shown great promising prospects 
in solid-state batteries. Compared with wet-coating techniques, dry-film 
techniques have the unique advantages of non-solvent processes, such as 
environmental friendliness and low cost. In addition, since there is no 
organic solvent used in dry-film techniques, the structure and high ionic 
conductivity of sulfide SSE can be maintained. Therefore, dry-film 
technique is especially suitable for the preparation of sulfide-based 
electrode/SSE films [90,91].

6. Electrochemo-mechanical effects in sulfur-based electrodes 
The electrochemo-mechanical issues in sulfide-based ASSBs are 
complex and multiscale [317–320]. The electrochemical processes are 
accompanied by corresponding volume changes at the particle scale of 
the active materials. In a restrained ASSB system, the volume changes of 
the active materials lead to changes in internal stress. Moreover, unlike 
liquid electrolytes, the rigid SSE in ASSBs cannot uniformly disperse and 
buffer the stress evolution. As a result, the stress generated inside the 
battery will accumulate, leading to a series of electrochemo-mechanical 
issues [321]. Particularly in ASSLSBs, the conversion reaction of CAMs 
will lead to huge volume changes (e.g., S + Li → Li2S and 2Li + CuS → 
Cu + Li2S result in 79 % and 75 % volume changes, respectively 
[107,108]), resulting in more obvious internal stress changes and more 
serious electrochemo-mechanical issues. Unlike the cracks that 
commonly occur in “brittle” CAMs (e.g., layered oxide cathodes), due to 
the mechanical parameters (elastic modulus, including Young’s 
modulus (E), shear modulus (G), bulk modulus (K), etc.) of S and its 
derivatives vary widely [322–324], CAM cracks are only found in some 
metal sulfide active materials with high Young’s modulus (E)[325,326]. 
Instead of CAM cracks, low Young’s modulus (E) sulfur electrodes 
exhibit contact loss at the active materials/SSEs/carbons triple-phase 
interface and electrode pulverization caused by large volume changes 
[189,327]. These electrochemo-mechanical issues ultimately lead to the 
mechanical deterioration and failure of the sulfide-based ASSLSBs. 
6.1. Cracking of metal sulfide materials 
Due to its high specific capacity and abundance, FeS2, a typical metal 
sulfide material, is considered a promising CAM [328,329]. As shown in 
Fig. 13a, similar to other conversion-type materials, FeS2 undergoes 
huge volume changes during the charging and discharging process 
(FeS2 → Li2S + Fe, the theoretical volume change is 159 %)[326]. In 
addition, FeS2 has similar mechanical parameters to layered oxide ma­
terials (Young’s modulus E = 192, 191, 199, 194 GPa, shear modulus G 
= 75, 80, 78, 77 GPa, and Poisson’s ratio ν = 0.28, 0.24, 0.25, 0.26 for 
FeS2, LiCoO2, LiNi0.33Co0.33Mn0.33O2, and LiMn2O4, respectively 
[322,330–332]). Therefore, cracks in FeS2 may occur during cycling. 
This possibility was confirmed in in-situ TEM characterization. As 
shown in Fig. 13b, once lithiated, the FeS2 nanorods began to expand. 
The dashed yellow line was indicated as the phase boundary between 
the pristine FeS2 and the reactive domain. After lithiation for 367 s, the 
FeS2 nanorod was not able to endure such a huge volume expansion and 
cracks formed at the bottom of the nanorods. Sun et al. reported FeS2 
cracks in a Li/Li7P3S11/FeS2 ASSLSB [325]. In ASSBs, in addition to the 
internal stress caused by the volume change of the active materials 
during the electrochemical process, the homogeneous transition of the 
conversion-type CAM has proven difficult, resulting in uneven stress and 
more severe cracks. As shown in Fig. 11c, the FeS2 particle exhibited 
significant volume expansion during the discharging process, resulting 
in FeS2 cracks. A schematic illustration of the cracks of cycled com­
mercial FeS2 in ASSLSBs is shown in Fig. 13d. Besides, contact loss be­
tween FeS2 and SSE was observed. Contact loss within the sulfur 
composite cathode will be discussed in more detail in the following 
section. 
6.2. Contact loss/pulverization of sulfur electrodes 
Electrochemo-mechanical issues with sulfur electrodes have been 
reported in liquid Li-S batteries [333,334]. In the low Young’s modulus 
(E) sulfur electrodes(12.8 GPa)[323], although no cracks occur in the 
sulfur active material due to its ductility, the electrochemical perfor­
mance is severely hindered by the degradation of the sulfur electrode 
structure caused by the huge volume changes and generation of soluble 
lithium polysulfides. Chinnam et al. reported the effect of different 
external pressures on the retention of the sulfur electrode structure 
before and after cycling in a liquid Li-S battery [335]. As shown in 
Fig. 13e (left), the sulfur electrode surface was smooth and without 
obvious cracks in the pristine battery, while in the sulfur electrode after 
120 cycles at low pressure (7.8 KPa) (Fig. 13e (right)), significant con­
tact loss and cracking were observed (highlighted in red circle). 
Although the structural degradation caused by soluble lithium poly­
sulfides is eliminated, the electrochemo-mechanical problems are more 
severe in volume-constrained ASSBs without liquid electrolytes to buffer 
the stress. Yamamoto et al. designed a nanoporous graphene (NPG) 
carbon material as a framework to buffer the volume changes of sulfur 
electrodes [327]. The designed NPG had a large pore volume (2.79 cm3 
g−1) and excellent mechanical toughness/flexibility with a bulk 
modulus of 0.79 GPa, allowing for reversible shape compression/re­
covery under large strain (88 %). Before and after cycling, a uniform 
distribution of elements was observed in the ASSLSBs with NPG. 
Compared with the NPG material, the sulfur active material in the 
composite cathode with activated carbon (MSC30) was found to be 
isolated as a bulk of a few hundred nanometers after cycling. This phase 
separation implied a contact loss between the sulfur active materials and 
the conductive materials in the composite sulfur cathode (Fig. 13f). Yao 
et al. designed a reduced graphene oxide (rGO)@S nanocomposite to 
improve the structural stability of sulfur cathodes by buffering the vol­
ume expansion with a low stress/strain rGO material [189]. The ASSLSB 
with rGO@S-40 cathode exhibited high capacity and long-cycle

7. Conclusions and perspectives 
With the rapid transition into a low-carbon society, new generations 
of battery technologies for electric vehicles and large-scale electrical 
energy storage applications are urgently needed. The ASSLSBs are one of 
the battery technologies gathering wide attention and providing the 
opportunity for achieving safety, high energy density, and long-term 
stability. However, due to existing challenges in processing highly 
conductive solid electrolyte materials, practical composite electrode/ 
SSE films techniques, and serious electrochemo-mechanical effects, 
much remains to be done prior to the practical application of ASSLSBs. 
Extensive research and developments on composite electrode/electro­
lyte materials, electrode/electrolyte film preparation, interfacial engi­

practical application requirements of ASSLSBs.  
(1) Materials & Interfaces 
Many sulfide-based SSEs with high ionic conductivity have been 
developed. However, in practical applications, the chemical instability 
under humid atmosphere obviously increases the industrial production 
cost of sulfide-based ASSLSBs, which becomes an urgent problem to be 
solved. Here, some coating techniques of sulfide electrolytes should be 
beneficial in enhancing stability. In addition, the large-scale production 
of sulfide SSEs to decrease their cost and composite cathodes with high 
areal active material loading and high areal capacity are necessary to 
make ASSLSBs practical. On the other hand, Li-In is widely used as an 
anode material in most reported sulfide-based ASSLSBs. The Li-In anode 
is chemically stable to the sulfide SSEs and its alloy characteristics can 
make the battery cycles well without distinctive short-circuiting at low 
current density. However, the Li-In anode is not a practical electrode 
material which is very expensive and its high working potential 
dramatically reduces the energy density of the battery. The Li metal 
anode may be the best choice for energy density consideration alone. 
However, the application of lithium metal anodes in sulfide-based 
ASSLSBs will suffer from severe short-circuit behaviors, especially in 
the case of high areal capacity and high current density. The develop­
ment of novel modification strategies and interfacial engineering for Li 
metal anodes to suppress the interfacial side reactions and inhibit Li- 
dendrite formation as well as the development some Li-alloy elec­
trodes, such as Li-B [336] or Li-Si alloys, is necessary for achieving long- 
term stability of SSLSBs with high energy density as well.  
(2) Scalable fabrication of composite electrodes/SSE membranes. 
The dry-film techniques avoid using organic solvents and are 
considered the most suitable for electrode/SSE film preparation in 
sulfide-based ASSLSBs. However, the application of dry-film techniques 
in sulfide-based ASSLSBs is still immature and needs further optimiza­
tion. Besides, achieving and controlling the consistency of the film 
thickness is still challenging, which is essential to the cycle stability and 
safety performance of the battery. The optimization of dry-film prepa­
ration procedures, such as the optimal type/amount of binders and the 
optimal temperature for fibrillization and following hot rolling pro­
cesses, still needs to be systematically investigated and optimized.  
(3) Electrochemo-Mechanical effects in sulfur-based electrodes. 
Although electrochemo-mechanical effects in layered oxide ASSBs 
have attracted increasing attentions in the last several years, few 
research works are reported on sulfide-based ASSLSBs. Recent studies 
have shown that the contact loss and voids inside the composite cathode 
are strongly affected by the mechanical properties of SSE (such as 
Young’s modulus, etc.) and volumetric changes of cathodes during 
charge/discharge cycles. Wang et al. demonstrated that ASSB with glass 
or glass–ceramic SSE could mitigate contact loss between CAM/SSE 
compared to crystalline SSE analogs [104]. Bucci et al. pointed out that 
while SSE with low Young’s modulus could better accommodate the 
contact loss caused by the volume change of CAMs, it may also be more 
prone to micro-cracks, leading to an increase in voids within the elec­
trode [337]. Therefore, the mechanical properties of each component 
need to be considered a critical design factor for sulfide-based ASSLSBs. 
In addition, the short circuit phenomenon in Li metal anode caused by 
SSE layer fracture has recently been reported in Li/Li symmetric batte­
ries. Due to the more complex internal stress coupling in sulfide-based 
ASSLSBs, worsened contact between electrode and electrolytes, non- 
uniform distribution of current density at interfaces and resulting 
short-circuit phenomenon which are all caused by electrochemo- 
mechanical effects will be a big challenge and require systematical 
investigation for an in-depth understanding of their impacts. 
Overall, unlike commercial liquid-type lithium-ion batteries, sulfide- 
based ASSLSBs is currently in the preliminary research stage. We believe 
that intensive research on basic scientific issues and fundamental 
application methods are urgently needed, which should make sulfide- 
based ASSLSBs feasible and practical in the future.
