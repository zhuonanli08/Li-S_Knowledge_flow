A B S T R A C T
All-solid-state lithium‑sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) have emerged as promising candidates for next-generation 
energy storage systems due to their high energy density, excellent safety, and low cost. However, the inher­
ently low electronic and ionic conductivity of sulfur, as well as inadequate solid-solid interfacial contact, leads to 
sluggish electrochemical reaction kinetics, severely impacting the electrochemical performance of ASSLSBs. To 
address these challenges, various modification strategies have been developed, focusing on improving charge 
carrier transport to enhance electrochemical activity and stability of the sulfur cathode. Here, we present a 
comprehensive review of the key electronic and ionic transport limitations in the sulfur cathode of ASSLSBs, 
along with the modification strategies and advanced characterization techniques. Finally, we highlight future 
research directions for the development of sulfur cathodes in realizing high-performance ASSLSBs.
1. Introduction
Lithium‑sulfur (Li −
S) batteries, owing to their exceptionally high 
energy density, abundant sulfur resource reserves, and low-cost raw 
materials, have emerged as one of the most promising candidates for 
next-generation high-energy storage systems [1]. Unlike conventional 
lithium-ion batteries that rely on the intercalation reaction mechanisms 
of metal oxide cathodes, where each transition metal ion typically fa­
cilitates the transfer of 0.5 to 1 electron, sulfur cathodes operate via a 
distinct two-electron conversion reaction (S8 + 16Li → 8Li2S) [2]. This 
reaction enables a theoretical specific capacity of 1675 mAh g−1, nearly 
7 times higher than that of traditional LiFePO4 cathode [3]. However, 
the commercialization of Li −
S batteries faces several significant chal­
lenges. In traditional liquid electrolyte systems, sulfur cathode un­
dergoes a multiphase redox transformation, progressing from solid 
sulfur to soluble lithium polysulfides (LiPSs, Li2Sn, 4 ≤n ≤8) in the 
liquid phase and eventually to solid lithium sulfide (Li2S2/Li2S) [4]. The 
high solubility of LiPSs in ether-based electrolytes induces a problematic 
shuttle effect that arises from the migration of LiPSs between the sulfur 
cathode and the lithium anode, driven by concentration gradients. The 
shuttle effect leads to the irreversible loss of active materials, degrada­
tion of the lithium anode, and severe self-discharge issues, all of which 
severely impair battery performance. Additionally, the use of flammable 
organic solvents in liquid electrolyte systems increases safety risks, 
posing further obstacles to the practical application of Li −
S batteries 
[5,6].
All-solid-state Li −
S batteries (ASSLSBs) based on sulfide solid 
electrolytes achieve a solid-solid conversion of S ↔ Li2S, fundamentally 
eliminating the shuttle effect caused by LiPSs [7]. Sulfide solid elec­
trolytes (SSEs) are widely recognized as one of the most promising 
candidate materials for ASSLSBs due to their exceptional advantages in 
ionic transport and interface stability [8,9]. The ability of Li+ to migrate 
between stable sites in the sulfide lattice enables these electrolytes to 
achieve ionic conductivities as high as 10−2 S cm−1. Additionally, SSEs 
exhibit outstanding mechanical strength and excellent chemical stabil­
ity, along with high chemical compatibility with active sulfur and its 
discharge product Li2S. Through process optimizations, such as cold- 
pressing, close contact can be established between the cathode mate­
rial and SSEs, forming a highly chemically compatible interface that 
enhances system stability and efficiency.
Despite these promising attributes, sulfide-based ASSLSBs face sig­
nificant scientific and technological challenges in practical applications, 
primarily concerning charge carrier transport dynamics [10]. The 
inherently low electronic/ionic conductivities of both S8 and Li2S

2. Fundamentals of ASSLSBs
ASSLSBs have garnered significant research interest due to their 
unique capability to mitigate the polysulfide shuttle effect and battery 
safety concerns. Table 1 summarizes the state-of-the-art electrochemical 
performance of sulfide-based ASSLSBs as reported in recent literature. 
Despite these encouraging developments, several fundamental chal­
lenges persist in achieving commercially viable ASSLSBs, including solid 
electrolytes, Li anode, and sulfur cathode.

on their structural characteristics: amorphous glassy, glass-ceramic, and 
crystal. The typical Li2S-P2S5 glassy electrolytes are prepared by me­
chanical ball milling or melt quenching methods, exhibiting local 
structural units such as PS4

glass-ceramic SSEs can reach the order of ~10−3 S cm−1. For exam–ple, 
the Li7P3S11 glass-ceramic sulfide obtained by calcining 70Li2S-30P2S5 
glassy sulfide at 360 ◦C for 2 h exhibits a high ionic conductivity of 3.2 ×
10−3 S cm−1 [37]. For crystalline SSEs requiring high-temperature sin­
tering (>400 ◦C), three major structural families dominate: Thio-

with the configuration of Li9.54[Si1−δMδ]1.74P1.44S11.1Br0.3O0.6 (M = Ge, 
Sn; 0 ≤δ ≦ 1) achieves exceptional ionic conductivity (3.2 × 10−2 S 
cm−1) [38]. However, SSEs suffer from chemical and electrochemical 
instability issues, which severely hinder their practical implementation. 
Most SSEs exhibit spontaneous hydrolysis reactions upon exposure to 
ambient moisture, resulting in structural collapse and severe ionic 
conductivity reduction [39]. Another critical characteristic is their sta­
bility in solvents, which is crucial during solution-processed of SSEs. 
Regrettably, the sensitivity of sulfide SSEs to common organic solvents 
induces irreversible structural damage and dramatic conductivity dete­
rioration [40]. The electrochemical stability window (ESW), a key 
parameter for evaluating SSEs stability, has been systematically char­
acterized through cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements using Li/ 
SSEs/SSEs‑carbon three-electrode cells. These studies consistently 
reveal a narrow ESW ranging from 1.7 to 2.1 V vs. Li+/Li [41], indi­
cating limited electrochemical stability. The electrochemical decompo­
sition behavior of SSEs leads to multiple detrimental effects, such as 
compromised interface contact, degraded electronic/ionic transport ki­
netics, and deteriorated battery capacity and cycling performance.

the practical application of ASSLSBs is hindered by critical challenges at 
the Li/SSEs interface, including chemical/electrochemical instability, 
dendrite formation, and mechanical instability. The chemical instability 
primarily originates from the relatively weak P–S bonds and M-S bonds 
(M = Si, Sn, Ge, Al) in SSEs, which undergo spontaneous reactions with 
Li metal to form interfacial by-products such as Li2S, Li3P and Li-M al­

in interfacial impedance [42]. Furthermore, the inherent microscopic 
defects (e.g., voids, cracks, and protrusions) at the Li/SSEs interface 
result in nonuniform current distribution, promoting localized lithium 
deposition and rapid dendrite growth. Notably, even at low current 
densities (<0.1 mA cm2), Li dendrites preferentially nucleate at these 
interfacial defects and propagate along grain boundaries and pores, ul­
timately causing interfacial contact failure. Compounding these chal­
lenges, the substantial volume changes of the sulfur cathode during 
cycling induce significant stress concentration at the interface [43]. This 
mechanical stress accelerates grain boundary fracture in SSEs particles, 
exacerbating dendrite penetration and leading to rapid capacity fading 
and premature battery failure. To address these challenges, current 
research efforts primarily focus on two strategies: constructing artificial 
interface modification layers and developing novel lithium-based alloy 
anodes. Nevertheless, critical limitations persist in achieving high cur­
rent density and low potential alloy anode, which remains a funda­
mental bottleneck for practical applications.
2.3. Challenge of carrier transport based on solid-solid sulfur conversion
In traditional liquid-phase Li −S batteries, the redox reactions of the 
sulfur cathode follow a multiphase conversion pathway, characterized 
by discharge/charge voltage profiles with two distinct plateaus (Fig. 2a). 
The dissolution-precipitation mechanism enhances reaction kinetics by 
leveraging liquid-phase diffusion [44,45]. In contrast, ASSLSBs exhibit a 
single plateau during cycling, achieving solid-solid conversion that 
eliminates the shuttle effect caused by LiPSs dissolution and improves 
cycling stability. Despite the high ionic conductivity of SSEs, ASSLSBs 
suffer from high overpotential due to the sluggish kinetics of the solid- 
phase conversion reactions. Recent studies reveal that the sulfur reac­
tion does not proceed through direct S8-to-Li2S conversion but involves 
the formation of Li2S2 intermediate, requiring substantial activation 
energy. [46]. Moreover, both Li2S2 and Li2S are electronically insu­
lating, and their discontinuous deposition leads to severe interface 
passivation, restricting ion/electron transport and limiting active sulfur 
utilization [23]. Furthermore, the significant local structural recon­
struction during transformation reactions exacerbates kinetic barriers 
[47]. Thus, developing deeper understanding of the carrier transport 
mechanisms and optimizing electron/ion migration within the sulfur 
cathode are critical for improving the electrochemical performance of 
ASSLSBs.
To address these challenges, common strategies include introducing 
conductive additives (e.g., carbonaceous materials) and ionic conduc­
tors (e.g., functionalized solid electrolytes) into composite sulfur cath­
odes (Fig. 2b) to construct continuous mixed electronic-ionic transport 
networks essential for sulfur redox reactions [48]. The interfacial con­
tact between the components in these multiphase composites de­
termines ion/electron transport behavior and the electrochemical 
performance of ASSLSBs. However, the inclusion of a large amount of 
non-active additives inevitably dilutes the active sulfur content, 
reducing the energy density of the cathode [49]. Furthermore, the 
substantial volumetric changes during Li2S formation/decomposition 
cause interfacial deterioration, obstructing carrier transport and 
compromising reaction reversibility [50]. Additionally, the high-activity 
three-phase interfaces (comprising the sulfur, carbon, and SSEs) are 
prone to accelerate the electrochemical decomposition of the SSEs [51]. 
The decomposition products are electronically and ionically insulating, 
forming inert layers over the active interfaces. This degradation

3. Optimization strategy of the carrier transport in sulfur

3.1. Electronic conduction
3.1.1. Sulfur
Elemental sulfur typically exists in the form of S8 cyclic molecules 
with intrinsically low electronic conductivity, leading to sluggish reac­
tion kinetics during the sulfur redox process, significant polarization 
voltage during charge/discharge, and low active material utilization. 
For active materials, sufficient electronic and Li+ conductivity is 
essential to enable efficient redox reactions. Thus, modifying the band 
structure of sulfur through external doping to enhance its intrinsic 
electronic conductivity has become a research focus. Among potential 
dopants, group VI elements such as selenium (Se) and tellurium (Te) are 
promising dopants due to their electrochemical similarity to sulfur and 
superior electronic conductivity. Sun et al. introduced Se to form Se–S 
solid solutions (Fig. 3a), significantly enhancing both the electronic and 
ionic conductivity of sulfur, which in turn improved the utilization of the 
active material in the sulfur cathode [52]. This incorporation also soft­
ened the electrolyte structure, reduced Li+ migration activation energy 
barrier, and increased the interfacial ionic conductivity to 10−6 S cm−1. 
Hong et al. studied the intrinsic relationship between chemical hardness 
and phase transformation kinetics, finding that Se-substituted Li2S 
cathodes exhibit a low chemical hardness for Li-X bonds, while Te-Li2S 
cathodes show an even faster reduction in chemical hardness (Fig. 3b). 
The Te-Li2S cathodes exhibit significant increases in bond/volume strain 
and more stabilized vacancy formation energies, thereby greatly 
enhancing Li+ transport efficiency and lowering the phase trans­
formation barriers during solid-state conversion reactions of crystalline 
Li2S [53]. More recently, Liu et al. reported a novel approach to regulate

3.1.2. Metal sulfide
Li2S, as a pre-lithiated material, exhibits an electronic conductivity 
of 3.6 × 10−13 S cm−1, enhancing charge transfer capabilities and pro­
moting solid-phase sulfur conversion reactions [30]. Yan et al. utilized 
the combustion reaction of metallic lithium in CS2 to synthesize Li2S@C 
nanocomposite materials [54]. As shown in Fig. 4a, this approach 
created a unique structure in which Li2S nanocrystals are uniformly 
embedded within a conductive carbon matrix, significantly improving 
electronic conductivity by five orders of magnitude. Fujita et al. devel­
oped an anti-fluorite structured Li2S-LiI solid solution and investigated 
its charge-discharge mechanism of Li2S-LiI-C cathodes without addi­
tional SSEs [55]. The cathode exhibited high reversible capacity due to 
the formation of LiI-rich phases during charging, which maintained 
stable ionic conductivity (Fig. 4b). This electrode design eliminated the 
SSEs interference and removed overlapping peaks in X-ray photoelec­
tron spectroscopy analysis, facilitating the mechanistic studies of sulfur 
cathode conversion reactions. Wu et al. adopted a Cu+ and I−co-doping 
strategy (Fig. 4c), which increased the electronic conductivity and 
lithium-ion diffusion coefficient of Li2S by five and two orders of 
magnitude, respectively. Notably, Cu+ acted as a bulk-phase redox 
mediator, significantly improving the electrochemical reaction kinetics 
and overall electrochemical performance of the Li2S cathode [56].
Many transition metal sulfides (Fig. 4d), such as CuS, FeS2, and 
MoS6, exhibit intrinsic electrical conductivities several orders of 
magnitude higher than those of S8 and Li2S, enhancing the utilization of 
active sulfur [34,35,57]. However, due to their high atomic weights, 
metal sulfide electrodes generally lack a substantial energy density 
advantage. To address this limitation, researchers have proposed hy­
bridizing metal sulfides with S8 or designing high‑sulfur-content tran­
sition metal sulfides to increase the proportion of active material in the 
composite cathode. Ulissi et al. prepared a carbon-pyrrhotite‑sulfur- 
based composite via a ball-milling process. Their study revealed that the 
introduction of sulfur suppressed the formation of the defective pyr­
rhotite phase, and fully oxidized state of iron improved electrochemical 
reaction reversibility. The pyrrhotite‑sulfur ratio significantly influ­
enced the synergistic effects of the material [57]. Additionally, ternary 
metal sulfides (e.g., Li2TiS3, NiCo5S8, and Mo0.5Ti0.5S4) exhibit superior 
charge transport properties due to the introduction of cooperative multi- 
metallic effects [58–60]. However, challenges with transition metal 
sulfides persist, including their relatively low sulfur content, uneven

3.1.3. Carrier material
Carbon materials, with high electronic conductivity (>102 S cm−1) 
and tunable pore structures, serve as the core carrier for cathode ma­
terial design in ASSLSBs. Zhang et al. proposed a quantitative indicator 
Carbon Carrier Sensitivity (SA), used to dynamically track pressure and 
stability induced changes in the carbon‑sulfur network (Fig. 5a) [61]. 
Their study demonstrated that composite cathodes made from C-811 
carbon material, a mixture of AB, KB, and VGCFs in an 8:1:1 ratio, 
exhibited excellent pressure stability, maintained effective electronic 
pathways, and enabled ASSLSBs with outstanding rate performance, 
high capacity, and reliable cycling stability. However, in ASSLSBs, sulfur 
embedded inside the conductive carbon pores fails to fully interact with 
SSEs, reducing active material utilization. To address this issue, Zhu 
et al. designed a nitrogen-doped carbon fiber structure with surface 
micropores [18]. This structure provided excellent electronic conduc­
tivity, while the abundant surface micropores ensured efficient contact 
between the active material and the SSE, thereby significantly 
enhancing battery performance. Additionally, Luo et al. developed a 
sulfur‑carbon composite cathode material with a hierarchical carbon 
nanocage structure [62]. As shown in Fig. 5b, this design achieved high 
sulfur encapsulation and promoted charge transport efficiency through a 
continuous three-dimensional conductive network, delivering remark­
able areal capacity under high sulfur loading.
Furthermore, transition metal‑nitrogen catalysts including TM-N- 
OMCs, NiNC, and CoNC have been extensively employed to enhance 
sulfur cathode redox reaction kinetics in ASSLSBs [24,63,64]. He et al. 
demonstrated that single-atom nickel catalysts significantly accelerated 
sulfur reduction kinetics. As illustrated in Fig. 5c, the d-orbitals of nickel 
underwent pronounced vertical hybridization, transforming from the 
inert dsp2 hybrid state to the active d2sp3 configuration [24]. This 
electronic reconfiguration substantially improved the reduction effi­
ciency of Li2S2 to Li2S (Fig. 5d). Zhong et al. synthesized Co-N4-modified 
carbon materials via a facile ball-milling process to support sublimed 
sulfur, achieving exceptional electrochemical performance in ASSLSBs 
[21]. The catalytic mechanism involves d-p orbital hybridization be­
tween Co–N4 and sulfur species. During discharge, the strong electron 
transfer facilitated by the Co–S bond effectively weaken S–S bonds, 
promoting the conversion of sulfur to Li2S (Fig. 5e). During charging, 
electrons are conducted via the Co–N4 structure to the external circuit 
while simultaneously weakening Li–S bond strength, thereby reducing 
the energy barrier for Li2S oxidation (Fig. 5f). These advantages enabled 
the assembled ASSLSBs to deliver a reversible specific capacity of 993 
mAh g−1 with a superior capacity retention of 89.6 % after 300 cycles at 
0.2C (Fig. 5g). Nevertheless, as the sulfur conversion reaction in 
ASSLSBs significantly differs from that in conventional liquid-state Li −
S batteries, the precise catalytic pathways and interfacial phenomena 
warrant further in-depth investigation to fully elucidate their role in

Recent advances in sulfur cathode design have developed several 
effective strategies for enhancing electronic conductivity, such as 
interconnected structures, defect engineering, and electroactive addi­
tives [65–68]. These approaches significantly improve electron trans­
port pathways within the cathode architecture, thereby enhancing sulfur 
utilization and electrochemical reaction kinetics. However, excessive 
incorporation of conductive additives inherently dilutes the active sulfur 
content in the cathode, substantially compromising the energy density 
of ASSLSBs. Therefore, the design of sulfur cathodes necessitates a 
precise balance among electronic conductivity, sulfur content, and sul­
fur loading to realize ASSLSBs with both exceptional cycling perfor­
mance and commercially viable energy densities.
3.2. Ionic diffusion
3.2.1. Bulk ionic diffusion
Despite the excellent ionic conductivity of SSEs, the inherently low 
ionic conductivity of sulfur and Li2S significantly constrains the overall 
ionic conductivity of composite cathodes, thereby limiting reaction ki­
netics in ASSLSBs. To address this issue, Zhao et al. have adopted a 
multi-chalcogen element synergy strategy with entropy engineering to 
optimize the sulfur chain structure, successfully constructing short- 
chain molecular cathodes (Fig. 6a). These innovative short-chain 
cathodes effectively shorten the sulfur reduction pathway and lower 
the binding energy of lithium ions, thereby accelerating reaction rates 
(Fig. 6b). Moreover, the mismatched lithiation lattice reduces the 
decomposition energy barrier of Li2S, enabling rapid redox kinetics and 
enhanced electrochemical reversibility at low temperatures. As shown in 
Fig. 6c, the short-chain cathodes exhibit remarkable performance, 
achieving a discharge capacity of 579.6 mAh g−1 at −40 ◦C and a ca­
pacity retention of 98.4 % after 100 cycles at −20 ◦C, with an electrode

3.3. Hybrid electronic/ionic transport
In the traditional design of composite sulfur cathodes, the limited 
triple-phase interface between sulfur, carbon, and SSEs restricts the 
accessibility of sulfur to both electronic and ionic conductive pathways. 
This results in the formation of “dead sulfur” domains that cannot 
participate in electrochemical reactions, thereby substantially reducing 
cathode utilizing efficiency. To overcome this limitation, the strategic 
development of mixed ionic and electronic conductors (MIECs) has 
emerged as a promising strategy to break the constraints of traditional 
triple-phase interfaces. These bifunctional materials provide simulta­
neous pathways for both ionic and electronic transport, dramatically 
enhancing active material accessibility and utilization. As shown in 
Fig. 8a, Wang et al. demonstrated an amorphous Li-Ti-P-S material as an 
effective MIEC [13]. This material demonstrated ionic conductivity 
comparable to conventional SSEs. Moreover, the incorporation of TiS2 
significantly reduced the bandgap of the material, leading to an 
impressive electronic conductivity of up to 10−2 S cm−1 at room tem­
perature. The assembled ASSLSBs with Li-Ti-P-S material achieved un­
precedented electrochemical performance: 87.3 % sulfur utilization

4. Challenges and strategies of carrier transport during the 
electrochemical reaction
4.1. Mechanical-electrochemical failure
The pronounced mechanical property mismatch between S8 and 
SSEs, particularly in elastic modulus and bulk modulus, leads to triple- 
phase interface contact failure during the charge/discharge process in 
ASSLSBs [74,75]. Masanori et al. conducted post-mortem analysis 
revealing the occurrence of micro-scale phase segregation phenomena 
on the surface of sulfur cathodes in ASSLSBs [47]. This interfacial 
degradation obstructs electron and ion transport pathways, severely 
reducing active material utilization. To address this issue, a three- 
dimensional conductive graphene structure with a large pore network 
was employed as a sulfur host material. This structure not only accom­
modated substantial sulfur content (50 %) but also effectively suppresses 
the phase segregation through spatial confinement and strong surface 
interactions, enhancing interfacial contact stability and electrochemical 
performance.

6. Conclusions and perspectives
The development of various electronic conductors and SSEs has 
significantly accelerated the advancement of ASSLSBs. However, the 
inherent properties of sulfur materials and challenges associated with 
solid-solid contact interfaces result in suboptimal electrochemical per­
formance, particularly under conditions of high sulfur content and 
loading. Recently, functional electrolytes have garnered increasing 
attention for overcoming these limitations by enhancing the carrier 
transport properties in composite cathodes. As a result, ASSLSBs achieve 
excellent specific capacity, rate performance, and cycling stability even 
with high sulfur content and loading. To build on the high energy 
density advantage of ASSLSBs, sulfur cathode design should focus on the 
following aspects:
6.1. Development of new MIECs
MIECs provide coexisting pathways for both ionic and electronic 
transport, effectively reducing interfacial impedance and significantly 
improving the utilization of active sulfur materials. In addition, MIECs 
facilitate rapid conversion reactions at the two-phase interface, effi­
ciently preventing SSE decomposition and improving structural stabil­
ity, ultimately enhancing battery cycling stability. Furthermore, the 
incorporation of MIECs can partially replace conductive carbon and 
SSEs, reducing the proportion of inactive components, improving energy 
density, and simplifying the electrode fabrication process. However, 
practical challenges such as insufficient ionic conductivity and poor 
structural stability need to be addressed through material innovations, 
interface engineering, and structural design. With these advancements, 
MIECs have immense potential for widespread application in ASSLSBs.
6.2. Practical application requirements
At present, research on sulfur cathodes predominantly focuses on the 
performance verification under laboratory conditions, such as low sulfur 
content, low loading, and high stack pressure. However, when the sulfur 
content of the cathode increases to 50 wt%, the reduction in solid-solid 
contact area significantly decreases electrochemical efficiency and in­
creases the proportion of inactive sulfur. When the sulfur loading ex­
ceeds 6 mg cm−2, carrier transport pathways become longer and more 
complex, drastically reducing rate performance. Additionally, during 
the operation of sulfur cathodes, high stack pressure must be maintained 
to compensate for solid contact losses induced by volume changes, 
thereby ensuring effective carrier transport. To meet the practical re­
quirements for high energy density and operational feasibility, it is 
imperative to strike a balance between high sulfur content, high loading, 
and low stack pressure conditions. Moreover, systematic investigations 
into the electron/ion transport mechanisms at Li/SSEs interfaces, com­
bined with the development of ultrathin lithium anodes through inter­
face engineering and alloying strategies, are critically important for 
achieving ASSLSBs with high current density, high areal capacity, and 
high energy density.
6.3. Sulfur conversion mechanisms and carrier transport dynamics
In ASSLSBs, sulfur conversion occurs through a solid-phase trans­
formation process, which is restricted by slow ionic transport, and its 
underlying mechanisms remain insufficiently understood. Furthermore, 
sulfur conversion reactions occur at solid-solid interfaces, where inter­
facial contact area and carrier transport pathways play vital roles in 
controlling reaction efficiency. To address these issues, advanced in-situ 
characterization techniques combined with multi-scale modeling are 
essential to elucidate the reaction pathways of solid-phase sulfur con­
version and the dynamics of ion transport. These efforts will provide the 
fundamental understanding for the design of high-performance sulfur 
cathodes and the further development of ASSLSBs.
