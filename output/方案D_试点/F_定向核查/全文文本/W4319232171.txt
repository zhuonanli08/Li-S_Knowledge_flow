to a high cell voltage and a high energy density of ASSB-LiS 
batteries.[8]

solid electrolytes. Different types are, for example, polymer, 
oxide, sulfide, and halide electrolytes, which all differ in elec-
trochemical stability window and ionic conductivity.[9] In this 
study, the argyrodite Li6PS5Cl is applied, because of its high 
ionic conductivity and ductility.[10]

they break with restrictions and limitations that liquid electrolytes impose

reaction of sulfur is discussed. Sheet-type cathodes reach nearly theoretical

cell stack gives fundamental insights into the volume change and breathing 
behavior of both cathode and anode.

1. Introduction
In the past decades, lithium-ion batteries (LIB) have made a 
huge impact on the progress of energy storage, especially for 
portable devices. LIBs are distinguished by a relatively high 
power density and long cycle life. However, the specific energy 
of LIBs is limited and a high level of optimization is reached 
already.[1,2] Therefore, it is necessary to develop next generation

and Li+ ions. Therefore, the particle distribution and intimate 
contact between the carbon, sulfur, and solid electrolyte is 
essential. Previous works showed different routes to prepare 
a carbon/sulfur composite with high sulfur utilization, which 
can be classified in two main strategies, depending on the 
used carbon. Hayashi et al. demonstrated that the preparation 
routine of the cathode batches needs to be adapted in depend-
ency of the used carbon. It was noticed, that ball milling with 
low energy (15 min @ 300 rpm) leads to different sulfur uti-

meso-porous carbons, where the sulfur was melt-infiltrated, 
a high sulfur utilization was demonstrated. In contrast, if the 
sulfur is mixed with acetylene black (AB), characterized by a 
low pore volume, the sulfur was not addressable sufficiently 
after low energy ball milling.[11] Further investigations showed, 
that high energy ball milling of sulfur/AB/SE enables a higher 
sulfur utilization.[12] These findings agree with the results of 
Zeier and Janek et al., who demonstrated that high energy ball 
milling (8 h @ 500 rpm) leads to the formation of triple phase 
boundaries.[13,14] These triple phase boundaries are required 
in order to maintain ionic and electronic pathways within the 
cathode structure, to overcome kinetic limitations of the ion-
transport and conversion reaction within the cathode.[14,15] To 
receive more insights about the electrochemical performance of 
the cathode under realistic conditions, it is necessary to prepare 
demonstrator cells. Therefore, sheet type cathodes are required. 
Typically, electrodes are produced via slurry casting with a suit-
able solvent. Another option is the production of sheet type 
cathodes via a solvent-free process.[16,17] The advantages of the 
solvent-free process are waste reduction by avoiding usage of 
environmentally harmful solvents, lower foot-print of coating 
equipment, and the lower production costs. Furthermore, pre-
vious works showed that the electrical resistance of the dry-film 
cathodes is lower, compared to slurry-based cathodes.[16,18–20] As 
mentioned before, the sulfur cathode is characterized by insuf-
ficient ionic and electronic conductivity. Therefore, the imple-
mentation of a solvent-free process for the preparation of large-
scale cathodes becomes a key factor to maintain the formed 
pathways within the cathode.
In this work, cathodes with a high sulfur utilization, 
obtained after high energy ball milling, were prepared. Further-
more, implementation of the solvent-free process was investi-
gated after the formation of free-standing dry-film cathodes 
containing different amounts of binder. Besides our publication 
on NMC-cathodes, the lowest reported binder amount (down to 
0.1 wt% PTFE) for dry-film cathodes was achieved. The unique 
properties of PTFE as binder do not interfere with ionic and 
electronic pathways within the cathode, leading to an uncom-
promised rate and long term performance. The prepared dry-
films were investigated in small lab-scale cells and pouch cells. 
The assembled pouch cell was cycled for more than 50 cycles, 
demonstrating an outstanding cycling behavior compared to 
previous works on lithium sulfur pouch cells.[20,21]
To the best of our knowledge, a thickness monitoring of an 
all-solid-state lithium–sulfur battery was explored for the first 
time. Based on the calculated theoretical thickness changes of 
anode and cathode, insights into the breathing behavior of the 
cell stack were obtained. Following, in this work, fundamental 
findings about the volume change of the sulfur within the 
cathode composite were received. The investigation of the 
breathing behavior addresses one of the major challenges in all-
solid-state batteries, leading to a higher understanding of pos-
sible contact losses (mechanochemical failure) and degradation 
processes in all-solid-state batteries.
2. Results and Discussion
2.1. Carbon Composite Characterization
To host the sulfur into a carbon matrix, the commercial avail-
able carbon black Printex XE2B was used. It is characterized by 
a high surface area and hierarchical pore structure (Figure 1). It 
was assumed, that the hierarchical pore structure leads to a good 
accessibility of the sulfur and to the formation of triple phase 
boundaries. To discover the influence of ball milling on the 
carbon structure, the used Printex was analyzed via scanning 
electron microscope (SEM) imaging before and after ball milling.
Before ball milling, agglomerated carbon particles are dis-
played (Figure  1A). The SEM-image after ball milling shows 
smaller particles, in which the agglomerates are destroyed 
(Figure  1B). During ball milling, the average particle size 
decreases from 5.72 to 3.94 µm and the particle size distribu-
tion is less defined, leading to smallest particles about 0.2 µm, 
compared to 0.6 µm before ball milling (Figure S1, Supporting 
Information). The isotherms and pore size distribution esti-
mated by nitrogen physisorption (Figure  1C,D) demonstrate 
differences between the raw material (blue line) and the ball 
milled material (red line). The total pore volume is lowered 
from 1.55  cm3  g−1  before ball milling to 0.18  cm3  g−1. At the 
same time, the specific surface area is decreased from 1029 to 
121 m2 g−1. Comparing the pore size distribution it can be seen, 
that the pore volume and pore size is also changed. Before ball 
milling, the micro pore volume is 0.16 cm3 g−1 and the meso 
pore volume is 1.29 cm3 g−1. After ball milling the carbon, the 
micro pore volume is lowered to 0.03  cm3  g−1  and the meso 
pore volume is 0.16 cm3 g−1. The physisorption data illustrate, 
that the process of ball milling has a significant impact on the 
structure of the used carbon. The pore structure of Printex con-
sists of agglomerated carbon nanoparticles, which is destroyed 
during ball milling. Hence, the porosity as well as the surface 
area is lowered drastically. The sulfur cannot be localized exclu-
sively in the pores of the carbon, but is rather distributed on 
the surface. The deposition of the sulfur on the carbon sur-
face, in combination with the lowered surface area caused by 
ball milling, leads to a reduction of the electric conductivity of 
the carbon from 260 to 36 S cm−1 after ball milling. To obtain 
the carbon/sulfur composite, the raw materials were mixed via 
ball milling and analyzed via X-ray powder diffraction (XRD), 
displayed in Figure  S2, Supporting Information. After ball 
milling, the sulfur is present in amorphous form. The SEM-
images of the carbon/sulfur composite (Figure S3, Supporting 
Information) display a homogenous morphology and well-
distributed particles where no difference between carbon and 
sulfur can be noticed. This indicates, that the ball milling step 
leads to a good mixture of carbon and sulfur, ensuring the elec-
tronic contact of the sulfur.

2.2. Characterization of Cathodes
The prepared carbon/sulfur composite was mixed with solid 
electrolyte via high energy ball milling, to obtain a homogenous 
cathode with triple phase boundaries, as described before. The 
SEM and EDX images of the powder cathode (Figure 2A,B and 
Figure  S4, Supporting Information) illustrate a homogenous 
distribution of the containing materials carbon, sulfur, and 
solid electrolyte. At higher resolution, different particles with 
an average particle size below 3 µm can be observed. Further 
investigations of the prepared cathode via Raman spectroscopy 
(Figure  2C) show a difference between the cathode without 
sulfur (black) and the prepared cathode batch containing 
sulfur (green). In both samples the characteristic D and G 
bond of the carbon is seen.[22] The cathode sample shows 
an additional peak at 420 cm−1, which can be related to SS 
bond of the contained sulfur.[23] After discharging the cathode 
once (red), the described D and G bond is still observed. The 
peak at 420  cm−1  is less pronounced in comparison to the 
pristine cathode material, while additional peaks at 580  and 
1100  cm−1  are noticed. After charging the cathode again, the 
peak at 580 cm−1 disappears and the peak at 1100 cm−1 is still 
present. The peak at 580 cm−1 can be assigned to the S3− radical 
and the bond at 1100 cm−1 to the CS vibration.[24] This means, 
that a bond between the sulfur and carbon is formed during 
the preparation of the cathode. The S3−  anion, which is only 
observed in the discharged state of the cathode, shows that 
the sulfur is converted to a shorter polysulfide anion during 
discharging and converted to sulfur again during charging. 
In the obtained Raman spectra no S2− vibration was noticed, 
which leads to the conclusion, that the sulfur is not converted 
completely. Furthermore, we cannot exclude side reactions 
from the solid electrolyte at this point. It is known, that side 
reactions with the solid electrolyte can take place in the pre-
sent system.[25] Besides powder cathodes, dry-film cathodes 
were prepared as described in previous work.[18] The prepared 
dry-film cathode containing 0.5  wt% PTFE was analyzed via 
SEM imaging and compared to the powder cathode material. 
PTFE fibrils can be observed (Figure 2D,E), which are required 
to build a stable cathode sheet. Furthermore, the cathode dry-
film shows a smoother surface. At higher resolution it can be 
seen, that the particles are flattened and compacted during 
the manufacturing process of the cathode sheet. The electro-
chemical stability of the powder cathode and dry-film cathode 
was investigated via cyclic voltammetry (CV). The CV-meas-
urement was carried out in the voltage window of interest 
between 1.0 and 2.4 V versus In–Li (Figure 2E) corresponding 
to 1.62 and 3.02 V versus Li.[26] Both electrodes display a broad 
reductive peak between 1.15 and 1.3 V versus In–Li, in which 
multiple peaks are overlapped. The resulting current density 
during the first cycle is about 0.4 mA cm−2 for both samples. 
In the following cycles, the current density is rising at the 
powder cathode up to −0.48  mA  cm−2, whereas the current 
density at the dry-film cathode is decreasing to −0.25 mA cm−2. 
Along the positive branch, an oxidative peak at 1.8  V versus 
In–Li is observed in each first cycle. In the following cycles,

the sulfur conversion mechanism differs from the known 
mechanism in liquid systems. In the literature, no detailed 
mechanism on the conversion reaction in solid-state lithium–

assumed, since the full conversion of the sulfur to the lithium

2.4.2. Electrochemical Performance of Dry-Film Electrodes
In order to investigate the influence of the PTFE on the cathode 
kinetics, the prepared dry-films were investigated via the same 
rate test at the same loading (Figure 5). The C-rate perfor-
mance displays comparable discharge capacities as the powder 
electrode (Figure  4). The initial discharge capacity is about 
1400 mAh gS−1 and afterward the theoretical capacity of sulfur 
is reached. Even at increasing C-rates, the discharge capacity is 
kept constantly above 1400 mAh gS−1 for nine cycles. At 0.5 C 
the capacity is lowered to 600 mAh gS−1. After the rate test, the 
sulfur is utilized nearly to 100%. Table 2 displays the average 
voltage during charging a discharging. It can be noticed that 
the overpotential is rising with rising C-rates, analogue as it was 
observed for the powder cathode (described in section 2.4.1). 
The average voltage during discharging and charging occurs in 
the same range compared to the powder cathode, where the dis-
charge voltage is lowered from 1.32 to 1.11 V during the C-rate 
test (Table 1) and the charge voltage is rising from 1.73 to 1.87 V. 
Again, a shoulder above 2.1 V was observed during charging at 
higher C-rates, as mentioned for the powder cathode.
The long term performance of the dry-film cathode 
(Figure  5C) is comparable to the performance of the powder 
electrode (Figure 4C). In the first cycles after the rate test, the 
theoretical capacity of sulfur can be cycled constantly up to cycle 
30. Hereafter, the discharge capacity decreases with each cycle. 
After 60 cycles, a discharge capacity about 1000 mAh gS−1 is still 
remaining. The dry-film cathode shows a better long term per-

3. Conclusion
A strategy to prepare all-solid-state lithium–sulfur cathodes 
with high sulfur utilization via high energy ball milling was 
demonstrated successfully. Furthermore, the solvent-free 
manufacturing of sheet-type cathodes was demonstrated. The 
free-standing sheet-type cathodes were prepared by a dry-film 
process using PTFE as binder material. The binder-content 
was reduced to 0.1 wt% and flexible dry-film cathodes with a 
thickness varying between 50 µm (1.5 mgS cm−2) and 300 µm 
(1.5  mgS  cm−2) were prepared. We observed that the elec-
trochemical stability and performance of the dry-film cath-
odes was not affected in a negative way by the addition of 
binder. Deeper insights into the conversion chemistry of the 
cathode suggested that the electrolyte takes part in a revers-
ible lithiation reaction. The dry-film cathodes were character-
ized by a similar rate and long term performance compared 
to the powder cathode without binder. After adaption of the 
cutoff voltage during charging, we demonstrated more than 
400  deep cycles with a capacity retention of 72% from ini-
tial discharge capacity at a loading of 1.5 mgS cm−2. Even at 
increased area loadings (cathode thickness) of 2.0 mgS cm−2, 
a high sulfur utilization and capacity retention was obtained 
for 200 cycles. The obtained freestanding cathode sheets were 
transferred successfully into a prototype pouch cell by imple-
menting an In–Li alloy anode. The pouch cell was cycled 
for more than 50  cycles, and a comparable electrochemical 
performance and sulfur utilization was observed. The moni-
toring of thickness change during charging and discharging 
gave insights about the breathing behavior of the cathode. We 
noticed, that the volume change of sulfur is less pronounced 
than predicted. The nm-sized sulfur domains are imbedded 
in carbon and solid electrolyte, which redirects the volume 
changes from a µm-sized swelling of the whole cathode to 
a local 3D breathing of S8  particles that is compensated by 
the (residual porosity of the) carbon framework. This paves 
the way for solid-state batteries that do not require a con-
stant pressure application and compensation for thickness 
changes, which crucially simplifies design for cell housing 
and safety components.
4. Experimental Section
Preparation of Cathode Composite: The preparation of the cathode 
composites was performed in an argon filled glovebox. In a first step, 
sulfur (Sigma Aldrich) and carbon (Printex XE2B, Orion) were mixed in 
weight ration 3:2 and ball milled for 4 h at a rotation speed of 400 rpm. 
In a second step, 150 mg of the prepared carbon sulfur composite and 
150 mg solid electrolyte (NEI) were mixed and ball milled for 4 h at a 
rotation speed of 400 rpm again.
Preparation of Dry-Film Cathode: To prepare a free-standing cathode 
dry-film, different amounts of PTFE (molecular weight = 107–108 g mol−1; 
emulsion polymerized) were added to the prepared cathode composite. 
Afterward, the blend was transferred in a heated mortar and sheared, 
until a single cathode flake was formed. The flake was rolled out on a 
heated plate to a thickness of ≈ 50 µm and the obtained dry-film was 
stomped out to the desired diameter of the cathodes.
Assembly of Solid-State Lab-Scale Cells: For the assembly of the 
lab-scale cells, an In–Li alloy (In/Li ratio  =  1.65) was used as anode. 
Therefore, an indium foil (100  µm thickness, 13  mm diameter) was 
placed on the stainless steel current collector. In the following step, a 
lithium foil (50 µm thickness, 13 mm diameter) with the same diameter 
was placed on top. To prepare the separator layer 150  mg of solid 
electrolyte (NEI) were filled on top of the anode and distributed evenly 
with a spatula. In a next step, the solid electrolyte was pelletized under 
moderate pressure and then 4.75 mg cathode composite was distributed 
with a spatula to a diameter of 11  mm on top to form the cathode. 
For the cells using a dry-film electrode, the dry-film was stomped to a 
diameter of 11 mm and placed on top of the solid electrolyte layer. The 
prepared cell was pressed in a hydraulic press under 110 bar for 30 s and 
afterward placed in an outer steel case and tensed at 3.5 Nm.
Assembly of Solid-State Pouch Cell: A pouch cell was prepared to carry 
out the cathode performance in a larger scale. Therefore, copper foil was 
used as anode current collector. At the top of the copper CC an indium 
foil (100 µm thick) and lithium foil (50 µm thick) was placed, to prepare 
the In–Li alloy anode. As separator layer, a free-standing dry-film was 
prepared, using solid electrolyte (NEI) and 0.25  wt% PTFE as binder. 
The PTFE was fibrillated in a heated mortar (100 °C) and rolled out to a 
homogenous thickness of ≈ 900 µm at a heated metal plate at the same 
temperature. The prepared free-standing solid electrolyte film was placed 
on top of the anode. Afterward the free-standing cathode containing 
0.5 wt% binder (thickness = 50 µm, n/p = 3) was sealed on a primered 
aluminum current collector and placed on the separator layer. Finally, 
the prepared cell stack was sealed into pouch foil and pressurized in an 
isostatic press at 500 MPa.
Electrochemical Characterization GCPL: Galvanostatic measurements 
were carried at a battery tester CTS-Lab (BaSyTec, Germany) at constant 
temperature of 25 °C. For the lab-scale type cell, the rate performance 
test consisted of three capacity-limited cycles at rising C-rates, starting 
at C/20 to C/10, C/5, C/2, and one last cycle at C/20 during discharge. 
During charging, the C-rate was kept at C/20  during the first three 
cycles and at C/10 afterward, using an additional CV-step after reaching 
the potential limit. After the rate performance test, the long term 
performance was tested for more than 50  cycles at C/10  for exclusive 
cells. The electrochemical performance of the prepared pouch cells 
were conducted at the same system at a temperature of 30  °C. In a 
first section, 30 cycles at C/20 were performed, before starting the rate 
performance test for five cycle per C-rate, starting from C/10 up to 2 C.
Cyclic Voltammetry: To investigate the stability of the prepared dry-
films during charging and discharging, CV was performed using a VSP-
300 (Bio-Logic, France). For the solid electrolyte, the CV was carried 
out at 0.1 mV s−1 between 0 and 5 V versus Li. The investigation of the 
cathode was performed at 0.019  mV  s−1 (corresponding to C/20) in 
the voltage window between 1.0 and 2.4 V versus In–Li, comparable to 
the galvanostatic measurements.
Physisorption Measurements: After activating the carbon samples at 
200 °C for 12 h, the physisorption measurements were performed at 77 K 
using a BELSORP instrument (Japan). Brunauer–Emmett–Teller method 
was used to calculate specific surface areas in the range of relative 
pressure from 0.05 to 0.3 p/p0. Pore size distributions were calculated 
using quenched solid density functional theory method for carbon (slit/
cylindrical/spherical pores) using the adsorption branch. The total pore 
volume was determined at relative pressure of p/p0 = 0.99.
SEM: SEM analysis was carried out at a scanning electron microscope 
JSM-6060 (JEOL, Japan).
