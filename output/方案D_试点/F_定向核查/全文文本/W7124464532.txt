SUMMARY
Lithium-sulfur all-solid-state batteries (Li-S ASSBs) offer ultrahigh specific capacity and enhanced safety but 
are hindered by low cathode-level energy density. Simulations reveal that intra-agglomerate mass-transport 
limitations in bulk sulfur represent the primary challenge. To address this, we propose an ‘‘active material 
(AM)-free’’ design using a cathode composed solely of Li 6 PS 5 Cl (LPSCl) and carbon, where AMs form in 
situ via electrolyte decomposition. The resulting submicron-scale AMs overcome mass-transport limitations 
through shortened diffusion paths. By expanding LPSCl-carbon interfaces, this cathode exhibits high cath-
ode-level energy density. The self-limiting AM growth prevents excessive degradation, ensuring cycling sta-
bility (95% retention after 500 cycles at 50 ◦ C). Subsequent optimization with LiI additive further elevates cath-
ode-level energy density to 1,277.8 Wh kg − 1 (9.25 mAh cm − 2 ) and extends cycle life to 767 cycles at room 
temperature (RT). This work provides a promising approach to designing high-performance cathodes by ex-
ploiting the redox activity of the solid electrolyte.
INTRODUCTION
Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries, which store lithium through sulfur’s 
conversion reactions, exhibit ultrahigh theoretical specific ca-
pacity of 1,672 mAh g s ⁻¹ (based on sulfur mass) 1 and high-energy 
density. 2–4 Moreover, the natural abundance of sulfur avoids reli-
ance on critical materials like nickel and cobalt, 5 which are essen-
tial for conventional layered oxide cathodes. These attributes 
position Li-S batteries as one of the most promising alternatives 
to current Li-ion batteries. 3,6,7 Li-S all-solid-state batteries 
(ASSBs) address the thermal runaway risks of liquid Li-S batteries 
by replacing flammable liquid electrolytes with non-flammable 
solid electrolytes (SEs), 8–13 which ensures inherent safety. Addi-
tionally, SEs mitigate the ‘‘shuttle effect,’’ which is a persistent
CONTEXT & SCALE Lithium-sulfur all-solid-state batteries offer high theoretical capacity and improved 
safety over conventional lithium-ion systems. However, lithiation and delithiation require simultaneous ac-
cess to both electrons and ions, and sulfur has poor ionic and electronic conductivity; therefore, conductive 
carbon and catholyte must be added to the composite cathode, which lowers practical energy density. The 
conventional view believes that achieving high-energy density in lithium-sulfur batteries requires maximizing 
the preloaded sulfur loading while keeping polarization under control. However, increasing the content of 
preloaded sulfur inevitably exacerbates mass-transport limitations within sulfur agglomerates. In this 
work, we propose an alternative route for the high-energy-density cathode: exploit the redox activity of 
the sulfide electrolyte itself. We introduce an ‘‘active material-free’’ cathode design without preloading sulfur. 
Instead, active materials are formed in situ via the decomposition of the solid electrolyte Li 6 PS 5 Cl at carbon 
interfaces. In situ-formed active materials are inherently accessible to both ions and electrons, and the self-
limiting decomposition reaction shortens mass-transport lengths within the active material. By increasing the 
interfacial area between the ionic conductor and the electronic conductor, we improved the kinetics of the 
Li 6 PS 5 Cl redox reactions, ultimately achieving a cathode with high-energy density and long cycle life.

In this article, we reveal that mass-transport limitations are the 
key constraint on performance in conventional Li-S ASSBs. To 
overcome this, we took a path completely opposite to the main-
stream: the cathode contains no preloaded sulfur (Figure 1D). 
Contrary to the prevailing view that regards electrolytes as inac-
tive, Li 6 PS 5 Cl (LPSCl) intrinsically undergoes oxidative decom-
position to form elemental sulfur. Therefore, enhancing energy 
density does not necessarily require increasing preloaded sulfur 
but can also be achieved by strengthening the redox activity of 
the electrolyte. The advantage is that the decomposition prod-
ucts of LPSCl are both ionically and electronically insulating, 
which inherently limits further reaction progression. 27–30 This 
self-limiting reaction constrains the size of in situ-formed AMs, 
thereby shortening the required mass-transport length, which 
contributes to enhancing mass-transport kinetics. Therefore, 
we employ the ‘‘AM-free’’ design, whereby the AM is in situ 
formed through electrolyte decomposition (Figure 1E). Through 
maximizing the effective LPSCl-C interfacial area (Figure 1F) to 
enhance redox activity of LPSCl, AM-free ASSB delivered a 
high cathode-level energy density and exhibited cycling stability: 
zero capacity fade after 127 cycles at room temperature (RT) and 
stable performance through 500 cycles at 50 ◦ C. After over-
coming the mass-transport limitations in conventional Li-S 
ASSBs through AM-free design, the next rate-limiting step shifts
to interfacial charge transfer kinetics. Accordingly, a spiral-
progression strategy, using the LiI additive to lower the 
charge transfer resistance, further boosts the RT performance 
to 853 mAh g cat ⁻¹ and 1,277.8 Wh kg cat ⁻¹ (the calculation of the 
cathode-level energy density is given by Note S1).

increasing the sulfur content inevitably deteriorates mass trans-
port within agglomerates, leading to more severe polarization.
Overcoming mass-transport limitations through 
exploiting the redox activity of SEs
Instead of increasing the preloaded sulfur, we exploit LPSCl 
reactivity to form electrochemically AMs. Upon oxidative
decomposition during charging, LPSCl in situ forms AMs. As 
shown in Figure S6, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and 
X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) indicate the formation 
of elemental sulfur in the charged LPSCl-C composite. The 
self-limiting nature of the decomposition products constrains 
the thickness of the AM layer, which is expected to overcome 
mass-transport limitations. As shown in Figures 3A and 3B, we 
simulated how the thickness of the product layer and the oxida-
tion current density evolved during LPSCl decomposing at a po-
tential of 2.4 V versus Li/Li + (the simulation method can be found 
in Note S4 and Figure S7). As the product layer gradually thick-
ened, the decomposition reaction rate decreased as evidenced 
by the decreased electronic current densities. This self-limiting 
behavior kept the thickness of the product layer below 0.7 μm 
even after 800 min, which constrains the required diffusion 
length L and thus contributes to overcoming mass-transport lim-
itations. In addition, the absence of large agglomerates also pre-
vents blockage of ionic pathways during charging, allowing 
gradual, more complete conversion of the electrolyte and fuller 
utilization.
The pathway to enhancing energy density lies in improving reac-
tion kinetics. Expanding SE-C interfaces helps enhance the redox 
activity of LPSCl. Volume ratios of the LPSCl-C composite were 
optimized to increase effective interfacial area. We prepared a se-
ries of composites with x vol % graphene (GN) and (100 − x) vol % 
LPSCl via high-energy ball milling. As shown in Figure 3C, the 
discharge specific capacity (based on the cathode mass) first 
increased and then decreased with higher C content. This 
behavior was mechanistically explained by the randomly gener-
ated two-phase models to represent the C and LPSCl (top panel 
of Figure 3C). At ∼45 vol % C, LPSCl formed large clusters with 
limited interfacial contact with GN, resulting in low discharge ca-
pacity. By contrast, ∼65 vol % C maximized the effective interfa-
cial area, enabling the highest capacity in our experiments. 
Beyond this ratio, excessive C introduced redundant, ineffective 
C-C interfaces and disrupted ionic transport, thus degrading per-
formance. To further increase the effective interfacial area, we 
tested C with distinct specific surface areas, including carbon 
nanotubes (CNTs), GN, and Ketjen black (KB) (Figure S8), at a fixed 
content of 10 wt % (∼65 vol %) in LPSCl-C composites. As shown 
in Figure 3D, CNT, with a fibrous morphology and ∼73 m 2 g − 1 sur-
face area, which established only limited contact with the SE, 
delivered a specific capacity of 181 mAh g cat 
− 1 . By contrast, KB, 
featuring a highly branched architecture and ∼1,368 m 2 g − 1 sur-
face area, formed dense interfacial networks that promoted in 
situ AM formation, resulting in 726 mAh g cat 
− 1 . GN, possessing a 
sheet-like structure and intermediate surface area, exhibited mod-
erate performance consistent with its interfacial characteristics. 
Figure S9 shows electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS) mea-
surements of the charge transfer resistances for as-prepared 
LPSCl-C composites. The lower charge transfer resistance of 
LPSCl-KB indicates a larger effective interfacial area within the 
KB-based cathode, further confirming that KB provides more 
effective interfaces, which promotes in situ AM generation. There-
fore, the LPSCl/KB composite (90/10, w/w) was selected for the 
following studies as a cathode material for AM-free ASSBs.

Cycling performance of AM-free ASSBs
We proceeded to evaluate the cycling stability of the composite 
cathode (LPSCl/KB = 90/10, w/w). The LPSCl-KB|LPSCl|LiIn 
cell, with an areal loading of 6.4 mg cat cm⁻ 2 , was cycled between 
0.3 and 2.8 V at 0.64 mA cm⁻ 2 (∼0.2C) and RT. The initial 
discharge specific capacity reached 574 mAh g cat ⁻¹, followed 
by a progressive increase over the subsequent 20 cycles 
(Figure 4A). This was an activation stage where LPSCl decom-
posed at KB interfaces and formed AMs, which contributed to 
the increasing capacity. During the stabilized cycling stage, the 
cell exhibited notable cycling stability, with nearly identical 
charge-discharge profiles at the 50th and 100th cycles 
(Figure 4B). Notably, instead of capacity fade, the AM-free cath-
ode exhibited a slow rise in the specific capacity, achieving an 
areal capacity of 4.2 mAh cm⁻ 2 (660 mAh g cat ⁻¹) after 127 cycles. 
This behavior shows that the self-limiting decomposition of sul-
fide electrolytes simultaneously achieved high AM utilization 
and maintained the conductivity of the bulk SE.
These findings also imply that in the prior reports of 
conventional Li-S ASSBs, the measured capacity might not orig-
inate entirely from preloaded sulfur. Instead, the in situ-formed 
sulfur through SE decomposition made non-negligible contribu-
tions to the overall capacity. Therefore, the specific capacities
based on sulfur mass (mAh g S 
− 1 ) are inappropriate, and the 
cathode-mass-based specific capacity (mAh g cat 
− 1 ) is more 
proper for such systems.
As a comparison, we investigated the cycling performance of 
the LPSCl-GN composite cathode (Figure S14). The LPSCl-GN 
cell similarly exhibited gradual capacity growth but plateaued 
at 384 mAh g cat ⁻¹ after 77 cycles due to GN’s lower surface 
area. This contrast underscores the important role of maximizing 
C-SE effective interfacial area in AM-free designs.
Next, we cycled the LPSCl-KB|LPSCl|LiIn cell at a higher cur-
rent density of 1.6 mA cm⁻ 2 with an areal loading of 4.84 mg cat 
cm − 2 at 50 ◦ C (Figure 4C). Consistent with observations at low 
current density, the capacity evolution exhibited a similar two-
stage profile: an initial activation stage followed by stable 
cycling. Even at a relatively large current, the LPSCl-KB cathode 
retained a capacity of ∼600 mAh g cat ⁻¹, and the discharge ca-
pacity was 95% of the initial value after 500 cycles.
The rate capability of the LPSCl-KB|LPSCl|LiIn cell was evalu-

The AM-free cathode design enables simultaneous achieve-
ment of high-energy density and power density, as demon-
strated in the Ragone plot (Figure S16). At 3C, LPSCl-KB 
matched the power density of Ni-Mn-Co-based ternary cath-
odes 42 while providing approximately twice their energy density.
Electrochemical reaction pathway of the AM-free 
cathode
LPSCl undergoes oxidative decomposition during the first
charge of the AM-free cathode, and the formed decomposition 
products serve as redox-AMs in subsequent cycles, enabling
reversible lithiation and delithiation. The charge-discharge 
voltage profiles (Figure 4B) and the cyclic voltammetry (CV)
curves (Figure S17) of LPSCl-KB differed between the initial cy-
cle and stabilized cycling stages. The initial charging oxidation
current onset occurred at ∼2.3 V versus Li/Li + , which corre-
sponds to the upper limit of the LPSCl stability window. The 
absence of S 8 plateau during the first charge and its appearance 
in subsequent cycles indicate that active S 8 is generated by
oxidative decomposition of LPSCl during the initial charge.
This is further confirmed by XPS results as shown in Figure 5A. 
In the as-prepared LPSCl-KB sample, only the S 2p peak origi-
nating from PS 4

Li-In/Li + ) detected an S 2p 3/2 peak at 163.5 eV and an S 2p 1/2
peak at 164.7 eV, respectively, which were attributed to S 8 . 43

In summary, during the first charge, LPSCl is oxidatively de-
composed to form LiCl, Li 3 PS 4 , and S 8 . Li 3 PS 4 is further oxidized 
to Li 2 P 2 S 6 and additional S 8 , and some Li 2 P 2 S 6 undergoes 
further delithiation to form other products. The main conversion 
pathways of these AMs during subsequent stable cycling are 
illustrated in the upper panels of Figure 5E. During discharging, 
S 8 is first reduced to Li 2 S and Li 3 PS 4 , which provide approxi-
mately 82% of the total discharge energy, based on the 
discharge voltage profile in Figure 4B. Further discharge partially 
reduces Li 3 PS 4 to Li 3 P. During charge, Li 3 P is oxidized to Li 3 PS 4 
initially, and incomplete oxidation can produce Li 4 P 2 S 6 . The 
main charge plateau corresponds to the oxidation of Li 2 S to S 8 
and to the oxidation of Li 3 PS 4 to S 8 and Li 2 P 2 S 6 . Although these 
conversions are thermodynamically allowed, the high delivered 
discharge energy density depends critically on the kinetic control 
of AM-free cathodes. In particular, the in situ generated AMs are 
inherently electronically and ionically connected, and their sub-
micron size mitigates transport limitations, making the theoreti-
cally high-energy-density kinetically accessible. Conventional 
Li-S ASSBs do not deliver high capacity under the same cutoff 
voltage (Figure S19).
In the initial oxidation products of LPSCl, LiCl is inactive and 
does not participate in redox reactions during cycling. Although 
its presence reduces specific capacity and energy density, as 
shown in Figure S20, the chloride-free sulfide SE cathode 
(L 3 PS 4 -KB) achieves 834 mAh g cat 
− 1 . We found that the L 3 PS 4 - 
KB cathode without LiCl retained only 53% of the initial capacity 
after 120 cycles. Electronically insulating species such as LiCl 
are thought to impede electron transfer, thereby slowing ongoing 
electrochemical decomposition and stabilizing the interface. 55 
Based on this, we speculate the cycling stability of LPSCl-KB 
is likely related to the relatively low electronic conductivity of
LiCl, which could help passivate the interface and limit excessive 
irreversible electrolyte decomposition.
Enhancing performance through lithium iodide additive 
The AM-free design has overcome the mass-transport limita-
tions of conventional Li-S ASSBs. In the new LPSCl-KB system, 
interfacial reaction kinetics emerge as the next rate-limiting step. 
A large charge transfer resistance after charging is shown in EIS 
in Figure 6A (the EIS and the distribution of relaxation time [DRT] 
analysis during charging and discharging of LPSCl-KB are 
shown in Figure S21). S 8 as an electronic insulator caused signif-
icant polarization. As shown in the left panel of Figure 6B, density 
functional theory (DFT) calculation exhibited a large band gap of
∼2.4 eV for elemental sulfur. Consequently, we further address 
charge transfer limitations. Figure S22 illustrates this spiral pro-
gression. LiI was introduced as an additive into the LPSCl-KB 
composite cathode (LiI|LPSCl|KB = 5/85.5/9.5 w/w/w). I⁻ ions 
are expected to oxidize to I 2 during charging in Li-S 
ASSBs, 19,23 and our interfacial calculations demonstrated that 
I 2 /S 8 interfaces introduced new energy states within sulfur’s 
band gap, as shown in Figure 6B, thereby enhancing the com-
posite cathode electronic conductivity. The addition of LiI mark-
edly lowered the charge transfer resistance observed in fully 
charged LiI-modified cathodes (Figure 6A).
The rate capability of the LPSCl-KB-LiI|LPSCl|LiIn cell was 
investigated at 0.64, 1.6, 3.2, and 6.4 mA cm − 2 at RT, respec-
tively, with an areal loading of ∼5.5 mg cat cm − 2 as shown in 
Figure 6C. At a current density of 0.64 mA cm⁻ 2 (∼0.2C), the 
composite cathode delivered a specific capacity of 853 mAh 
g cat ⁻¹, and at 1.6, 3.2, and 6.4 mA cm − 2 (∼0.5C–2C), the capac-
ities still achieved 820, 612, and 465 mAh g cat ⁻¹.
The LPSCl-KB-LiI cathode achieved high cathode-level energy 
density with high areal capacity at RT. As shown in Figure 6D, at 
0.07C, the discharge areal capacity reached 9.25 mAh cm − 2 , and 
the energy density at the cathode level achieved 1,278 Wh 
kg cat 
− 1 , which was further improved compared with the value of 
1,174.8 Wh kg cat 
− 1 achieved by LPSCl-KB. We compared the 
areal capacities and cathode-level energy densities of reported 
conventional Li-S ASSBs with this work in Figure S23.
Next, we tested the cycling stability of the LPSCl-KB-
LiI|LPSCl|LiIn cell with a cathode loading of 5.35 mg cat cm − 2 
(Figure 6E). At RT and 1.9 mA cm − 2 (∼0.6C), the specific capac-
ity was sustained at 81% of the initial capacity over 767 cycles. 
These indicate that the addition of LiI not only maintained the 
high specific capacity and stability of LPSCl-KB but also 
improved the cycling rate, which marks a significant step toward 
practical high-energy-density solid-state batteries.
Conclusions and outlook
Overall, the AM-free ASSB abandons preloaded elemental sulfur 
and instead utilizes the self-limiting electrochemical decomposi-
tion of the sulfide SE (LPSCl) to in situ form AMs at the conductive 
C interfaces. Mechanistic studies indicate that the self-formed 
AMs contribute capacity via the S/P redox pathway. This strategy 
overcomes the mass-transport limitations and achieves high-en-
ergy density and stable cycling. Furthermore, the introduction of 
the LiI additive optimizes charge transfer, thereby enhancing per-
formance. This AM-free design demonstrates that improving the

METHODS
SE synthesis
Stoichiometric amounts of Li 2 S (>99.9%, Alfa Aesar), P 2 S 5 
(>99.9%, Alfa Aesar), and LiCl (>99.9%, Alfa Aesar) were first 
mixed and ground to achieve a homogeneous blend. The 
mixture was then transferred to a zirconia ball-milling jar and pro-
cessed in a planetary ball mill at 500 rpm for 30 h. After ball mill-
ing, the powder was heated in an argon atmosphere at 550 ◦ C for
6 h to obtain LPSCl.
Cathode preparation
AM-free cathodes were prepared via high-energy ball milling. 
LPSCl and conductive C were mixed at a weight ratio of 
(100 − x):x (where x = 5, 10, 20, 30, and 50) and then loaded 
into ZrO 2 jars. ZrO 2 balls of 10, 8, 5, and 0.7 mm diameter 
were added, with a ball-to-material ratio of approximately 30:1. 
The milling procedure consisted of a milling step (600 rpm, 
15 min) followed by a rest step (5 min), for a total of 20 h under 
an argon-protected atmosphere.
The conductive C materials—CNTs (Chengdu Jiacai Technol-
ogy), GN (Chengdu Jiacai Technology), and KB ECP-600JD (KB, 
Lion Specialty Chemicals.)—were used directly without further 
processing. The resulting AM-free LPSCl-C cathodes had C 
weight fractions of 5, 10, 20, 30, and 50 wt %, corresponding 
to volume fractions of 46, 65, 80, 88, and 94 vol %, respectively. 
For sulfur-based cathodes, sulfur (99.5%, Alfa Aesar) and KB 
were mixed at a weight ratio of x:10 (where x = 10, 30, and 50), 
ground for 30 min, and then heated at 155 ◦ C for 12 h. The sul-
fur-KB composite was subsequently combined with LPSCl at a 
weight ratio of (x + 10):(90 − x) (where x = 10, 30, and 50), ground 
for 10 min, and milled using the same ball-milling procedure as the 
AM-free cathodes. Cathodes with total sulfur weight fractions of 
10, 30, and 50 wt % were obtained. Cathodes and electrolytes 
were prepared in an Ar atmosphere with H 2 O and O 2 < 0.1 ppm.
Cell fabrication
Cells were fabricated as follows: 70 mg of LPSCl powder was 
pressed into a pellet (10 mm in diameter) at ∼150 MPa for
1 min. Cathode powders with the targeted area loading were uni-
formly spread onto one side of the electrolyte pellet and pressed 
at ∼765 MPa for 3 min. Lithium foil and indium foil were roll-
pressed at a mass ratio of 2:98 to form a lithium-indium (LiIn) 
alloy (∼0.25 mm thickness), which was used as the anode. The 
LiIn anode was then placed on the opposite side of the electro-
lyte pellet. Finally, stainless steel was used as the cathode 
current collector, and copper foil was used as the anode current 
collector. The assembled cell was compressed at 150 MPa 
before testing. All the cells were fabricated in an Ar-filled glove-
box with H 2 O and O 2 < 0.1 ppm.
Materials characterization
The cross-sectional morphology of the LPSCl-KB cathode was 
analyzed using a scanning electron microscope and energy-
dispersive X-ray spectrometer (JEOL, JSM-7900F, Japan). Prior 
to SEM-EDS analysis, the cross-section of the sample was pol-
ished using a cross-section polisher (JEOL, IB-19520CCP, 
Japan). The sample was transferred under an Ar atmosphere 
to avoid air exposure.
To prepare the sample for Raman measurement, the LPSCl-
KB|LPSCl|LiIn cell was removed from the cell mold using a sepa-
rator. The cross-section was then cut with a ceramic blade. 
Subsequently, the sample was placed in a custom-made 
argon-protected optical chamber. Raman spectra were ob-
tained using a HORIBA LabRAM HR Evolution Raman system 
with a 633 nm excitation laser and a 50× objective lens.
The XPS samples were prepared in the glove box and trans-
ferred through a vacuum transfer chamber to the Thermo Scien-
tific K-Alpha X-ray photoelectron spectrometer for probing. 
Nitrogen gas adsorption was performed to obtain specific 
surface area for different conductive Cs based on the 
Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method with a surface area 
analyzer (Micromeritics, ASAP2460, USA).
Electrochemical testing
Cells were cycled under a stack pressure of ∼50 MPa at either 
50 ◦ C in a thermal chamber or at RT using a battery testing sys-
tem (Land CT2001A). All battery cycling was conducted inside 
the glove box (H 2 O, O 2 < 0.1 ppm).
Impedance spectra were measured using an electrochemi-
cal workstation (Bio-Logic VSP-300) over a frequency range 
of 1 Hz to 6 MHz, with 8 points measured per decade. A sinu-
soidal alternating voltage with an amplitude of 10 mV was
applied. 
A pulse current of 0.2 mA cm − 2 was applied for 1 h during the 
GITT measurement, followed by a 5-h relaxation at RT. The
