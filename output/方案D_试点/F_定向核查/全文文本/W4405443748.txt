ABSTRACT: The utilization of earth-abundant and high-capacity
sulfur in solid-state batteries presents a promising strategy to
circumvent the use of rare transition metals and enhance achievable
specific energy. However, numerous challenges remain. The
transport limitation within the cathode composite, particularly
with sulfide electrolytes during charging, has been identified as a
major degradation mechanism in solid-state Li−S batteries. This
degradation is linked to electrolyte oxidation and a concomitant
reduction in the effective ionic conductivity of the cathode
composite. Inspired by the sufficiently high oxidation stability of
halide-based electrolytes, we investigated their compatibility with
solid-state Li−S batteries in this work. The electrochemical stability
of halides in contact with conductive additives, the stability window
of fast ion transport in the composite electrodes, and chemical compatibility with sulfur-active materials (e.g., S and Li2S), in
addition to the cyclability of the halide-based composite electrodes, are explored. Three halides were employed as model
electrolytes: Li3InCl6, Li3YCl6, and Li3YBr6. Despite its high oxidation stability, Li3InCl6 exhibited rapid degradation due to
electrolyte reduction. The composite with Li3YCl6 lost its capacity because of chemical incompatibility, especially with Li2S, resulting
in the formation of LiYS2 at the interface. In contrast, Li3YBr6 demonstrated superior performance, maintaining a capacity of 1100
mAh gS
−1 for 20 cycles (normalized to the sulfur content in the cathode material). This study elucidates the degradation mechanisms
of halide-based solid-state Li−S batteries and proposes potential design strategies to mitigate chemical incompatibility issues.
1. INTRODUCTION
Lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries are a promising next-
generation energy storage technology owing to their high
specific capacity, earth abundance, environmental friendliness,
and cost-effectiveness.1−6 Despite outstanding theoretical
performances, their commercialization has been hindered by
a fast capacity fade. In particular, the degradation arising from
the reaction intermediates that dissolve into the liquid
electrolyte is critical to the overall performance and lifespan
of Li−S batteries.7 This degradation mode is inherent to
conventional Li−S batteries and has been a critical challenge
ever since the birth of Li−S batteries; however, by eliminating
the liquid electrolyte and replacing it with a solid electrolyte
(SE), solid-state Li−S batteries offer inherent resistance to the
dissolution of reaction intermediates, thereby ceasing the
thought-to-be unavoidable failure mechanisms to exist.8
Solid-state batteries require SEs to possess fast ion
conduction and sufficient stability to allow the cell to cycle
for a long time.9 The two major material classes of SEs have
been oxides (e.g., Li-garnets,10−12 and Li-NASICONs13,14) and
sulfides (e.g., Li-argyrodites,15−18 thio-LISICONs,19−21 LGPS
family,22−25 and Na3PhS4
26,27). In general, oxide electrolytes
have better oxidation stability and a more rigid mechanical
property but require high-temperature sintering to create
sufficient ion-transport pathways.28 More polarizable sulfide
electrolytes tend to exhibit a higher ionic conductivity and
require only pressure to be sintered at room temperature;
however, they possess limited electrochemical stability.29,30
The insulating nature of sulfur-active materials requires a
significant number of triple-phase boundaries in a cathode
composite, where lithium ions and electrons simultaneously
meet sulfur for an electrochemical reaction. The need for
sintering and sulfur’s low melting point limits the use of oxide
electrolytes to specially designed cathode structures, such as a
three-dimensional bilayer garnet framework with sulfur
infiltration.31 While a sulfur composite cathode can be easily
obtained with sulfide electrolytes through mechanical or gas-

Emerging halide electrolytes, e.g., Li3YCl6, Li3InCl6, and
NaTaCl6, possess higher oxidation stability than sulfides and
softer mechanical properties than oxides.34−37 Their high
oxidation stability enabled the cell with LiCoO2 to cycle well
with a high initial Coulomb efficiency.34 Using such stable SEs
may sustain the highly conductive pathways even upon
charging in sulfur composite cathodes; however, the only
halide-based sulfur cathode was fabricated with Li3HoBr6, and
the mechanistic understanding of its poor cycle efficiency has
not been clarified.38
Inspired by the promising transport properties and oxidation
stabilities of halide electrolytes, here, we investigate their
compatibility with sulfur cathodes. Three halide SEs, Li3InCl6,
Li3YCl6, and Li3YBr6, are selected as the model electrolytes. To
assess their practical stability in the sulfur composite cathode
conditions, these three electrolytes are mechanically mixed
with carbon through ball milling to establish intimate contact
with electron-conducting media. While Li3InCl6 exhibits poor
reduction stability that can cause severe degradation even
within the operation voltage of the Li−S battery, the effective
ion-transport measurement of the composite with Li3YCl6 and
Li3YBr6 electrolytes demonstrates the well-maintained effective
ion transport within the composite around the redox potential
of sulfur. Yet, the fabricated solid-state Li−S battery cell with
the cathode composite, composed of Li3YCl6, carbon, and
sulfur, exhibits very poor cyclability. In contrast, a significantly
improved cycle life is achieved with Li3YBr6. The decom-
position energy calculation and postannealing experiments
reveal the chemical incompatibility of halide-based electrolytes
and sulfur-active materials, particularly Li2S. These findings
allow us to understand the possible degradation of halide
electrolytes in contact with sulfur and help us develop cathode
design strategies for the future use of halide SEs.
2. METHODS
2.1. Materials and Composite Electrode Preparation.
2.1.1. Solid Electrolytes. All of the halide electrolytes were prepared
by mechanochemical synthesis. Due to the sensitivity to air and
moisture, all of the sample handlings were done under an Ar
protective atmosphere. The precursor of Li3InCl6 was prepared by
mixing commercially available LiCl (99.9%, Kojundo Chemical
Laboratory Co., Ltd.) and InCl3 (99.999%, Sigma-Aldrich) in the
appropriate stoichiometric ratio. Similarly, the precursor of Li3YCl6
was prepared by mixing commercially available LiCl and YCl3 (99.9%,
Kojundo Chemical Laboratory Co., Ltd.), and the precursor of
Li3YBr6 was prepared by mixing commercially available LiBr (99.9%,
Sigma-Aldrich) and YBr3 (99.9%, Alfa Aesar). The mass ratio of the
ZrO2 milling media to the precursor powder was 30:1, and the mill
was carried out for 99 cycles each (one cycle = 15 min milling at 500
rpm, followed by 5 min rest) in 80 mL ZrO2 milling cups using
PULVERISETTE 7 premium line (Fritsch GmbH). The separator
Li6PS5Cl was synthesized through a solid-state reaction by mixing the
precursors (LiCl, Li2S, and P2S5) with a mortar and pestle, followed
by palletization of the obtained mixture with a single axle press. The
reaction was conducted in a vacuum-sealed ampule with a heating rate
of 100 °C/h from 25 to 550 °C, and the temperature was held at 550
°C for 7 days before turning off the heater power.
The samples were investigated by X-ray diffraction (XRD) to
evaluate their purity and to calibrate the composite using a D8
ADVANCE (Bruker AXS GmbH, Karlsruhe, Germany) in the lab.
The measurements were performed using Cu Kα radiation, and a
domed sample cover was used to prevent atmospheric exposure.
2.1.2. SE-C Composite. The SE-carbon (C) composites with the
mass ratios of m(SE):m(C) = 90:10 were obtained by ball milling the
SE and the conductive additive (ECP600JD, LION). The milling was
conducted with a total of 200 mg of SE-C mixture with 30 g of ZrO2
milling media with a diameter of 5 mm at 300 rpm for 4 h. A 10 min
break was taken every 10 min of milling.
2.1.3. SE-C-S Composite. The SE-C-sulfur (S) composite was
produced by two-step milling. First, a sulfur−carbon (ECP600JD)
composite was obtained by ball milling at 600 rpm for 20 cycles (one
cycle = 5 min of milling, followed by 15 min of rest) at a weight ratio
of 1:1. It was then mixed with SE at 300 rpm for 2 h to achieve finally
m(SE):m(C):m(S) = 3:1:1. Each of the three SEs used in this study
has different densities, SE volumes, and surface area. Therefore, we
followed the above-described procedure to prepare an exact volume
ratio for each halide SE based on Li6PS5Cl; the sulfide SE used in ref
30 is used in this study. The effective ion transport of the prepared
composite was confirmed by electron-blocking ion-transport measure-
ment with DC current, as shown in Figure S1. The effective ionic
conductivity of 1.6 × 10−5 S cm−1 is obtained in the composite of
Li3YBr6-C-S.
2.2. Temperature-Dependent Impedance Spectroscopy.
The total ionic conductivities and activation energies of the obtained
samples were measured by temperature-dependent impedance
spectroscopy. Impedance measurement was performed by packing
the sample powder in a custom-made press cell that maintains the
external pressure during the measurement. Aluminum foils were
placed as current collectors between the sample powder and the
stainless rods. All cells were constructed in a glovebox under an argon
atmosphere, and the samples inside the press cell were sealed by an
O-ring to avoid air exposure. The cell pressure of about 10 N m
(which equals about 60 MPa) was applied with a torque screw
attached to the external duralumin frame. The constructed cells were
connected to a VMP3 impedance analyzer (Biologics) and placed in a
temperature-controlled climate chamber (Espec). The set voltage
amplitude was 20 mV, and the frequency range was from 1 MHz to
100 mHz. The temperature was set in the range from −40 to 60 °C
with 10 °C steps. An additional measurement at room temperature of
25 °C was performed. The measured spectra were fitted by equivalent
circuits using Relaxis3 software (rhd instruments).
2.3. Analysis of Electrochemical Stability of Solid Electro-
lytes. The potential operating window of the solid electrolyte was
measured by Linear Sweep Voltammetry (LSV). The cell was built by
using 12 mg of a fabricated SE-C composite as the positive electrode,
80 mg of argyrodite (Li6PS5Cl) as the separator, and In−Li foil as the
negative electrode. Measurements were made in the 0−5 V range with
a constraining pressure of 10 N m and a voltage sweep rate of 0.05
mV s−1. Two cells were built for each SE-C composite, and the
voltage was swept from the OCV to the high and low potential sides.
2.4. Potential-Dependent Impedance Spectroscopy. In
addition to LSV, the electrochemical stability was further investigated
by measuring the resistance at each voltage. Two identical cells with
the LSV configuration were prepared. One was used for the reduction
test, and the other for the oxidation test. During the measurements,
the working electrode’s potential was scanned from the OCV to the
target reversal potential and kept for 12 h to let the degradation
proceed. The delivered or consumed charges were monitored to
assess the degree of material degradation. The impedance measure-
ments were conducted after one h of cell relaxation after being
subjected to the reversal potential. The whole process was undertaken
to provide insight into how reductive and oxidative degradation
influences the overall transport of the composites. The data obtained
from these measurements were fitted with a Transmission Line Model
(TLM) by using Relaxis3 software.
2.5. Cycling Tests. To verify the performance of halide SEs as a
cathode electrolyte, a battery cell was prepared using the SE-C-S

spectra, and 50 eV was utilized for survey scans. All spectra were
calibrated based on the C 1s line corresponding to adventitious
aliphatic carbon to the binding energy of 284.8 eV. CasaXPS software
was used for the data analysis.

3.1. Investigation of Conductivity and Electrochem-

Li3YCl6, and Li3YBr6, were selected as model SEs and
synthesized through a mechanochemical synthesis. Figure

be limited.43 Among the three halide SEs here, Li3InCl6
experiences particularly severe reductive decomposition, as
predicted by theoretical calculation. As this reduction occurs
above the typical lower cutoff potential of Li−S battery cycling,
this reductive decomposition of Li3InCl6 is expected to largely
impact the cycling performance of Li−S batteries. As for the Y
analogues, we observe a less severe but observable reaction
current, even within the predicted stability window. This small
reaction current could be due to lithium being absorbed into
the amorphous carbon employed in this study, but the exact
causation is unknown.

3.4. Degradation Mechanisms of the Li3YCl6-C-S
Cathode. To further explore the origin of the capacity loss
in the composites with halide SEs, the effect of the temperature
on the cyclability and stability of the cathode components was
investigated. Although the electrochemical stability of Li3YCl6
is superior to that of Li3YBr6, the cyclability of the cathode
composite with Li3YBr6 significantly outperforms the one with
Li3YCl6. Since Li3YBr6 possesses higher ionic conductivity, the
impact of the ion-transport kinetics was assessed by cycling the
cells at an elevated temperature (60 °C). Whereas the initial
discharge capacity increases slightly at 60 °C, it is very evident
that the elevated temperature accelerates the capacity fade
(Figure 5). As for the case with Li3YCl6, the critical difference
between room temperature and 60 °C is in the conversion
efficiencies (Figure 5b), which are low at room temperature
and further deteriorate at 60 °C. Interestingly, the cathode
composite with Li3YBr6 shows only a slight deterioration at an
elevated temperature.
Such intensified degradation, seen particularly with Li3YCl6,
implies that the low cyclability of Li−S composites with halide
SEs is not due to their inferior ionic conductivity to sulfides.

To address the chemical compatibility of halide SEs in the
cathode composite, the materials in the charged state (SE-C-S)
and discharged state (SE-C-Li2S) were prepared by ball
milling, and the fractions of the phases were examined by XRD

the SE-C-S composite, those peaks completely disappear in the
annealed Li3YCl6−Li2S composite. This implies poor chemical
compatibility of Li3YCl6 and Li2S. On the other hand, the
characteristic peaks of Li3YBr6 are still observable even after
being annealed with Li2S.
Following the compatibility test with ball milling, a possible
degradation reaction pathway was explored by annealing

It should be noted that the strong chemical reaction between

is the critical reason for the capacity loss (Figure S6).

nisms mentioned above, the XPS analysis was performed for
the Li3YCl6 cathode composite at the pristine state, after initial
discharge, after the first charge, and after the 20th charge, as
shown in Figure S7. While the reduced sulfur (S2−) could

unreacted S2−suggests the loss of capacity primarily upon
charging, which is consistent with the low conversion efficiency
observed in Figure 4b. Such loss can be due to the residual Li2S
on the current collector sides, as seen in the cathode

due to the formation of metal sulfides, as proposed in the
recent report.47,48 Yet, XPS analysis cannot conclude if the S2−

peaks of S 2p and Y 3d.

4. CONCLUSIONS
This study investigated the electrochemical and chemical
stabilities of three halide solid electrolytes (Li3InCl6, Li3YCl6,
and Li3YBr6) in cathode composites for Li−S batteries. The
electrochemical stability of these halide SEs was demonstrated
in SE-C composites, showing oxidation resistance even with
high interfacial area density with carbons. Li3YCl6 and Li3YBr6
exhibited high macroscopic ionic conductivity within the
operating voltage range of the Li−S batteries. Despite the
promising results from the SE-C composites, the cycling
performance of the cell, especially with Li3YCl6, exhibited poor
cyclability due to the chemical incompatibility with Li2S. This
incompatibility was attributed to anion exchange forming Y−S
bonds, as evidenced by the formation of LiYS2 and LiCl
through X-ray diffraction on the postannealed samples. The
use of Li3YBr6 mitigated this parasitic reaction, likely due to
lower decomposition energy and slower diffusion kinetics at
the interface. These findings suggest that employing chemically
distinct anions at the interface may be a viable strategy to
address chemical incompatibility issues within cathode
composites for Li−S batteries.
