ABSTRACT
Despite the exceptionally high theoretical energy density of all-solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs), their practical
performance is severely limited by discontinuous interfacial contact and sluggish transport of electrons and lithium ions. These
issues cause accumulation of locally electrochemically inactive sulfur, resulting in electrode passivation and uneven reaction
distribution, which are critical bottlenecks to performance improvement. In recent years, redox mediators (RMs) have garnered
significant attention due to their ability to effectively regulate complex multiphase reaction processes in liquid-phase lithium–
sulfur (Li–S) systems. However, whether RMs can enable efficient and closed-loop redox cycling under solid–state conditions
remains a central challenge. To address this issue, this review systematically summarizes and compares sulfur conversion pathways
across different electrolyte systems, with a particular focus on the mechanistic roles of RMs in regulating the transformation of
sulfur species in ASSLSBs. It further discusses the relationship between the molecular structures of representative RMs and their
electrochemical performance. The key scientific problems and engineering challenges currently facing the field are identified.
The review outlines future directions for molecular designs of tunable RMs and a deeper understanding of redox mechanisms in
solid–state environments to provide theoretical guidance and design strategies for high–performance ASSLSBs.
1
Introduction
All-solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) have attracted
considerable attention as highly promising next-generation high-
energy-density energy storage systems owing to the sulfur
cathode’s ultrahigh theoretical capacity, natural abundance, and
low cost [1–3]. However, their practical electrochemical perfor-
mance still falls far short of theoretical expectations [4–10]. The
fundamental bottleneck lies in the intrinsically sluggish redox
kinetics under solid-state conditions and the severely constrained
charge transport pathways.
In liquid electrolyte lithium-sulfur batteries (LSBs), effective
electrolyte wetting creates continuous electronic and lithium-
ion transport pathways among the active materials, electronic
conductors, and electrolytes. This enables efficient and homo-
geneous utilization of sulfur species [11–17]. In contrast, solid
electrolytes are unable to effectively infiltrate porous cathode
architectures, confining sulfur conversion reactions to the three-
phase boundaries where sulfur, conductive additives, and solid
electrolytes coexist [18–22]. This solid-state reaction characteristic
not only leads to pronounced polarization but also induces
highly heterogeneous reaction distributions, thereby promoting
the localized accumulation of electrochemically inactive sulfur or
Li2S [23–28]. Such accumulation ultimately results in electrode
passivation and reduced sulfur utilization [29–31]. Therefore,
restricted charge transport and interfacial failure constitute the
most fundamental scientific challenges governing solid-state

2
Sulfur Conversion Pathways in Liquid Versus
Solid-State Lithium–Sulfur Batteries
How can ASSLSBs truly realize their ultrahigh theoretical energy
density when the sulfur conversion pathways under solid-state
conditions remain unresolved? In liquid Li–S systems, the “solid–
liquid–solid” reduction mechanism has been well established,
corresponding to two typical voltage plateaus, but directly apply-
ing this framework to solid-state batteries is questionable. The
phase transition characteristics, stability of intermediates, and
voltage responses in ASSLSBs differ fundamentally from those in
liquid systems, yet current understanding remains highly limited.
Unless these critical issues are addressed, the intrinsic energy
advantage of ASSLSBs will be difficult to achieve in practice.
From a thermodynamic perspective, the formation of voltage
plateaus is primarily associated with phase transitions. During
two-phase coexistence, the Gibbs free energy (ΔG) of the system
remains nearly constant, leading to a stabilized voltage. In
contrast, in solid-solution reactions, the free energy changes
smoothly with ion concentration without distinct phase transi-
tions, and thus voltage plateaus are generally absent. Therefore,
the appearance of a voltage plateau is closely related to whether
the material undergoes a pronounced phase transition and its
associated thermodynamic behavior. Sun and colleagues pro-
vided a fundamental overview of all-solid-state Li–S batteries
through an in-depth study of the basic redox mechanisms of
solid-state sulfur [42].
Figure 2 systematically compares the differences in sulfur reduc-
tion pathways and their corresponding thermodynamic behaviors
between liquid and solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries. In con-
ventional liquid Li–S batteries, sulfur reduction follows a typical
“solid–liquid–solid” mechanism, characterized by a stepwise
decrease in free energy. During discharge, elemental sulfur (S8)
is gradually converted into a series of soluble lithium polysulfide
(LiPS) intermediates (e.g., Li2S8, Li2S6, Li2S4, Li2S2), ultimately
forming insoluble Li2S. Each key transformation approaches
thermodynamic equilibrium, resulting in a relatively smooth
change in ΔG and a distinct two-plateau voltage profile.
In contrast, the sulfur conversion mechanism in ASSLSBs
remains unclear. On the right side of the figure, a cubic “black
box” represents the potentially complex and poorly understood
reaction pathways in solid-state systems. Some intermediates

standpoint, solid-state sulfur conversion offers superior theoret-

like kinetics and reversibility through material and structural
innovations [55].
3
Basic Definition and Mediation Mechanisms
of RM
Redox mediators are a class of molecules, polymers, ions, or
compounds that can undergo reversible redox transformations
during electrochemical cycling [56–58]. Their core function is
to mediate the transfer of electron or hole equivalents [59–61].
Owing to their structural and functional regenerability, RMs
generally do not alter the final reaction products, but they
can effectively overcome the sluggish charge-transfer kinetics
caused by the intrinsic insulating nature of sulfur species [62]. In
addition, by regulating the reaction pathways and the conversion
of sulfur intermediates, RMs can make the conversion process
more controllable and lower the reaction energy barriers, thereby
suppressing side reactions and improving the overall reversibility
and structural stability of the system [63].

Importantly, the mediation behavior of RMs is closely associated
with their physical state within the battery system. In liquid
electrolyte systems, soluble RMs can mediate the transfer of
electron or hole equivalents between electronically accessible
electrode surfaces and sulfur species through diffusion-assisted
homogeneous reactions. In contrast, in solid-state lithium–sulfur
batteries, the system lacks continuous liquid-phase mass trans-
port and is constrained by limited solid–solid contact, restricted
molecular diffusion, and interfacial charge-transfer resistance.
Therefore, the liquid-phase picture that relies on macroscopically
long-range diffusion of RM molecules cannot be directly applied
to solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries. In solid-state environ-
ments, RM mediation should instead be described as interfacial
confinement and localized redox-state relay. Therefore, the medi-
ation role of RMs must be reinterpreted by considering the
specific constraints of solid-state electrochemical systems.
3.1
Solid-State Localized Redox-State Relay
Mediation
The incorporation of RMs into solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries
can construct localized RMox/RMred redox-state relay channels at
solid–solid reaction interfaces, thereby promoting the transfer of
electron or hole equivalents between the conductive network and
adjacent sulfur/Li2S domains [64]. This RM-mediated process
should be clearly distinguished from the diffusion-dominated
redox shuttle mechanism in liquid Li–S batteries. In solid-state
cathodes, the absence of continuous liquid-phase mass transport
makes it difficult for RMs to undergo macroscopically long-range,
round-trip migration between the carbon matrix and sulfur or
Li2S particle surfaces [65]. Therefore, RMs in ASSLSBs should
be regarded as spatially confined redox-active sites rather than
molecular shuttles capable of long-range migration.
As shown in Figure 4a,b, this process can establish a localized
redox-state relay chain among solid particles, thereby enhancing
nanoscale charge coupling between the conductive network and
neighboring sulfur/Li2S domains, while reducing the dependence
on continuous three-phase boundaries and ideal intimate solid–
solid contact [66]. Specifically, RM sites electronically coupled
with the conductive network first undergo local electrochem-
ical oxidation or reduction, forming RMox or RMred states
[67]. Therefore, the truly transported species in this process
are electron or hole equivalents, rather than RM molecules
themselves.
The localized redox-state regeneration of RMs can be generally
expressed as:
RMox + ne−⇌RMred
In this configuration, RMs generally exist as insoluble, weakly
soluble, polymer-immobilized, framework-confined, or solid-
catholyte-integrated redox-active sites located inside the sulfur
cathode, near conductive carbon domains, or at sulfur/solid-
state electrolyte (SSE) interfaces. Through nanoscale solid–solid
contact, interfacial redox-state relay, and localized electronic
coupling, these RM sites promote cathode-side charge transfer
[68]. This mechanism can effectively bypass the direct electron-
injection barrier caused by the intrinsic insulating nature of
sulfur and Li2S, while avoiding the uncontrolled shuttle behavior
associated with long-range RM migration in liquid systems.
To achieve efficient mediation, RMs should possess redox
potentials matched with sulfur/Li2S conversion, strong electronic
coupling with the conductive network, sufficient local contact
with sulfur species, and good chemical compatibility with the
SSE.
It should be emphasized that the function of RMs is not to
increase the electronic conductivity of the entire solid electrolyte,
but to provide reversible RMox/RMred redox-state relay at cathode-
side localized interfaces [69]. Properly designed RMs should
remain spatially confined and maintain low bulk electronic
conductivity, so that they only promote local charge transfer
between conductive domains and adjacent sulfur/Li2S regions
without forming continuous electronic pathways across the solid
electrolyte. Therefore, under proper spatial confinement, RMs
do not directly induce lithium dendrite formation. However, if
RMs react with the SSE, exhibit excessive electronic conduc-
tivity, or form continuous RM-derived electronic percolation
pathways, interfacial electronic leakage may occur and indirectly
disturb local Li+ deposition behavior. Accordingly, RM design
in ASSLSBs should simultaneously consider redox reversibility,
spatial confinement, interfacial compatibility, and suppression of
electronic leakage.
Therefore, to match the localized redox-state relay mediation
mechanism in ASSLSBs, RMs are preferably located in cathode-
side regions, including: (1) inside the sulfur composite cath-
ode, where they bridge electronically inaccessible insulating
domains and activate deeply embedded sulfur species; (2) at
sulfur/SSE interfaces, where they improve solid–solid contact and
lower interfacial electron-transfer barriers; (3) near conductive
network/SSE contact regions, where they construct localized
electron-ion coupling nodes and expand the effective reaction
interface. Regardless of their specific location, the essential
transport picture should be understood as localized RMox/RMred
redox-state relay rather than macroscopic migration of RM
molecules.
3.2
Chemical Mediation Mechanisms: Reactant
Activation and Reaction Pathway Reconstruction
In ASSLSBs, mediated electron transfer (MET) can only enable
indirect electron transport but is fundamentally incapable of
overcoming the intrinsically high reaction barriers of solid sulfur
species or addressing the “pathway interruption” caused by
product accumulation and the absence of soluble intermediates
at solid–solid interfaces. Consequently, chemically regulated RMs
have emerged as a key strategy to enhance the reaction kinetics
of solid-state sulfur conversion. The essence of their chemi-
cal mediation lies in enabling reaction pathway reconstruction
through reversible chemical interactions between RMs and solid
sulfur species. As shown in Figure 5, under this mechanism, the
direct conversion of “solid sulfur species →targeted products” is
replaced by a continuous sequence of “solid sulfur species→RM–
sulfur composite intermediates→targeted products.” This not
only weakens the structural stability of solid sulfur species
through chemical bond activation and lowers the associated
reaction barriers, but also introduces intermediate phases that

Reactant activation mediation focuses on enhancing the intrinsic

Through reversible coordination, hydrogen bonding interactions,
polarization regulation, or electron density redistribution, RMs
can modify the chemical state of sulfur species and substantially
reduce the delithiation barrier of Li2S, thereby accelerating the
conversion of solid sulfur phases [71, 72].
In contrast, reaction pathway reconstruction mediation repre-
sents a deeper level of chemical intervention. Because solid
electrolytes cannot sustain the long-chain LiPSs commonly found
in liquid systems, the traditional S8→Li2Sn→Li2S sequence often
fails to proceed in the solid-state, leading to severely hindered
sulfur conversion [73]. Through reversible reactions with sulfur-
containing species, RMs generate new sulfur-based intermedi-

constructing an alternative reaction pathway with a lower energy
barrier that is better suited to solid-state environments [74]. This
mechanism effectively “rewrites the sulfur conversion sequence,”
bypassing the energy-intensive steps and solid-state diffusion
constraints that otherwise hinder deep sulfur reduction and the
reversible oxidation of Li2S.
Overall, reactant activation mediation primarily addresses the
poor intrinsic reactivity of solid sulfur species, whereas path-
way reconstruction mediation resolves the inability of the
solid-state system to sustain the conventional sulfur reaction
sequence [75]. These two modes respectively target the core
limitations of low intrinsic reactivity and missing interme-
diates in ASSLSBs. In situations where MET cannot alter
the chemical nature of solid sulfur species or overcome the
interruption of the conversion pathway, these chemical medi-
ation mechanisms provide indispensable means to improve
solid–solid interfacial kinetics and enable high sulfur uti-
lization, high reversibility, and long-term cycling stability in
ASSLSBs.
3.3
Space-Charge Regulation and RM-Induced
Interfacial Charge Redistribution
In addition to localized redox-state relay, regulating space-charge
effects at solid–solid interfaces is also important for understand-
ing RM-mediated solid-state lithium–sulfur chemistry. When
a solid electrolyte comes into contact with active materials,
conductive phases, or interfacial components in a composite
cathode, differences in electrochemical potential, Li+ chemi-
cal potential, and interfacial electronic structure may induce
the formation of a space charge layer (SCL), accompanied
by Li+ enrichment or depletion, interfacial potential bending,
and a built-in electric field [76, 77]. In particular, when a
Li+-depleted SCL forms at the interface, the Li+ migration
barrier across the cathode/electrolyte interface increases, which
may further lead to nonuniform sulfur/Li2S conversion, espe-
cially in poorly contacted S/Li2S–solid electrolyte interfacial
regions [78].

Previous studies on solid-state battery interface engineering
have shown that SCL can be regulated by balancing interfa-
cial electrochemical potentials, modulating interfacial electric
fields, optimizing defect concentrations, and reconstructing Li+
transport pathways. Among these strategies, studies on strong-
dipole or polar groups have shown that local polar environments
can regulate charge transfer, promote Li+ migration, and sta-
bilize interfacial chemistry [79]. Based on this understanding,
RMs containing strongly polar functional groups can reversibly
interact with Li+ and influence the local Li+ coordination
environment and migration behavior near solid-state interfaces,
thereby synergistically promoting S/Li2S conversion [80]. Such
molecular-scale Li+ coordination and migration regulation also
provides design guidance for further modulating SCL-related
interfacial Li+ redistribution.
In addition, inorganic oxide doping or halogen-component incor-
poration can regulate local defect chemistry, interfacial chemical
potentials, and charge distribution from the perspective of mate-
rial composition, thereby alleviating SCL-induced interfacial
polarization [81, 82]. Unlike conventional inorganic doping, inor-
ganic RMs or halogen-type redox-active components place greater
emphasis on reversible valence-state evolution and local charge
buffering. In spatially confined solid–solid interfacial environ-
ments, these redox-active sites can realize local charge transfer
through reversible redox-state cycling, thereby improving S/Li2S
conversion. A typical example is the iodine/iodide system, in
which reversible I−/I2/I3
−redox chemistry can function as an
RM at solid-state interfaces, promoting sluggish sulfur conversion
reactions and providing design inspiration for further regulating
interfacial charge distribution and SCL evolution.
It should be emphasized that the goal of space-charge regulation
should not be simply understood as the complete elimination of
SCL. Instead, it should be to tune the Li+ enrichment/depletion
state, carrier concentration, and potential gradient within the
SCL, thereby mitigating its barrier effect and, where favorable,
generating Li+-rich or low-barrier interfacial regions that facili-
tate Li+ migration and reactant conversion. This understanding
provides new insights into the molecular and structural design of
strongly polar organic RMs, halogen-type RMs, and redox-active
inorganic RMs for SCL-oriented regulation.
4
Differences Between Redox Mediators and
Conventional Catalysts in Solid‑State
Lithium–Sulfur Batteries
All-solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) face several
critical challenges, including the low electrochemical activity
of sulfur cathodes, the intrinsically poor electronic and ionic
transport properties of both sulfur and lithium sulfide (Li2S), and
sluggish electrochemical reaction kinetics at multiphase solid–
solid interfaces. Conventional heterogeneous catalysts generally
lower reaction barriers by regulating surface adsorption and
the binding strength of reaction intermediates. However, the
effectiveness of such “anchoring-type” catalysts is often limited
in solid-state systems, not only because mass transport and
interfacial contact are restricted, but also because fixed surface
active sites cannot readily adapt to the continuously evolving
interfacial structures and reaction environments during S/Li2S
conversion [83].
Unlike conventional catalysts, RMs can not only establish
dynamic charge-transfer pathways within confined solid–solid
interfacial environments through the reversible interconversion
of RMox/RMred, but also undergo reversible chemical coupling
with neighboring sulfur species to form short-lived RM–S inter-
mediates, thereby reconstructing the S8/Li2S conversion pathway.
Therefore, the essential role of RMs is to establish a closed “elec-
trochemical regeneration-chemical mediation” cycle that trans-
forms an originally direct, high-barrier solid–solid conversion
into a reversible mediated pathway consisting of multiple lower-
barrier steps, thereby alleviating reaction retardation caused by
the limited availability of conventional triple-phase boundaries.
From the perspective of electron-transfer mechanisms, RM-
mediated processes can be understood in terms of inner-sphere
and outer-sphere electron-transfer pathways (Figure 6a,b) [84,
85]. During inner-sphere electron transfer, transient coordination
structures, bridging configurations, or chemical bonds may form
between the RM and sulfur species. Electrons are then transferred
through the resulting bridging structure, often accompanied by
bond formation or cleavage. This mechanism generally provides
relatively strong orbital coupling, thereby enhancing electron
transfer and facilitating Li2S decomposition or sulfur-species
conversion.
By contrast, outer-sphere electron transfer does not involve
direct bond formation. When the energy levels of the donor
and acceptor are appropriately matched, electron transfer can
proceed through through-space tunneling or nonbonding orbital
coupling, with the electrode or RM acting primarily as an electron
donor or acceptor [87]. This process can generally be described
using Marcus theory:

5.2
Inorganic Redox Mediators
In general, inorganic RMs (iRMs) are inorganic species pos-
sessing reversible valence evolution or multielectron transfer
capability, whose activity originates from reversible electronic
rearrangements at anionic groups or metal centers during redox
processes [102]. According to their chemical composition and
reaction characteristics, iRMs can be broadly classified into three
categories: halogen and polyhalide mediators, multivalent metal
sulfide/oxide mediators, and polyanionic or composite inorganic
framework mediators [103–105].
Distinct from conventional solid-state catalysts that rely on fixed
active sites to stabilize reaction transition states, the fundamental
mechanism of iRMs is based on MET driven by reversible
valence changes [106]. Through their intrinsic redox cycling,
iRMs can act as dynamic electron or hole carriers between
the electrode and sulfur species, thereby reconstructing electron
transfer pathways and decomposing a single high-energy direct
electron injection step into multiple consecutive steps with lower
activation barriers [107–109]. Meanwhile, certain inorganic medi-
ators generate short-lived oxidized or metastable intermediate
species during cycling, which can directly activate elemental
sulfur or Li2S via chemical oxidation or reduction, enabling
chemical promotion of sulfur conversion rather than relying
solely on direct electrochemical reactions at localized electrode
interfaces. In addition, many iRMs intrinsically exhibit partial
ionic or electronic conductivity, allowing them to simultane-
ously mediate redox reactions and optimize interfacial charge
transport networks, thereby effectively reducing polarization
and suppressing continuous interfacial impedance accumulation
[110].
In ASSLSBs, the absence of liquid-phase diffusion fundamentally
alters the function of iRMs compared with liquid systems. Under
such conditions, iRMs typically exist in interfacially confined,
locally distributed, or embedded forms within sulfur cathodes or
at sulfur/solid–electrolyte interfaces, rather than acting as freely
diffusing homogeneous electron shuttles. In this solid-state reac-
tion environment, inorganic redox mediators can extend sulfur
conversion reactions from the highly restricted “solid electrolyte
| sulfur | conductive additive (SE|S|C)” three-phase interface
to two-phase reaction interfaces such as “SE|Li2S” or “SE|S”,
significantly increasing the accessibility of active reaction sites
and activating sulfur domains that are otherwise electronically or
ionically inaccessible [111]. By virtue of a coupled electrochemical
oxidation/reduction chemical conversion mechanism, iRMs can
effectively bypass the high activation barriers associated with
direct Li2S oxidation or elemental sulfur reduction, thereby
alleviating critical challenges in ASSLSBs, including difficult
initial activation, severe polarization, and sluggish solid–solid
conversion kinetics [112].
Based on these characteristics, different classes of inorganic RMs
exhibit distinct mediation behaviors and functional advantages
in ASSLSBs. Specifically, halogen and polyhalide mediators (e.g.,
I−/I2/I3
−and Br−/Br3
−couples) enable cross-interface electron
transfer and chemical oxidation of Li2S through reversible halo-
gen valence cycling, thereby overcoming three-phase interface
limitations and accelerating solid-state sulfur conversion kinetics
(Figure 8a,b) [113, 114]. Multivalent metal sulfide/oxide mediators
(such as VS2, WS2, TiS2, In2S3, and metal halides) exploit metal
center valence modulation and mixed ionic-electronic conduc-
tivity to construct continuous charge transport networks within
the cathode while catalyzing the transformation of insulating
by-products into conductive phases, thus enhancing sulfur uti-
lization and stabilizing solid electrolyte interfaces. In contrast,
polyanionic or composite inorganic framework mediators (e.g.,
LPSI, LBPSI) derive their redox activity from reversible structural
units such as S─S, P─S, or X─S (X = I, Br) linkages and simulta-
neously function as both solid electrolytes and RMs (Figure 8c).
Through distributed interfacial activation and structural flexi-
bility, these materials systematically improve sulfur conversion
reversibility, cycling stability, and high-rate performance in solid-
state systems [115–117].
Notably, transition-metal-based inorganic RMs with relatively
high electronic conductivity, such as VS2, WS2, and TiS2, also
introduce a significant performance-safety trade-off. Moderate
localized electronic conduction can improve interfacial charge
transfer and expand the effective reaction region. However,
when the RM loading is excessive or when a continuous elec-
tronic percolation network forms at the cathode/solid–electrolyte
interface, it may trigger solid–electrolyte decomposition and
self-discharge [118]. More seriously, if the electronic conduction
pathway extends into the solid electrolyte, it may induce local
lithium deposition and increase the risk of soft short circuits,
lithium filaments, or dendrite growth [119]. Therefore, the design
of transition-metal-based inorganic RMs should balance localized
charge-transfer capability with long-range electronic insulation.

Redox Mediators in All-Solid-State

In all-solid-state systems, RMs no longer function as freely
diffusing homogeneous electron shuttles [120, 121]. Owing to
their reversible oxidized/reduced cycling and transient chemical
or electrochemical interactions with sulfur species, RMs can
effectively bypass the high-energy barrier associated with direct
electron injection into electrically insulating sulfur or Li2S. In
doing so, they activate kinetically hindered solid-state reactions
and reconstruct electron-transfer pathways that are more compat-

conventional solid-state catalysts that rely on surface adsorption
and transition-state stabilization, RMs exhibit a distinctive advan-
tage in alleviating the intrinsic kinetic limitations of all-solid-state
lithium–sulfur systems.

depends on the chemical starting point and conversion pathway

conversion process. In contrast, for lithium sulfide cathodes,
where electrochemical reactions initiate from a fully reduced Li2S
phase, RMs predominantly function as oxidative electron accep-

material utilization. Redox mediators fundamentally alleviate
these bottlenecks by coupling reversible RMox/RMred cycling

phases, RMs markedly enhance Li2S activation, reversibility, and
interfacial kinetics in the solid-state environments.

7
Design Principles for Redox Mediators in
Solid-State Lithium–Sulfur Batteries
In solid-state environments, RMs are required to function within
confined geometries and discontinuous interfacial contacts,
where both electron and ion transport are highly localized.
Their effectiveness, therefore, depends not only on their intrinsic
redox activity, but also on their ability to sustain reversible
redox cycling, interfacial regeneration, and chemical stability
under solid-state constraints. Based on these considerations, the
following sections summarize the key solid-state-specific design
criteria for RMs in ASSLSBs.
7.1
Design Criteria for Redox Mediators—Redox
Potential Matching
Redox potential matching remains a necessary but insufficient
criterion for the design of RMs in all-solid-state lithium–sulfur
batteries. Unlike liquid systems, where appropriately matched
redox potentials mainly ensure thermodynamic driving force and
reaction selectivity, in solid-state systems, they additionally deter-
mine whether RMs can undergo electrochemical regeneration at
confined solid–solid interfaces [131].
Specifically, the redox potential of an RM must be carefully
positioned within the narrow electrochemical stability win-
dow of solid electrolytes, particularly sulfide-based electrolytes,
to avoid interfacial decomposition or parasitic side reactions.
Moreover, excessively high or low redox potentials may induce
irreversible oxidation or reduction of RMs at localized interfaces,
thereby interrupting the closed redox cycle and leading to RM
deactivation.
Figure 11 presents a Sankey diagram summarizing representative
RMs and their redox potential distribution relative to the sulfur
redox couples [133]. Although an overall potential window of
approximately 1.8–2.5 V is thermodynamically compatible with
sulfur conversion reactions, only a subset of RMs simultaneously
satisfies the requirements of solid electrolyte stability and inter-
facial regenerability. In this context, RMs with relatively lower
redox potentials are more suitable for promoting stepwise sulfur
reduction during discharge, whereas those with moderately
higher yet electrolyte-compatible potentials are more effective
in facilitating Li2S oxidation during charging. Importantly, in
ASSLSBs, redox potential matching must be evaluated in con-
junction with interfacial electrochemical accessibility, rather
than being treated as an isolated thermodynamic parameter
[134]. Beyond static potential matching, emerging concepts
such as electrochemical wavefunction engineering—tailoring the
HOMO/LUMO of RMs to hybridize with sulfur p-orbitals—offer
a promising direction for future design.
7.2
Design Criteria for Electrochemical Stability
and Kinetics of Redox Mediators
In all-solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries, the performance of
RMs is critically governed by their regeneration kinetics and long-
term stability under severely constrained molecular diffusion
conditions. Unlike liquid electrolytes, where RMs can freely
diffuse to access fresh reaction sites, the solid-state environment
imposes strong spatial confinement, necessitating rapid and
highly localized redox reactions.
Accordingly, RMs must exhibit low activation energy barriers and
fast electron transfer kinetics to ensure timely electrochemical
regeneration within confined interfacial regions. Insufficient
kinetics would lead to the accumulation of electrochemically
inactive RM species, effectively terminating the mediated redox
cycle and exacerbating interfacial polarization [135].
Equally important is the chemical and electrochemical stability
of RMs during repeated solid-state cycling. Given the limited
electrochemical stability windows of most solid electrolytes,
RM redox reactions must remain fully reversible and strictly
confined within these windows to avoid electrolyte degradation
or irreversible RM consumption. This requirement is particularly
stringent for sulfide-based solid electrolytes, which are prone to
oxidative decomposition at elevated potentials.
Moreover, the diffusion-limited environment highlights the
necessity of designing RMs capable of selectively interacting with
solid sulfur species, such as S8 deposits and Li2S domains, to pro-
mote localized activation and conversion. RMs that can stabilize
intermediate species or facilitate homogeneous solid-phase trans-
formations help mitigate reaction heterogeneity and suppress the
formation of electrochemically isolated sulfur domains.

7.3
Cross-System Insights for RM Design
Recent studies on RM–electrolyte synergy in liquid Li–S batteries
provide important cross-system insights for the rational screening
of RMs and interface construction in ASSLSBs [136]. In liquid
systems, the function of RMs is closely related to the electrolyte
solvation structure, polysulfide reaction behavior, cathode-side
sulfur conversion kinetics, and Li-metal anode stability [137].
Therefore, RM design should not only focus on redox potential
and electron-transfer capability, but also consider their synergy
with the electrolyte environment, sulfur-species structure, and
electrode interfaces.
For example, weakly solvating electrolytes can suppress polysul-
fide dissolution and shuttling, thereby mitigating side reactions
at the Li anode, but they also slow cathode-side sulfur conversion
kinetics. Jin et al. showed that capacity decay at high rates
mainly originates from sluggish polysulfide oxidation during
charging, while introducing a suitable RM can accelerate charge
transfer and improve pouch-cell performance [138]. Similarly,
the encapsulating polysulfide electrolyte proposed by Liu et al.
can reduce polysulfide corrosion of the Li anode but sacri-
fices cathode kinetics. After introducing dimethyl diselenide as
a redox comediator, more reactive organopolysulfides can be
generated, promoting both liquid–liquid and liquid–solid sulfur
conversion while retaining the anode-protective function of the
electrolyte [139].
In addition, studies on the structure and properties of lithium
polysulfides indicate that polysulfides are not only soluble inter-
mediates, but also key active species affecting cathode kinetics,
shuttle behavior, Li-anode corrosion, and solid electrolyte inter-
phase evolution. Their dissociation/association, solvation struc-
ture, Li+ coordination, and aggregation behavior can be regulated
through electrolyte composition, ion coordination, molecular
interactions, and redox mediation [140]. This suggests that RM
screening should not rely only on redox-potential matching, but
should also consider their effects on sulfur-species structure, Li+
transport, and interfacial side reactions. Although long-range
polysulfide dissolution and liquid-phase shuttling are largely sup-
pressed in ASSLSBs, these principles remain instructive. Overall,
as schematically summarized in Figure 12, the rational design
of solid-state RMs should integrate redox-potential matching,
dynamic RMox/RMred regeneration, interfacial compatibility, and
cross-system electrolyte-inspired regulation to bridge solid-state
reaction challenges with improved sulfur conversion kinetics and
cycling stability.
8
Summary and Outlook
When can all‑solid‑state lithium–sulfur batteries become com-
mercially viable? As shown in Figure 13, despite their ultrahigh
theoretical energy density and intrinsic safety, ASSLSBs remain
far from practical application. The main bottlenecks include
insufficient ionic conductivity of solid electrolytes, unstable
electrode–electrolyte interfaces, limited cycling stability under
high sulfur loading and harsh operating conditions, as well as
the lack of scalable fabrication processes. The introduction of
RMs offers a new opportunity to overcome kinetic and inter-
facial challenges. Therefore, the commercialization of ASSLSBs
will strongly depend on the synergistic progress of mechanistic
breakthroughs and scalable engineering strategies.

8.1
Summary for Future Solid-State
Lithium–Sulfur Batteries
To address the critical challenges of electronic and ionic transport
bottlenecks at solid–solid interfaces, this review emphasizes an
emerging strategy that utilizes RMs to regulate sulfur redox
pathways at the molecular and electronic levels. In solid-state
environments, RMs can directly participate in the sulfur redox
cycle, enabling cooperative transport of electrons and ions
while effectively suppressing side reactions. This mechanism
establishes a dynamic and self-sustaining conversion network
that integrates electron mediation and ion conduction into a
unified reaction framework, thereby expanding the electrochem-
ically active regions and significantly enhancing charge transfer
efficiency under solid-state conditions.
This review systematically classifies and summarizes representa-
tive RMs, providing a comprehensive analysis of the structure-
function relationships between these RMs and solid-state sulfur
conversion pathways. In addition, key design principles for effec-
tive RMs in all-solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries are proposed,
emphasizing the importance of redox potential, electrochemical
stability, and interfacial compatibility for achieving robust cycling
performance. Overall, this study highlights the critical role of
RMs in improving the redox kinetics and interfacial reactivity of
solid-state sulfur conversion, and the latest advances reviewed
herein provide theoretical insights and strategic guidance for
the development of high-performance and commercially viable
all-solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries.
