Catalysis and Conductive Enhancement in All-

ABSTRACT: The electrochemical performance of all-solid-
state lithium−sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) remains inadequate
for commercial viability due to the inherently low electronic
conductivity of elemental sulfur, which results in sluggish
reaction kinetics. Here, we propose a dual modification strategy
combining doping and catalysis to promote solid-state
conversion reactions in the sulfur cathode. This synergistic
approach has been shown to enhance the electrical conductivity
of the sulfur cathode, thereby improving the reaction kinetics.
Through XPS and 7Li ssNMR analysis, we reveal the sequential
formation and quantitative evolution of various lithium polysulfide intermediates during the sulfur reduction process. The
ASSLSBs demonstrated an areal capacity of 6.3 mAh cm−2 and a capacity retention of 95.9% after 100 cycles. Furthermore, the
ASSLSBs paired with a Li metal anode achieved an electrode energy density of 818.4 Wh kg−1. These findings provide valuable
insights into the solid-phase conversion process of sulfur and establish a promising strategy for developing high-performance
ASSLSBs.
A
ll-solid-state lithium−sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) have
garnered considerable attention due to their high
theoretical energy density, high safety, and long cycling
life.1 However, the low electronic conductivity of both sulfur
and its discharge product, lithium sulfide (Li2S), along with the
severe volume changes, still limit the utilization and
reversibility of active material.2 Various strategies have been
proposed to overcome these limitations, including optimiza-
tion of conductive agents, development of novel solid
electrolytes, and design of appropriate three-phase interfa-
ces.3−7 Nevertheless, the low intrinsic electronic conductivity
of sulfur remains a major obstacle for the efficient utilization of
active material in ASSLSBs, particularly under high-loading
and high-rate conditions.8
To address this limitation, doping with exogenous elements
has emerged as a promising strategy to fundamentally enhance
the electrochemical activity of elemental sulfur, improve its
utilization, and accelerate redox reaction kinetics.9 Recently,
Liu et al. have demonstrated that a S9.3I molecular crystal
exhibits semiconductor-grade conductivity of approximately
5.9 × 10−7 S cm−1 at 25 °C.10 In this system, iodine (I2)
introduces new electronic states within the sulfur bandgap,
facilitating the formation of reactive polysulfides and thereby
enhancing the electrochemical performance of ASSLSBs.
Similarly, tellurium (Te), characterized by its high electronic
conductivity of 2 × 102 S m−1, has been extensively
investigated as an effective dopant for improving the
electrochemical properties of Li−S batteries.11−13 Additionally,
transition metal catalysts have emerged as an effective strategy
to address the sluggish sulfur redox kinetics in ASSLSBs.14,15 In
our previous research, cobalt phthalocyanine (CoPc) was
incorporated into the sulfur cathode, where the atomic-level
CoN4 active sites were demonstrated to facilitate the solid-
phase sulfur conversion via electron transfer mediation and
improve reaction uniformity at the interface, thereby
improving battery performance.16
While substantial efforts have focused on enhancing carrier
transport and electrochemical performance, the fundamental
understanding of conversion reaction processes and mod-
ification mechanisms in sulfur cathodes remains relatively

species, the formation and evolution of polysulfides, and the
role of dopants or catalysts in solid-state conversion remain
elusive. Recent advances in characterization methodologies
have provided crucial insights into these processes. Zhu et al.
demonstrated the formation of Li2S2 during discharge through
in situ Raman spectroscopy in custom-designed cells, revealing
sluggish solid-state conversion among S8, Li2S2, and Li2S,
which results in low active material utilization.18 Furthermore,
Sun et al., using XAS and TOF-SIMS techniques, reported that
the discharge products in ASSLSBs comprise a mixture of Li2S2
and Li2S rather than singular compounds.19 Their research
proposed strategic approaches, including cutoff voltage
regulation and LiI incorporation, to induce Li2S2 as the
predominant product, thereby reducing the formation energy,
enhancing reaction kinetics, and improving electrochemical
performance. More recently, a study employing operando
neutron radiography and in situ neutron tomography
elucidated Li+ transport behavior during cycling, confirming
the detrimental effects of low ionic conductivity and associated
irreversible loss mechanisms.20 These investigations reveal the
multi-step reaction mechanisms and Li+ transport rate-limiting
behaviors in the composite cathodes of ASSLSBs. Therefore,
understanding the Li+ transport mechanism during sulfur
conversion and optimizing composite electrode design are
crucial for accelerating solid-state conversion reactions and
enhancing the overall electrochemical performance of
ASSLSBs.
Here, we demonstrate a dual modification strategy that
synergistically enhances the sulfur conversion reaction kinetics
and active sulfur utilization through Te doping and CoPc
catalysis, resulting in the significantly improved electro-
chemical performance of ASSLSBs. XPS and Raman analyses
reveal a sequential sulfur conversion process, progressing from
elemental sulfur through polysulfide intermediates to the final
Li2S product. Furthermore, using ssNMR spectroscopy, we
directly monitored the evolution of the Li−Sx complex within
the sulfur cathode. As a result, the assembled ASSLSBs
demonstrate exceptional electrochemical performance under
challenging operating conditions.

To elucidate the impact of Te doping on the intrinsic
properties of sulfur, DC techniques were employed to
quantitatively assess the electronic conductivities of both S/
C and TeS/C materials. As shown in Figure S9a-b, the
calculated electronic resistivities for the S/C and TeS/C
samples were determined to be 0.51 and 0.11 Ω, respectively.
The electronic conductivity of the TeS/C composite was
measured at 3.4 × 10−1 S cm−1, which is higher than that of S/
C (7.1 × 10−2 S cm−1) (Figure 2a). In addition, the electronic
density of states (DOS) analysis reveals that TeS exhibits
substantially higher electronic states at the Fermi level
compared with pristine S (Figures S10 and S11). The inherent
metallic character of Te facilitates band gap reduction between
the valence and conduction bands upon incorporation into the
S structure, thereby enhancing the charge transport efficiency.
Furthermore, the calculated Li+ migration energy barrier in
Te−Li2S is reduced to 0.20 eV, compared to 0.41 eV in
pristine Li2S (Figure S12a-b). These computational results
suggest that Te doping elevates the electronic conductivity and
Li+ diffusion kinetics, leading to an accelerated redox reaction
kinetics.

In situ EIS measurements were performed on ASSLSBs with
S/C and TeS/C positive electrodes to investigate the effect of
Te doping on the interfacial impedance (Figure S15a-b). The
EIS for both S/C and TeS/C electrodes displayed systematic

during discharge and decreasing during charge. This reversible
impedance behavior suggests a corresponding reversible
change of interfacial contact during cycling. The in situ EIS
data were subjected to distribution of relaxation times (DRT)
analysis to provide further insight into the mechanism of Te
doping at the cathode interface.22 The DRT spectrum revealed
three distinct impedance contributions characterized by time

cathode−electrolyte interphase (τ = 10−4−10−3 s); and charge

frequency time constants, DRT analysis is generally unsuitable

provide compelling evidence for the beneficial effects of Te
doping on enhancing charge transfer kinetics and accelerating
sulfur conversion during cycling.

In conventional liquid Li−S batteries, the coexistence of
soluble lithium polysulfides with lithium salts (LiTFSI) and
additives (LiNO3) in the electrolyte complicates the
distinction of individual chemical shifts in the
7Li NMR
spectra.25 However, ASSLSBs eliminate interference from
dissolved lithium polysulfides with the electrolyte, enabling
precise differentiation of various solid Li polysulfide species
(Li2S4, Li2S2, and Li2S) through 7Li MAS NMR spectrosco-
py.26,27 As shown in Figure 4a, upon discharging to 1.3 V, a
characteristic peak emerges at −0.13 ppm, indicating Li2S4
formation. As the discharge progressed, two additional peaks
manifested at 1.33 and 2.36 ppm, corresponding to Li2S2 and
Li2S, respectively. Due to the inherent instability of reaction
intermediates Li2S4 and Li2S2, only the chemical shift of Li2S
could be verified independently, showing a peak at 2.30 ppm
(Figure S25). These observations confirm the formation of
diverse Li-containing intermediates during sulfur conversion.28
When discharged to 0.9 V, intensification of Li2S2 and Li2S
peaks concurrent with Li2S4 signal attenuation indicates
progressive conversion of Li2S4 to Li2S2 and Li2S. Upon
discharge voltage to 0.6 V, the Li2S4 peak was virtually extinct,
accompanied by a substantial intensification of the Li2S peak,
demonstrating complete reduction to Li2S. Comparative
analysis of the TeCoS/C electrode at equivalent discharge
states (Figure 4b) revealed enhanced Li2S peaks coupled with
attenuated Li2S4 and Li2S2 signals, indicating enhanced sulfur
conversion efficiency.
Quantitative analysis of the fitting integral revealed the
evolution of the relative proportions of Li2S4, Li2S2, and Li2S
species in the electrode during discharge (Figure 4c). In the S/
C electrode, the proportions of Li2S2 and Li2S gradually
converge, indicating a suppressed conversion of Li2S2 to Li2S.
By contrast, the TeCoS/C electrode exhibits a progressive
increase in the Li2S to Li2S2 ratio, suggesting efficient
conversion of Li2S2 to Li2S during discharge. The NMR
analysis of the TeS/C electrode reveals that the concentration
of Li2S2 was higher than that of Li2S (Figure S26a-b),
demonstrating enhanced conversion kinetics along the S8→
Li2S4 pathway, while the subsequent Li2S4→Li2S2→Li2S
transformation remains limited. Additionally, the
7Li MAS
NMR spectra of the TeS/C and CoS/C electrodes are
presented in Figure S27a-b. The integrated area ratio of Li2S to
Li2S2 in the CoS/C electrode is higher than that observed in
the TeS/C electrode but significantly lower than that in the
TeCoS/C electrode. These observations indicate that CoPc
effectively enhances the conversion of Li2S2 to Li2S.
Furthermore, the energy barrier for the Li2S2-to-Li2S
conversion step is substantially reduced on CoPc (0.100 eV)
compared to graphite (0.428 eV) (Figure 4d). These DFT
calculations corroborate the results from the above NMR
spectra, demonstrating that CoPc effectively facilitates the
selective catalytic conversion of Li2S2 to Li2S. These findings
provide compelling evidence that the synergistic effect of Te
and CoPc expedites the sulfur conversion process (Figure 4e),
resulting in enhanced reaction kinetic.

The electrochemical performances of S/C, TeS/C, and
TeCoS/C cathodes were evaluated. As shown in Figure 5a and
S28, the TeCoS/C cathode demonstrated an initial discharge
specific capacity of 1689 mAh g−1 and a Coulombic efficiency
of 97.2%, surpassing both the S/C and TeS/C cathodes.
Notably, both TeS/C and TeCoS/C cathodes demonstrated
reduced charge−discharge voltage polarization (Figure S29a).
The dQ/dV curve of TeCoS/C (Figure S29b) reveals two
distinct reduction peaks at 1.4 and 1.3 V, corresponding to the
stepwise conversion of Li2S4 and Li2S2. The TeCoS/C cathode
delivered an initial capacity of 1553 mAh g−1, with a capacity
retention of 91.7% after 100 cycles (Figure S30), out-
performing both the S/C and TeS/C cathodes. This capacity
measurement incorporates some contributions from electrolyte
decomposition effects, as thoroughly investigated in our prior
work.5 With increasing cycle numbers, the TeCoS/C cathode
maintains a superior specific capacity with a minimal
polarization increase (Figures S31 and S32). Furthermore,
the influence of Te doping content on electrochemical
performance is evaluated. The Te0.02S0.98/C cathode exhibited
a reduced discharge specific capacity of 1340 mAh g−1 and a
retention of 83.3% at 0.2 C (Figure S33) compared to the
Te0.01S0.99/C cathode (1456 mAh g−1, 88.4% retention).
Additionally, the TeCoS/C cathode demonstrates superior
rate performance compared to S/C and TeS/C cathodes
(Figures S34 and S35), indicating enhanced reaction kinetics
of sulfur conversion. The TeCoS/C cathode maintains a
capacity of 695 mAh g−1 after 1000 cycles, corresponding to an
83.6% retention (Figure S36). To ensure data reliability, we
evaluated three independently fabricated TeCoS/C batteries
under identical test conditions. As illustrated in Figures S37
and S38 and Table S1, the TeCoS@C cathode exhibited
consistent electrochemical stability at both 0.2 and 1 C, which
validates the reproducibility of the sulfur cathode. These
results demonstrate that the combined effects of Te doping
and CoPc catalysis not only enhance reaction kinetic but also
significantly improve the long-term cycling stability of the
sulfur cathode.
Furthermore, the TeCoS/C cathode achieved a remarkable
areal capacity of 6.4 mAh cm−2 at 0.1 C (Figure 5b), which
substantially surpassed those of both S/C and TeS/C
cathodes. The TeCoS/C cathode maintained exceptional
cycling stability, retaining 95.9% capacity retention after 100
cycles while maintaining nearly 100% Coulombic Efficiency
(Figure S39). The initial Coulombic efficiency exceeding 100%
can be attributed to the gradual enhancement of battery
capacity, which comes from the redistribution and more
effective utilization of sulfur particles within the composite.29
The TeCoS/C cathode exhibited a superior rate capability at
60 °C, outperforming the S/C cathode (Figure 5c). Under
increased sulfur loadings of 6.0 and 9.0 mg cm−2, the TeCoS/
C cathode achieved remarkable areal capacities of 7.2 mAh
cm−2 and 13.3 mAh cm−2, while maintaining a stable cycling
performance over 40 cycles (Figure 5d). To assess the energy
density of the electrode, ASSLSBs were fabricated by pairing
the TeCoS/C cathode with a Li metal anode, and their
electrochemical performance was investigated within the
voltage range 1.5−2.8 V. As shown in Figure 5e, the
TeCoS/C−Li battery achieved an areal capacity of 4.33 mAh
cm−2, while demonstrating a stable cycling performance over
40 cycles. With a sulfur loading of 4.5 mg cm−2 and a N/P
ratio of 2.9, the TeCoS/C−Li cell achieved an exceptional
electrode energy density of 818.4 Wh kg−1, accounting for all
positive electrode components and the lithium metal negative
electrode (Figure S40). Comparative analysis with ASSLSBs
reported in recent literature30−33 reveals that the TeCoS/C
cathode exhibits superior overall electrochemical performance
at high sulfur loading (Figure 5f).
In summary, we have developed a dual-modification
TeCoS/C sulfur composite material via a facile, high-energy
ball-milling strategy to enhance the electrochemical perform-
ance of ASSLSBs. The incorporation of Te and CoPc
significantly reduces interfacial charge transfer resistance and
facilitates Li+ transport kinetics, thereby enhancing sulfur
conversion reactions. Moreover, through spectroscopy charac-
terizations including XPS, Raman, and ssNMR, we reveal the
multi-step sulfur conversion mechanism and verify the crucial
roles of Te and CoPc in promoting the conversion of lithium
polysulfides to Li2S. These fundamental insights provide a
deeper understanding of the reaction pathways of sulfur
cathode in ASSLSBs. As a result, the battery assembled with
the TeCoS/C cathode exhibits remarkable reversible specific
capacity and cycling performance. This study not only
demonstrates an effective strategy for enhancing the perform-
ance of ASSLSBs but also offers valuable insights into the
design principles of high-performance sulfur cathodes,
grounded in fundamental reaction mechanisms.
