ABSTRACT: Sulfide-based Li-argyrodites (Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5) are considered as promising
solid-state electrolytes for all-solid-state Li−S batteries (ASSLSBs) due to their high ionic
conductivity and suitable mechanical properties. However, their hygroscopic nature and
intense decomposition behavior when combined with conductive additives at the sulfur
cathode introduce many variables that can affect cell performance. This study
demonstrates that an optimized amount of ambient air contamination during cycling
can enhance cell performance. Multiple spectroscopic analyses reveal that the hydrolysis of
Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 can incorporate oxygen into the argyrodite structure, thereby increasing the
oxidative stability and generating additional redox active sulfur species. Methods, such as
monitoring cell impedance growth, open-circuit voltage changes, and 31P NMR spectroscopic analysis, are proposed to
determine the air-tightness of cell configurations. The research findings suggest that verifying the air-tightness of cell
configurations is critical for ASSLSBs research to prevent misleading results.
A
ll-solid-state batteries (ASSBs) have attracted signifi-
cant research interest due to their potential for higher
energy density and improved safety compared to
current liquid electrolyte-based Li-ion batteries.1−4 Specifically,
all-solid-state Li−S batteries (ASSLSBs) that utilize earth-
abundant, environmentally friendly sulfur as the cathode
material can maximize cost-effectiveness and achieve much
higher gravimetric energy density owing to the remarkable
specific capacity of sulfur (1,675 mA h g−1).5−9 Additionally,
the application of a solid-state electrolyte addresses the
polysulfide dissolution and shuttling encountered with liquid
electrolytes, which are chronic issues with conventional Li−S
batteries. Therefore, research on ASSLSBs is actively
progressing to meet the high demand for energy storage
systems that require high energy density, enhanced safety, and
sustainability.
In ASSBs, the most critical component to investigate first is
the solid-state electrolyte (SSE). Oxide- and sulfide-based
electrolytes are the two most actively researched classes of
materials. While oxides possess superior electrochemical
stability, sulfides exhibit much higher ionic conductivity (>1
mS cm−1), even comparable to that of liquid electrolytes.10−13
Additionally, sulfides have a mechanically soft nature, enabling
them to form dense and intimate contacts through a simple
room-temperature pressing. These characteristics, combined
with their ease of processing and handling, make sulfides some
of the best candidates for potential commercial applications.
Within this category, Li-argyrodites with the formula Li6PS5X
(X = Cl, Br, I) have been extensively researched due to their
ease of synthesis and use of inexpensive precursors.14−17
Recently, the relationships between different halide anions
(Cl−, Br−, I−) and the ionic conductivity of argyrodites have
been systematically investigated by monitoring the Li+
occupancy and X−/S2−site disorder.18−20 The studies have
shown that a higher degree of site disorder is beneficial for
intercage jumps, contributing to increased ionic conductivity.
Similarly, increasing the halide content from Li6PS5Cl to
Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 has been found to create more Li+ vacancies and
introduce Cl−/S2−site disorder. The weakened bonds between
Li and anions (Cl−and S2−) were detected by the substitution
of sulfur with chlorine, resulting in a Li-sublattice with a high
ionic conductivity of up to 9.5 mS cm−1 at room temper-
ature.21 These anion-disordered Li-argyrodites, owing to their
tunability and high ionic conductivities, are being considered
as promising SSE candidates for ASSLSBs.
However, these Li-argyrodites have critical weaknesses that
need to be addressed for commercialization. The P5+ in sulfide
electrolytes has a high affinity for oxygen, leading to
spontaneous hydrolysis when exposed to ambient air

accompany a decrease in ionic conductivity.
Another drawback of Li-argyrodites is their narrow electro-
chemical stability window. The theoretical stability window of
Li6PS5Cl is reported to be 1.71−2.01 V (vs Li/Li+), which
cannot fully cover the voltage range required for sulfur
conversion reaction in ASSLSBs.33 Even though the practical
stability range seems to be around 1.25−2.30 V (vs Li/Li+),34
which is wider than the thermodynamically calculated
windows, Li-argyrodites in sulfur cathodes are still susceptible
to rapid degradation. In sulfur cathodes, the inclusion of
conductive additives is inevitable to supply electrons to
compensate for the insulating nature of sulfur. However,
introducing conductive additives significantly increases the
“reaction volume” where ions and electrons are supplied and
extracted, which in turn amplifies the decomposition of SSE
during cycling.35 Additionally, Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 has been found to
show a more pronounced decomposition at the interface with
carbon compared to Li6PS5Cl, due to its thermodynamically
unstable nature.36 Such electrolyte decomposition can produce
reversible redox-active species, but it can also lead to structural
degradation resulting in cell failure, making it a critical factor to
control.37,38
On the whole, it is crucial to understand the effects of air/
moisture contamination, as well as the electrolyte decom-
position behavior of Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5, when applied with sulfur
cathode in ASSLSBs. This study demonstrates that commercial
solid-state cell setups, which are often regarded as airtight, may
not always maintain this standard. It is found that ambient air
contamination on the sulfur cathode (S @ SSE @ C) can
sometimes misleadingly enhance cell performance based on
the cycling performance comparison between the cells cycled
inside and outside the glovebox (GB). A systematic analysis
comparing the sulfur cathode and Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 electrolyte-
only cathode (SSE @ C) reveals that the S @ SSE @ C is more
sensitive to air/moisture contamination. Furthermore, a series

Figure 1a presents a schematic illustration of the ASSLSB
battery setup. Li−In alloy was applied as an anode due to its
high compatibility with inorganic SSEs,39,40 combined with
Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 (σ = 3.7 mS cm−1, Figure S1) as a separator
layer, and S @ SSE @ C at the cathode. The assembled cells
were cycled at a stack pressure of 50−60 MPa. A detailed
description of the cell configuration is provided, featuring a
digital image of the actual cell (Figure S2a) and schematics of
the cell interior (Figure S2b). To ensure the air-tightness of the
cell as much as possible, O-rings were installed on both the
anode and cathode plungers with a screw-type mechanism
applied to maintain the seal throughout cell cycling. Such cell
configurations with O-rings are widely utilized in the solid-
state research field.
To verify the air-tightness of these cells, the performance of
the cells with the same setup is compared when cycled inside
an argon-filled GB and outside the GB (Figure 1b). The cells
cycled inside the GB show a peak discharge capacity of 864
mA h g−1 at the second cycle, followed by a continuous
capacity decay. In contrast, the cells cycled outside the GB
exhibit a gradual capacity increase during the early stages of
cycling to reach a peak capacity of 1,057 mA h g−1 at the 10th
cycle, followed by a decaying capacity trend. This trend is also
evident in the voltage profiles, where the capacity of the cells
cycled inside the GB decreases to 616 mA h g−1 after 60 cycles
(Figure 1c), whereas the cells that cycled outside the GB
maintain a higher capacity of 921 mA h g−1 (Figure 1d). Both
the cells cycled inside and outside the GB show a major
discharge voltage plateau at around 1.9 V (vs Li/Li+),
indicating that the redox reactions are predominantly driven
by sulfur conversion.41,42 However, it is noteworthy to point
out that the cells cycled outside the GB exhibit higher capacity
and superior cycling performance. This trend persisted
consistently even at higher rates (Figure S3). As shown in
Figure S4, the environment outside the GB maintained a
temperature of 20 °C and a relative humidity of 15%, while a
similar temperature of 25 °C was maintained inside the GB,
with argon-filled inert conditions keeping oxygen and water
levels below 0.1 ppm.
To identify the factors contributing to the differences in cell
performance, 50 cycled cells were disassembled. As shown in
Figure 1e, the cell cycled outside of the GB shows faint
discoloration at the edge of the cathode. Such a discoloration is
not observed in the cells that are cycled inside the GB,
suggesting that ambient air might have an influence on the
cathode. To confirm a more definite change in the cathode
surface, the cells were aged outside the GB for 3 days without
an O-ring at the cathode plunger to intentionally induce
heavier air contamination. As shown in Figure S5, the cell
without the O-ring exhibits clear wetting and discoloration at
the cathode edge. Generally, sulfur and carbon, which are
components of the cathode, do not react with the argyrodite
electrolyte and are air-stable materials. Therefore, such
discoloration might be due to the hydroscopic nature of
Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 reacting with ambient air.
To investigate these changes in Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5, only the
Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 electrolyte was pelletized with the previously
described cell configuration, followed by aging both inside and
outside the GB for 3 days. Analyzing the surface of
Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 with Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spec-
troscopy reveals no noticeable peaks for the cell aged inside the
GB (Figure 1f). In contrast, the cell assembled with the
standard O-ring configuration and aged outside the GB
exhibits CO stretching (1,705 cm−1) and O−H stretching
(3,000−3,500 cm−1), indicating carbonate formation and
hydration due to the reaction of Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 with ambient
air. To confirm these peaks more definitively, the O-ring at the
cathode was removed from the cell configuration and aged
outside the GB for 3 days, resulting in much clearer O−H and
CO peaks, as shown by the black curve in Figure 1f.
Given that air contamination in Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 is observed
despite the use of an O-ring and screw-type sealing in the
current cell configuration, the impact of these changes in
Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 on cell performance was investigated. A
composite of Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5, ketjen black, and carbon nanofiber
(SSE @ C) was ball-milled and applied to the cathode, then
cycled both inside and outside the GB. In the case of an SSE @
C cathode, the sufficient electron-conducting carbon network
facilitates the decomposition of SSE. During this process,
redox-active species are generated, contributing to the
reversible cell capacity generation.37,43 An identical mass of
Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 and C was applied to the SSE @ C cathode with
only the sulfur portion excluded compared to the conventional
S @ SSE @ C cathode, and the same cycling conditions were
applied. The capacity generated by the electrolyte decom-
position of the SSE @ C cathode was converted to the sulfur
mass basis of the S @ SSE @ C cathode to calculate the
specific capacity, providing an indirect measurement of the
capacity contribution from electrolyte decomposition. As
shown in Figure S6, the cell with SSE @ C cycled outside
the GB exhibits a slightly higher peak capacity of 462 mA h g−1
(sulfur basis) compared to the cell cycled inside the GB (272
mA h g−1). Although the cell cycled outside the GB shows a
more rapid capacity decay, it still demonstrates a higher
capacity of 264 mA h g−1 after 50 cycles compared to 209 mA
h g−1 for the cell cycled inside the GB. Similar trends observed
in both SSE @ C and S @ SSE @ C cathodes indicate that the
superior cell performance of the cells cycled outside the GB
likely originates from the changes in the behavior of the
Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 electrolyte.
These results suggest that what is considered an adequately
airtight solid-state cell setup may not always be the case, and
the air exposure can potentially impact cell performance.
However, the observation that cells cycled outside the GB with
air/moisture contamination exhibit superior performance
compared to those cycled inside the GB is contrary to existing
findings. It is widely accepted that exposure of Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 to
air triggers hydrolysis of the argyrodite, resulting in structural
damage and decreased ionic conductivity.44−46 Similar to the

even higher peak capacity of 1,431 mA h g−1, but exhibits a
more rapid capacity degradation trend (Figure S7), potentially
due to the severe hydrolysis-induced degradation of

Based on a series of our experiments, it has been
demonstrated that ambient air exposure can influence the
performance of ASSLSBs and, in certain cases, may even
appear to enhance it. This finding underscores the importance
of verifying the air-tightness of the cell configuration in
ASSLSB experiments to avoid potentially misleading results.
Thus, what parameters can be used to determine whether an
assembled cell is free from air exposure? One of the most
straightforward methods is to measure changes in cell
impedance through electrochemical impedance spectroscopy
(EIS). As shown in Figure 2a, the LiIn ∥SSE ∥S @ SSE @ C
cell rested inside the GB shows a small increase in bulk
impedance from 5.8 to 7.6 ohms after 3 days, whereas the cell
rested outside the GB exhibits a large impedance growth from
6.2 to 12 ohms. The detailed bode magnitude plots are
included in Figure S8. The hydrolysis of Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 is
known to produce byproducts, such as LiCl, Li2S, and Li3PO4,
which are poor ionic and electronic conductors. Therefore, the
generation of these insulating byproducts and the structural
degradation of Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 can increase the interfacial
resistance.47 Thus, if there is a noticeable difference in
impedance changes between cells rested inside and outside
the GB, it is necessary to question the air-tightness of the cell
configuration.
Another criterion to evaluate the air-tightness of the cell
configuration is the open-circuit voltage (OCV) measurement.
As shown in Figure 2c, the cell rested inside the GB maintains
a stable OCV of 2.25 V (vs Li/Li+) over 3 days, whereas the
cell rested outside the GB exhibits a gradual increase in OCV,
reaching up to 2.38 V. As seen in Figure S9, when there is no
O-ring installed at the cathode with a severe air exposure, a
more rapid OCV increase is detected stabilizing at a potential
of 2.4 V over 3 days. Therefore, the increasing trend in the
OCV for the cell rested outside the GB likely reflects the

the cathode but also the separator layer. The cathode portion

although the intensity is low, the presence of a P−O peak
indicates that ambient air contamination can propagate

the separator layer. These findings further suggest that 31P
NMR spectroscopy is another effective technique for verifying
the air-tightness of the cell.
Another interesting observation is detected in the compar-
ison of the 31P spectra of the S @ SSE @ C and SSE @ C
cathodes after 3 days of rest outside the GB. As shown in
Figure S11, a noticeable P−O bond is detected in the S @ SSE

dramatic change in argyrodite structure.
It has been demonstrated that what was previously
considered an airtight solid-state cell configuration does not
always guarantee complete air-tightness, which can sometimes
make the cell performance appear better than it actually is.
Criteria, such as FTIR, EIS, OCV changes, and solid-state
NMR, are suggested to verify the air-tightness of the cell
configuration. Thus, given the common understanding that
hydrolysis-induced structural damage of Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 reduces
the conductivity and degrades cell performance, a remaining
question is how an optimized amount of ambient air exposure
can seemingly improve the performance of the ASSLSBs. To
investigate this, cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves of cells with
SSE @ C and S @ SSE @ C cathodes were examined. Both
cathodes were discharged from the OCV to 1.5 V and
subsequently charged up to 3.1 V (vs Li/Li+) inside and
outside the GB. As shown in Figure 3a, both the SSE @ C
cathodes cycled inside and outside the GB exhibit an OCV of
2.22 V and show negligible current response during the initial
cathodic sweep, indicating that stable Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 is
maintained without decomposition in this potential range.
However, during the subsequent anodic scan to 3.1 V, two
current peaks appeared: a minor peak at 2.55 V and a major
anodic peak at 2.95 V. These anodic responses can be
attributed to the oxidation of Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5, corresponding to
the formation of LixPSy + LiCl + S (2.55 V) and S + P2Sx (2.95
V).34,35 After the initial cell charge, redox-active species are
formed, which can undergo reversible reactions, where a peak
at 2.2 V during the cathodic scan and 2.4 V at the anodic scan
appears in the subsequent scan. The cyclic voltammogram of
SSE @ C cathodes shows that apart from a slightly higher
current response in cells with air contamination, no distinct
redox activity differences are observed between samples cycled
inside and outside the GB during the initial 2 cycles. As
previously discussed, SSE @ C cathodes are relatively less
sensitive to air and moisture exposure, which likely explains
why cells performed inside and outside the GB do not show
significant differences in the early stages.
However, as cycling proceeds, some differences between
cells with and without air/moisture contamination begin to
emerge. As shown in Figure S12a, after the first cycle, two
peaks are observed during the anodic scan at 2.4 and 2.8 V
corresponding to respectively the oxidation of redox active
sulfur conversion and additional oxidation of argyrodite. In
cells cycled inside the GB, the peak intensity for argyrodite
oxidation remains high throughout the cycles. In contrast, cells
cycled outside the GB show a decrease in the argyrodite
oxidation peak over cycling, indicating a shift toward a sulfur
oxidation-dominant process (Figure S12b). The results
demonstrate that as air exposure progresses during the cycling
of SSE @ C cathode, there is a tendency for less electrolyte
oxidative decomposition to occur.

the reduction of active sulfur in the cathode. During the

NMR, as shown in Figure S14. The gradually increasing
intensity at the position corresponding to LiCl, which is a
byproduct of hydrolysis, provides evidence of ongoing air/
moisture contamination of Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 throughout cycling.
However, despite the cell being charged to a voltage of 3.1 V,
the cell cycled outside the GB shows an almost negligible
intensity of the P2Sx peak. Such 31P NMR results demonstrate
that while the Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 in the cathode cycled outside the
GB formed P−O containing species due to hydrolysis, the
electrolyte decomposition especially happening at the cell
charging process seems to be suppressed.
To examine how this trend manifests in SSE @ C, a similar
NMR analysis was conducted on cells in both charged and
discharged states, comparing those cycled inside and outside
the GB. As shown in Figure S15a, the cells cycled inside the
GB exhibit no significant changes in the discharged state,
similar to that of the S @ SSE @ C cathode. Analogously, in
the charged state, the emergence of P2Sx species indicates that
the oxidation of Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 occurs when the cathode is not
exposed to ambient air. For the cells cycled outside the GB, no
noticeable P−O peak emerged during the initial discharge
(Figure S15b). The result correlates with the previously
discussed data that the SSE @ C cathode is less sensitive to air
exposure, resulting in the P−O species not being prominently
formed yet. Although a small amount of P−O bond is detected
in the charged state, its intensity is much weaker compared to
that in the S @ SSE @ C cathode (Figure 3d). Interestingly,
the electrolyte decomposed products of P2Sx species are also
observed at the end of the charging process. The observations
again confirm that the SSE @ C cathode is less sensitive to air
exposure compared to the S @ SSE @ C cathode, forming
fewer P−O bonds. Furthermore, as the structure containing
P−O bonds is less pronounced, Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 appears to
undergo oxidation to some extent to form the P2Sx species
during cell charging.
The fact that air/moisture contamination leads to structural
changes in Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5, including the formation of P−O
bonds, and results in less decomposition under oxidative
conditions aligns with previous reports. According to the hard
and soft acid and base theory, introducing oxygen into Cl-rich
Li-argyrodite enhances the stability of the P−O chemical bond
containing structure because the nonbridging oxygen in
argyrodite is more stable than sulfur.24,50 P5+ is a strong acid,
which easily combines with the strong base, such as O2−,
forming a much stronger P−O bond compared to the pristine
argyrodite with its weaker P−S bonded PS4
3−tetrahedra.
Therefore, ambient air exposure potentially transforms the
surface of Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 through hydrolysis to form an
intermediate phase containing P−O bonds, which may
enhance the oxidative stability of the electrolyte. Additionally,
this air contamination creates a passivating layer on the
outermost surface of Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5, which may gradually
reduce further hydrolysis over time and prevent the complete
degradation of ionically conductive Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5.51,52 Fur-
thermore, hydrolysis of Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 leads to the formation of
Li2S, which is the active material in the Li−S system. It is
clearly shown in Figure S16 with XPS that sulfide species are
formed after Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 is exposed for 3 days outside the
GB. Such a generation of additional sulfur species can
compensate for the structural degradation of Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5
caused by hydrolysis, leading to a higher overall capacity and
stable cycling performance. The Cl 2p spectra further reveals
that the exposure of the cell at ambient air impacts the material
to a thickness of 125−250 nm, which is equivalent to an Ar+
sputtering of 5−10 min, as shown in Figure S17.
To verify the NMR results, cathode particles in charged and
discharged states were compared with XPS. Figure 4a shows
the S 2p spectra of the S @ SSE @ C cathode cycled inside the
GB. The pristine sample can be deconvoluted into an
elemental sulfur peak at 164.2 eV and a PS4
3−peak at 162.1
eV. After the initial discharge, a majority of the elemental sulfur
species is reduced to sulfides, resulting in the disappearance of
the S0 peak and the appearance of the S2−peak at 160.5 eV.
Subsequent charging shows these S2−species are oxidized back
to the S0 state. Whereas Figure 4b indicates the changes in the
S 2p spectra when the same process occurs outside the GB
with ambient air exposure. Similarly, the pristine S @ SSE @ C
cathode shows peaks for S0 and PS4
3−. When the cell
undergoes initial discharge, the high intensity of the S2−peak
emerges with a small peak for oxidized sulfur species (SOx)
being observed at 168.7 eV. The highly dominant intensity of
S2−compared to PS4
3−suggests that in addition to the
conversion of elemental sulfur to S2−, supplementary sulfide
species is further generated by the hydrolysis of Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5.
When the cell is charged back to 3.1 V, these S2−species
convert back to S0, confirming that the sulfides generated by
hydrolysis can also be utilized as an active material, thereby
contributing to the capacity.
To confirm the decomposition of Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 alone and
the resulting generation of active sulfur species upon cycling,
the SSE @ C cathode was similarly analyzed in discharged and
charged states via XPS, both inside and outside the GB. As
shown in Figure S18, the SSE @ C cathode discharged inside
and outside the GB does not show additional peaks beyond
PS4
3−, confirming that the electrolyte does not decompose at
the end of discharge. However, during the charging process,
both the cells inside and outside the GB exhibit the formation
of the S0 peak, indicating that electrolyte decomposition occurs
during cell charging and that elemental sulfur is one of its
products. Since SSE @ C is less sensitive to ambient air
contamination, the S 2p spectra does not reveal the differences
as evident as in the S @ SSE @ C cathode between the cells
cycled inside and outside the GB during the initial charge and
discharge process.
Aside from the S 2p spectra, the P 2p spectra provide a
clearer view of how the incorporation of oxygen into the
argyrodite structure due to hydrolysis affects electrolyte
decomposition during the charging process. As shown in
Figure 4c, the pristine and discharged S @ SSE @ C cathode
inside the GB only shows the PS4
3−peak at 132.6 eV,
confirming the maintenance of the pure argyrodite structure
upon discharge. However, after the cell is charged, a new peak
emerges at a binding energy of 133.5 eV, which can be
assigned to the P2Sx species, known to be the product of
argyrodite oxidation. Thus, the S @ SSE @ C cathode cycled
inside the GB undergoes electrolyte decomposition during the
charging process, which is consistent with the results of the 31P
NMR analysis.

In summary, an in-depth investigation was conducted to
comprehend the effect of ambient air exposure on the
performance of ASSLSBs. It is highlighted that even cell
configurations conventionally considered as airtight can still
experience air and moisture contamination. Various methods,
such as monitoring changes in cell impedance and OCV,
combined with the analysis of the 31P NMR of rested cathodes,
are proposed to evaluate the air-tightness. Comparing them to
the cells maintained in oxygen and moisture free conditions
such as a glovebox, as a reference, is suggested. To ensure
sufficient air-tightness, the use of O-rings on plungers is
essential, and the addition of vacuum grease around the O-
rings can be beneficial. It is also found that ambient air
contamination on the electrolyte, when controlled at an
optimized level with gradual exposure, can sometimes
seemingly enhance cell performance likely due to the
hydrolysis of the Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5. A series of characterization
techniques, including FTIR, NMR, and XPS, indicate that
Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 forms species containing P−O bonds upon air/
moisture contamination. These stronger P−O bonded
structures exhibit enhanced oxidative stability compared to
pristine argyrodite, suppressing electrolyte decomposition
upon cycling. Additionally, hydrolysis results in the formation
of active sulfide species, which contributes to improved cell
capacities. Cells with optimized ambient air exposure
demonstrated excellent performance with 97% capacity
retention (921 mA h g−1) after 60 cycles. This study
underscores the necessity of verifying the air-tightness of cell
configurations to prevent misleading results in research and
highlights the mechanisms through which cell performance can
appear improved, providing important insights for the future
development of ASSLSBs.
