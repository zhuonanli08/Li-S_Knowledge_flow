ABSTRACT
All-solid-state lithium-sulfur battery (ASSLSB) represents one of the most promising energy storage technologies attributed to
the high energy density, intrinsic safety, and economic viability. However, introducing solid-state electrolytes (SEs) into the sulfur
cathode triggers thorny problems of deficient sulfur loading, inadequate sulfur utilization, and severe interfacial degradation. Here,
we demonstrate a spatially tailored sulfur cathode architecture with concentration-gradient SE and conductive agent distribution.
From the separator side to the current collector, SE concentration is decreased from 65 to 55 wt.%, which promotes lithium-
ion transport through continuous pathways and concentration-driven forces. Conversely, the concentration of conductive agent
increases from 5 to 15 wt.% to ensure efficient electron conduction. The mutually opposing concentration gradients simultaneously
suppress the SE degradation by minimizing its interfacial contacts with conductive agents. As a result, the ASSLSB delivers
enhanced sulfur utilization (1315 mAh g−1) under 7.6 mg cm−2, while maintaining a low capacity fading rate of 0.05% per cycle
over 225 cycles. Even further increasing the sulfur loading to 11.4 mg cm−2, a high capacity of 10.96 mAh cm−2 is achieved.

Introduction
With the advantages of high energy density (2600 Wh kg−1)
and low cost (US$340 per ton for sulfur), lithium-sulfur bat-
teries (LSBs) are deemed as one of the most promising energy
storage systems for fields that prioritize the long-running per-
formance, such as unmanned aerial vehicles [1–3]. However,
the lithium polysulfides dissolution problem severely limits the
sulfur utilization, which results in inferior capacity and cycling
performance of batteries [4]. For all-solid-state LSBs (ASSLSBs),
a solid-state electrolyte (SE) is applied to replace the ether-
based liquid electrolyte of conventional LSBs, which not only
addresses the polysulfides dissolution issue fundamentally, but
also enhances the battery safety by mechanically suppressing the
lithium dendrite growth [5, 6]. Nevertheless, limited by the slow
and medium-dependent lithium-ion hopping conduction mech-
anism of SEs, incorporating SEs into the sulfur cathode, except
for electron-conducting additives is indispensable to guarantee
smooth ion transport within ASSLSBs [7]. The extra SE additives
decrease the sulfur loading from 70–80 wt.% to less than 30 wt.%,
which seriously decreases the energy density of ASSLSBs [8].
Furthermore, insufficient contacts between SE and SE (or sulfur)
solid particles, and inevitable void formation within the solid-
state sulfur cathodes result in slow and interrupted ion transport,
which diminishes the sulfur electrochemical reaction kinetics,
especially under high sulfur loadings [9]. Additionally, the

To this end, we demonstrate a spatially modulated sulfur cath-

progressively decreases from 65 to 55 wt.% from the separator
side toward the current collector, while conductive additives
increase concomitantly from 5 to 15 wt.% in the opposite direc-

with homogenous compositional concentrations, this architec-
ture enhances ion transport through continuous SE percolation
pathways, simultaneously optimizing electron conduction via
smooth conducting path designs. Crucially, the counter-gradient
design physically minimizes the interfacial contacts of SE and
conductive additives to suppress parasitic SE degradations.
Consequently, the ASSLSB delivers a high specific capacity of
1315 mAh g−1 at the sulfur loading of 7.6 mg cm−2 with a low
fading rate (0.05% per cycle over 225 cycles). Even at a high
loading of 11.4 mg cm−2, the gradient design achieves a capacity of
965 mAh g−1, alongside a significantly higher capacity retention
(48%) than non-gradient analogues under equivalent conditions.

Results and Discussions
As mentioned above, the solid-state sulfur cathode is composed
of three functional components: sulfur as the electrochemically
active material, SE as the ionic conductor, and conductive agents
as the electron transport medium. In this study, we selected
the chlorine-rich Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 (LPSC) as the SE, and single-
walled CNTs (SWCNTs) as the electron-conducting network.
Compared with the widely employed Li6PS5Cl, the chlorine-
rich LPSC manifests greater lattice disorder, which is beneficial
for enhanced ionic conductivity and electrochemical activity.
The particular 1D structure of SWCNT not only endows it with
reduced tortuosity for electron transport, but also facilitates the
construction of a 3D network for continuous electron conduction.
To fabricate the concentration-gradient sulfur cathode (herein
termed CGS), three layers of cathode mixtures with varying mass
concentrations of SE and CNTs were sequentially pressed and
integrated. First, the cathode mixtures containing 30 wt.% sulfur
and LPSC:SWCNT mass ratios of 65:5, 60:10, and 55:15 were
prepared by ball milling. Subsequently, the mixture with the
highest LPSC concentration of 65 wt.% was layered and pressed
on the SE separator side, and then mixtures with 60 and 55 wt.%
LPSC were added as the middle and current collector-side layers,
respectively. For comparison, a conventional sulfur cathode
(herein denoted as CS) was fabricated. Its LPSC:S:SWCNT mass
ratio is controlled to be 60:30:10, which is equivalent to the
average composition ratio of the CGS. To ensure the absence
of undesired sulfur concentration gradients, thermogravimetric
analysis (TGA) was conducted on each layer as well as the
fabricated gradient cathode. As shown in Figure S1, all groups
were determined to have an identical sulfur content of 30 wt.%,
with no sulfur gradient.
To investigate the through‑thickness morphology and component
distribution of solid-state sulfur cathodes, cryogenic cross-section
polisher-scanning electron microscopy (CP‑SEM) measurement
was conducted. As shown in Figure 1c and Figure S2, both CGS
and CS cathodes were densely fabricated without any discernible
delamination or cracks, which enables efficient charge transport
by minimizing interfacial resistance. Different from the homoge-
neous compositional concentration within the CS cathode, the
CGS architecture exhibits a pronounced concentration gradient
of SE and CNT additives. In the layer adjacent to the separator
side, micrometer-scale SE particles are the dominant component,
which is consistent with the markedly high Cl signal observed
in the elemental mapping result (Figure 1c; Figure S3a). In
the middle layer, with decreasing the SE concentration, the
intensity of Cl signal is reduced, while the C signal intensity
is strengthened due to the increased mass ratio of CNT addi-
tive (Figure 1c; Figure S3b). For the layer near the current
collector, the amount of SE particles is notably decreased, and
they are embedded within the CNT-based cathode mixture.
Correspondingly, the Cl signal intensity is the lowest, whereas
the C signal is remarkably enhanced from the elemental mapping
result. Quantitatively, the energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy
(EDX) line-scan results reveal a distinct Cl gradient from the
separator toward the current collector, which corroborates the
designed SE gradient within the CGS architecture (Figure S4). In
addition, quantitative EDX mapping spectrum analysis confirms
that the measured elemental mass ratios (S: 57.1 wt.%, C: 14.3
wt.%, Cl: 17.4 wt.%, P: 8.1 wt.%, Li: 3.1 wt.%) are close to the
theoretical composition (LPSC:S: SWCNT ratio of 60:30:10) of
the CGS cathode (Figure S4c). To further examine the internal
architecture of the cathode, 3D X‑ray computed tomography
(X‑ray CT) was performed (Figure S5). The reconstructed 3D
image reveals a dense and crack‑free microstructure with SE
particles maintaining good interfacial contact. Clear differences
in the volume fraction across the three layers are observed,
consistent with the intended gradient architecture. Furthermore,
the X-ray CT result indicates that the concentration‑gradient
design leads to improved through‑thickness continuity of the
SE network, which is beneficial for reduced tortuosity of ion
transport pathways, contributing to enhancements in lithium-ion
mobility.

the cathode, two models with and without concentration-
gradient SE and CNT conductive agents were constructed
using COMSOL Multiphysics (Figure 2). From Figure 2b,c, a
pronounced intensity disparity can be observed in the sim-
ulated electrolyte potential and current density from the SE
separator to the current collector for the CS cathode, which
manifests its severely limited charge transport kinetics. Con-
versely, the simulations demonstrate superior intensity and
uniformity in both electrolyte potential and current density
for the CGS cathode (Figure 2e,f). The tortuosity factor k

Furthermore, the ionic and electronic conductivity of the solid-

polarization method with electron- and ion-blocking symmetric

by measuring the current values at three different voltages

were then calculated based on Ohm’s Law. As with the ionic

LPSC and the LPSC-Li interface was accounted for and subtracted
using the equivalent circuit model (Figure S8 and Table S1).
Consistent with the simulation results, the CGS exhibits better
performance with lower resistance and higher ionic and elec-

how composition and stacked architecture govern the ionic and
electronic conductivity of the sulfur cathode, we evaluated the
charge transport properties of the three constituent layers in
the CGS cathode, and a reversed concentration-gradient sulfur

Although this result confirms the positive correlation between
electrode conductivity and the content of conducting agents,
the CS exhibits lower ionic and electronic conductivity than its
CGS counterpart of identical average composition, highlighting
the critical role of architectural design in the sulfur cathode.

The galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) is a
powerful approach to study electrochemical reaction kinetics
of ASSLSBs during the working process. In Figure 3c, it can
be observed that the CGS cathode delivered a higher discharge
capacity of 1523 mAh g−1 than the CS cathode (1272 mAh g−1)
at the current density of 0.05 C, which indicates that the
concentration-gradient structure contributes to a higher sulfur
utilization. Previous research has verified that a lower open
circuit voltage (OCV) of ASSLSBs originates from a deeper
lithiation of the sulfur cathode during the discharge process,
which diminishes the potential difference between the electrodes
[14]. In Figure 3c, the OCV of CGS cathode (1.13 V) is lower than
that of the CS cathode (1.19 V), which confirms that enhanced
complete electrochemical lithiation reactions are facilitated by
the concentration-gradient electrode architecture. More impor-
tantly, both overpotential and ohmic (IR) drop obtained from the
GITT curves are significantly smaller in the CGS cathode than in
the CS during the lithiation process, especially when the voltage
fell below 1.5 V (Figure 3d; Figure S9). These results demonstrate
that sulfur conversion reaction kinetics to the final discharge
product of Li2S are remarkably promoted by the concentration-
gradient architectural design through simultaneously enhanced
ion and electron transport.
To further elucidate the resistance details within the ASSLSBs
during the operating process, electrochemical impedance spec-
troscopy (EIS) measurements were performed. As shown in
Figure 3e, the Nyquist plot is composed of three parts: a high-
frequency ohmic resistance (Re) from the SE and electrodes,
an interface resistance (Rint), and a low-frequency Warburg
diffusion tail [16]. Initially, both CGS and CS electrodes exhibit
a small ohmic resistance of approximately 3.6 Ω. After 10
charge/discharge cycles, the CS shows a more pronounced
increase in resistance than the CGS cathode. Since the cycling-
induced rise in ohmic resistance stems primarily from the LPSC
decomposition, the attenuated resistance increase in the CGS
electrode corresponds to its superior capability to mitigate the SE
(LPSC) oxidation. Furthermore, distribution of relaxation time
analysis (DRT) was employed to deconvolve overlapping signals
in the Nyquist plots, specifically to distinguish the contributions
from the low- and middle-frequency regions. Based on the
relaxation time, three distinct regions are defined: τI (10−4 to
10−2 s) assigned to the lithium-ion transport across the cathode-
electrolyte interphase; τII (10−2 to 100 s) corresponding to the
charge transfer at the cathode surface; τIII (100 to 102 s) represent-
ing the solid-state lithium-ion diffusion within the cathode [20].
The DRT analysis reveals that the relaxation time of characteristic
peaks within the τIII region for the CGS cathode is shifted
downward by about half an order of magnitude when compared
with the CS cathode (Figure 3f). The shortened relaxation time
suggests that the lithium-ion diffusion and transport kinetics are
significantly improved within the CGS cathode, which corrob-
orates our findings from GITT [21]. The CGS cathode exhibits
significantly lower impedance than the CS cathode, further
confirming its superior ionic transport kinetics. Consistent with
the EIS results, the CS cathode displayed a substantially larger

Operando Raman spectroscopy was performed using a cus-
tomized setup to elucidate the underlying reaction mechanism
of ASSLSBs (Figure S11). Raman spectra of sulfur and LPSC are
first shown in Figure S12. Three characteristic peaks are identified
in the LPSC Raman spectrum: a dominant PS4
3−νs stretching
mode at 420 cm−1, a PS4
3−δdef bending mode at 270 cm−1, and
a weak lattice deformation (LD) band at 180 cm−1 [22]. As with
the sulfur, three S─S vibrational peaks at approximately 160,
220, and 470 cm−1 are observed in the Raman spectrum [23]. In
the CGS cathode, upon discharging, the characteristic Raman
peak of LPSC at approximately 420 cm−1 decreased dramatically
in intensity, concomitant with an intensity strengthening of the
broad band at around 180 cm−1, which indicates that the lithiation
induces the lattice distortion of PS4
3−tetrahedra in LPSC and the
formation of metastable intermediates (Figure 4a). Furthermore,
the deepening of lithiation leads to a marked intensification
of these structural distortions in LPSC, most notably over the
voltage range from 1.2 to 0.9 V. Concurrently, the Raman peaks
corresponding to pristine sulfur gradually diminished, while new
peaks of sulfur lithiation products emerged. Specifically, the
Raman peak at 470 cm−1, corresponding to the S─S stretching
mode of sulfur, gradually broadened and disappeared, which
indicates the successful sulfur reduction reaction. A new peak
at 380 cm−1 gradually appeared, representing the formation of
Li2S. The intensification of the peaks at 450 and 480 cm−1 can be
assigned to the formation of Li2S4 and Li2S2, respectively [24, 25].
These results reveal that the lithiation of sulfur follows a stepwise
pathway, with the intermediate polysulfides formation prior to
the final product of Li2S.
Upon charging, the above spectral changes are highly reversible:
peaks assigned to the Li2S and Li2Sx diminished, the sulfur
signals recovered, and the LSPC Raman peaks at 420 and
180 cm−1 returned to their initial states with negligible changes
in intensity (Figure 4a). It corroborates the critical role of the
concentration-gradient structure of the CGS cathode in promot-
ing the reversibility of sulfur redox conversions and the structural
stability of LPSC. However, in the CS cathode, the Raman peak of
LSPC at 420 cm−1 endured a significantly irreversible attenuation
after being cycled, while the 180 cm−1 band showed a pronounced
increase in intensity, which is indicative of partially irreversible
oxidation and structural degradation of LPSC during the charging
process (Figure 4b). To quantify and compare the structural
evolution of LPSC in the CS and CGS cathodes, we employed
the Raman peak intensity ratio (I180/I420) as a metric for its lattice
stability. A more pronounced change in the I180/I420 ratio between
the initial and cycled states was observed in the CS cathode
(0.06) compared to the CGS cathode (0.03) (Figure 4c,d). This
demonstrably indicates that the concentration-gradient cathode
architecture can effectively suppress the structural deformation
of LPSC, thereby enhancing the cycling stability of ASSLSBs.

For the assessment of practical applications, ASSLSBs were
then assembled with the Li-In alloy anode and sulfur cathodes.
Compared with pure lithium foil, the Li-In alloy possesses the
advantage to stabilize lithium anode during repeated opera-
tions. It is well known that the electrochemical performance
of ASSLSBs is highly sensitive to the stack pressure; therefore,
cycling tests were performed across a range of externally applied
pressures (60, 80, and 100 MPa) using the customized fixture
mold to investigate the pressure dependence (Figure S13). The
results show that a higher stack pressure enhances the initial
specific capacity by improving the interfacial contact; however,
excessively high pressure increases the risk of short‑circuit failure
(Figure S14). Therefore, the optimized pressure of 80 MPa was
applied for all electrochemical measurements in this study.
At the galvanostatic testing condition within the voltage range
from 0.9 to 2.4 V (vs. Li-In/Li+), the battery with the CGS cathode
delivered a high initial specific capacity of 1315 mAh g−1 at 0.1 C
under a sulfur loading of 7.6 mg cm−2, which corresponds to a
high sulfur utilization of 78.9% (Figure 5a). In contrast, the battery
with the CS cathode showed a lower capacity of 1095 mAh g−1
under the same testing conditions. Besides, it exhibited larger
polarization than the CGS counterpart, which indicates less
favorable reaction kinetics within the CS cathode system. The
differential capacity curves were then derived from galvanostatic
charge and discharge profiles to analyze the electrochemical
conversion reactions of sulfur (Figure 5b). During the discharge
process, a prominent peak at 1.41 V (peak II) accompanied by
a small peak at 1.48 V (peak I) was observed in the CGS. The
peak II is widely assigned to the electrochemical process in which
sulfur is reduced to the final product of Li2S. The appearance
of small peak I indicates the formation of lithium polysulfide
intermediates, which is conducive to promoting the kinetics
of solid-solid sulfur reaction. When being charged, the peak
observed at 1.70 V (peak III) corresponds to the oxidation of Li2S
to sulfur. In contrast, the CS cathode exhibits significantly weaker
redox peaks with smaller integrated areas, pointing to insufficient
sulfur utilization and the resulting lower capacity. Compared to
the CGS, the redox peak gap in the CS is much broader, revealing
its slower reaction kinetics and larger polarization. Furthermore,
a slope at 2.40 V, corresponding to the SE oxidation, is observed
during charging for both CS and CGS cathodes. The much gentler
slope in the CGS can be attributed to its concentration-gradient
structure, which effectively mitigates the SE decomposition. To
evaluate the cycling performance, the batteries were cycled at the
current density of 0.1 C (Figure 5c,d). The obtained results show
that the CGS cathode group can be steadily operated over 225
cycles with a capacity retention of 88.9%. However, the control
group of the CS cathode experienced a sharp capacity decay
from an initial 1115 mAh g−1 to less than 390 mAh g−1 after only
110 cycles. Moreover, the discharge voltage plateaus of the CGS
cathode group exhibit superior stability with a minimal decay
rate of 0.46 mV per cycle, which is significantly lower than that
of the CS cathode (2.00 mV per cycle). It demonstrates that the
kinetically favorable electrochemical reactions enabled by the
strategically designed CGS cathode are effectively maintained
with negligible polarization growth during the repeated charge
and discharge processes (Figure S15).
Furthermore, the rate capability was evaluated at increasing
current densities of 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, and 1 C. As shown in Figure 5e,
the corresponding capacities are 1287, 1070, 735, and 451 mAh g−1
for the battery using the CGS as the cathode. When the current
density was returned to 0.1 C after cycling at 1 C, the battery with
the CGS cathode restored a high capacity of 1165 mAh g−1, which
highlights the exceptional reversibility and structural stability of
the CGS-based battery system. By comparison, the battery with
the CS cathode showed a marked decrease in capacity, delivering
only 1054, 761, 433, and 180 mAh g−1 at the same current densities
ranging from 0.1 to 1 C. In addition, the capacity dropped to
793 mAh g−1 at 0.1 C after the rate performance test, revealing its
poor reversibility.
As previously discussed, maintaining favorable reaction kinetics
under high sulfur loadings (>10 mg cm−2) remains a great chal-
lenge for developing high-energy-density ASSLSBs [8]. To probe
the role of the concentration-gradient structure in enhancing
reaction kinetics, we further increased the sulfur loading from 7.6
to 11.4 mg cm−2. From Figure 5f, it can be observed that the areal
capacity of the CGS-based battery can reach up to 10.96 mAh cm−2
at 0.1 C, which is much higher than that of the CS-based battery
(8.65 mAh cm−2). Besides, it showed smaller polarization than
the CS group, which indicates the CGS cathode can effectively
promote the sulfur conversion reaction kinetics even at a high
sulfur loading of 11.4 mg cm−2 (Figure S16). Moreover, the areal
capacity of the CGS-based battery can be retained at a relatively
high level of 7.2 mAh cm−2 after 100 cycles (Figure 5g). However,
the capacity of CS-based battery has quickly decayed from 8.6
to 2.7 mAh cm−2. The high areal capacity delivered by the
CGS-based battery remains competitive with previously reported
systems [14, 15, 18, 26–39], which can be attributed to its enhanced
reaction kinetics and improved sulfur utilization (Figure 5h).

was fabricated by sequentially stacking layers of SE65, SE55, and

3
Conclusion
In summary, a spatially modulated sulfur cathode was developed
by introducing dual-concentration gradients of the chlorine-rich
SE and SWCNT conductive agent across the electrode from the
separator to the current collector. Promoted by the concentration
gradients, rapid and uniform transport of both lithium ions and
electrons is achieved, which facilitates electrochemical reaction
kinetics. Moreover, the minimized interfacial contact between
SE and conductive agent has been demonstrated to mitigate
the SE degradation efficiently and guarantee stable lithium-
ion conduction. As a result, the sulfur utilization and cyclic
stability of ASSLSB are simultaneously promoted, even when
the areal loading is increased to 11.4 mg cm−2, a high areal
capacity of 10.96 mAh cm−2 is realized with durable operation
over 100 cycles. Owing to the operational feasibility, this strategy
enables the scalable fabrication of high-sulfur-loading cathodes
and promotes the practical application of high-energy-density
ASSLSBs. Furthermore, our findings offer valuable insights into
the electrode design through compositional optimization, which
may be applicable to other electrochemical energy storage and
conversion systems that require high-loading electrodes with
improved reaction kinetics.
