Growing concerns about safety incidents and range anxiety in pure electric vehicles powered by
lithium-ion batteries have led to increasing interest in solid-state lithium metal batteries. Compared to
non-aqueous liquid electrolytes, the use of solid-state electrolytes not only avoids volatility, flammability,
and short-circuits, but also inhibits the crossover of active species in various lithium-metal batteries,
such as Li–sulfur, Li–organic and Li–air batteries. Among them, active species crossover comprises oxy-
gen crossover, organic molecule diffusion, the shuttle effect of soluble polysulfides and lithiated organics
in
non-aqueous
electrolytes.
Although
different active species
possess diverse
physicochemical
properties, the challenge of crossover can be effectively addressed with solid-state electrolytes. Based
on the above, this review focuses on the application of solid-state electrolytes for handling active
species crossover in lithium metal batteries. Moreover, the key factors determining the usability of the
method are summarized based on current applications, guiding strategies for inhibiting active species
crossover in lithium-metal batteries. Finally, this review offers an insight into future research on practical
solid-state lithium metal batteries.
1. Introduction
Energy storage is indispensable for modern human survival
and technological advancement. Li-ion batteries have become a
mainline energy storage technology in numerous fields includ-
ing portable electronic devices, electric vehicles and energy
storage power stations by virtue of their fabulous specific
energy, energetic eﬃciency and service life.1 In order to avoid
climate catastrophe and fossil energy depletion, there is an
urgent need for energy restructuring, and many countries have
gradually promoted ‘‘carbon neutrality’’ as a national strategy.
Therefore, Li-ion batteries will meet more stringent require-
ments to maintain their predominance.2 Unfortunately, the
energy density of Li-ion batteries based on intercalation mate-
rials has approached the theoretical limits through nonaqu-
eous electrolytes and component structure optimization.3
Then, it is imperative to exploit state-of-the-art battery chemis-
tries superior to Li-ion battery chemistry.
Among various anodes, lithium metal is appealing in repla-
cing graphite because of its ultrahigh theoretical specific
capacity (3860 mA h g1) and ultralow reduction potential
(3.04 V vs. standard hydrogen electrode). Li metal batteries
(LMBs) employing lithium metal as an anode oﬀer feasibility
beyond Li-ion batteries. However, lithium metal anodes (LMAs)
are subject to serious challenges, such as short circuits and
lithium depletion respectively caused by lithium dendrites and
side reactions.4,5 In recent decades, various research studies on
protected LMAs have been popping up, involving artificial solid
electrolyte interphases (SEIs), 3D lithium hosts, electrolyte
design, etc.6,7 Concretely, protected LMAs have been matched
with intercalation compound LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 (NCM811) to
achieve a remarkable breakthrough of 400 W h kg1, but its
lifespan still needs to be improved.8 Indeed, Li-free conversion
cathodes (e.g., S and O2) substituting intercalation cathodes
will further increase the energy density in LMBs.9 Although Li-
free conversion cathodes possess higher specific capacity, their
reaction kinetics are sluggish, which hinders practical applica-
tions of Li–sulfur batteries and Li–air batteries.10–12 Among
various cathodes, there is a special category of organic cathodes
whose capacity is approximately between those of intercalation
cathodes and sulfur, but organic cathodes feature fast reaction
kinetics. Since all the above three systems will grant a new high-
density energy limit for Li metal batteries, Li–sulfur, Li–organic

solid-state electrolytes. It is clear that solid-state electrolytes are
a pivotal research direction for Li metal batteries.

2. Active species crossover eﬀect
2.1.
Shuttle eﬀect of soluble polysulfides
Li–sulfur batteries are a class of Li metal batteries employing
sulfur as a cathode. The electrochemical reaction in which S8
and Li2S mutually convert involves the solid–liquid–solid trans-
formation process in conventional ether-based electrolytes, and
the conversion reaction provides an ultrahigh theoretical capa-
city of 1675 mA h g1. During discharge, solid S8 is sequentially
reduced to soluble long-chain and short-chain lithium poly-
sulfides (PS) followed by solid Li2S deposition (Fig. 1(a)).23
From the reaction mechanism, the insulating nature of S8
and Li2S results in sluggish redox kinetics, and soluble lithium
polysulfides in nonaqueous electrolytes cause irreversible capa-
city loss. However, soluble lithium polysulfides favor sulfur
utilization and reaction kinetics as intermediate products.
Hence, it is inevitable that the existence of PS is a double-
edged sword in Li–sulfur batteries. As shown in Fig. 1(b), sulfur
electrochemistry is divided into PS-bypass solid conversion and
PS-retain solid–liquid conversion depending on whether PS
participates in electrochemical reactions.24 The charge–dis-
charge curve displays a two-plateau profile in solid–liquid
conversion, while there is a single plateau in solid conversion.
In solid–liquid conversion, PS continuously formed and dis-
solved in nonaqueous electrolytes during discharge, which
exposes inner parts of sulfur particles to maximize sulfur
utilization. Nevertheless, the formed PS inevitably leads to a
notorious shuttle effect of soluble polysulfides, which will
cause overcharging. The oxidized long-chain PS shuttle to the
anode and are chemically reduced to short-chain PS, which
subsequently diffuse back to the cathode and are electroche-
mically oxidized back into long-chain PS during the charging
process. In order to circumvent the shuttle effect, strategies
including polymer vulcanization, use of solid-state electrolytes
and microporous adsorption are explored to realize better cycle
stability in PS-bypass solid conversion. Simultaneously, the
amount of electrolyte used is another critical condition

2.2.
Diﬀusion and the shuttle eﬀect of organic species
Li–organic batteries are a class of Li metal batteries employing
organics as cathodes. Organic cathodes have different reaction
mechanisms depending on their functional groups. Then, the
shuttle effect of organic cathodes is discussed according to the
classification of organic cathodes. Organic cathodes can be
classified into N-type, P-type and bipolar-type based on the
charge variation of active centers (Fig. 2(a)).28 Specifically, N-
type organics such as carbonyl, cyanide and azo compounds are
generally reduced to anions interacting with Li+, while P-type
organics such as thioether, nitrogen radicals and conducting
polymers are oxidized to cations interacting with the anions of
lithium salts. As shown in Fig. 2(b), bipolar-type organics
incorporate the reaction mechanisms of both N-type and P-type
organics.29 In fact, there are pending research studies on new
reaction mechanisms of other organic cathodes. Undeniably,
the electrochemical reactions of many organic cathodes are
commonly faster than those of inorganic materials, because
many organic cathodes tend to be accompanied by limited
rearrangement of bonds and minimal structural changes dur-
ing electrochemical reactions.30 In marked contrast, sulfur and
oxygen cathodes undergo large structural and volumetric
changes owing to the conversion reaction. Therefore, organic
cathodes draw more and more attention because of fast
reaction
kinetics
and
abundant
structural
designability.
Organic cathodes naturally have drawbacks regarding their
electronic conductivity, tap density and capacity retention,
any of which would be a huge challenge for commercial

2.3.
Oxygen crossover eﬀect
Li–air batteries were first proposed by Blurton and Sammells in
1979.38 Subsequently, the chargeability of Li–air batteries
(LABs) was verified by Abraham and Jiang in the mid-1990s.39
In 2006, Bruce et al. demonstrated that Li2O2 could be a
discharge product for reversible charge/discharge reactions in
their system with the nonaqueous electrolyte and MnO2
catalyst.40 Since then, LABs have made great progress in
research and development. So far, Li–air batteries have pro-
vided a high practical capacity of 3500 W h kg1 (based on Li
and O2), matching the usable energy density of gasoline. Three
types of liquid LABs incorporating nonaqueous, aqueous,
hybrid nonaqueous/aqueous have been reported. Compared
with aqueous electrolytes with low decomposition voltage,
nonaqueous liquid LABs have gained more attention due to
their high energy density and simple structure.41

2.4.
Strategies for inhibiting the crossover eﬀect
There are numerous strategies for inhibiting the crossover
eﬀect in Li–sulfur, Li–organic and Li–air batteries. In the
section, we will sort out the advantages and disadvantages of
different strategies. First of all, most strategies definitely are
based on the decoration of cathodes, anodes, separators and
electrolytes.53–57 In terms of cathodes, strategies for inhibiting
the crossover effect comprise novel cathode synthesis, micro-
porous adsorption and chemical adsorption, which inevitably
compromise active material ratios. In terms of anodes and
separators, strategies essentially prevent anodes from corrosion
by active species crossover, which cannot circumvent active
species dissolution and electrolyte decomposition in nonaqu-
eous electrolytes. Electrolytes as ionic conductors in batteries
actually play a vital role in active species dissolution and
electrode–electrolyte interphase formation. Hence, electrolyte
design is expected to solve active species crossover without
sacrificing the electrode capacity. In terms of electrolytes,
weakly solvating electrolytes, highly concentrated electrolytes
and localized high-concentration electrolytes are utilized to
construct
anion-derived
electrode–electrolyte
interphases,
which realize high coulombic efficiency.58–60 However, it is
difficult to simultaneously realize favorable regulation of
solid–electrolyte interphases and cathode–electrolyte inter-
phases through electrolyte design. And Li metal batteries in
which nonaqueous electrolytes couple with lithium metal
anodes still face serious safety issues such as lithium dendrites,
thermal runaway and flammable electrolytes.61 Then solid-state
Li metal batteries have attracted growing interest in virtue of
their nonvolatility, nonflammability, air stability, high Young’s
modulus and inhibiting active species crossover. In the next
section, we will introduce the application of solid-state electro-
lytes to handle active species crossover in Li–sulfur, Li–organic
and Li–air batteries, and discuss compatibility between solid-
state electrolytes and cathodes. In addition, we will propose
selection criteria of solid-state electrolytes for the distinct
nature of active species.
3. Solid-state electrolytes for Li metal
batteries
Pairing with solid-state electrolytes (SSEs), solid-state Li metal
batteries are considered safer than nonaqueous lithium-ion
batteries.62,63 Moreover, solid-state electrolytes can utilize bipo-
lar electrodes to internally serial connect batteries, which can
further decrease bulk volume and manufacturing cost. Judging
from materials, solid-state electrolytes are classified as polymer
electrolytes, inorganic solid electrolytes and composite electro-
lytes. However, there may be a small amount of liquid electro-
lyte in solid-state electrolytes owing to the residual solvent after
processing, which is beneficial for ionic conductivity in solid-
state electrolytes, especially polymer electrolytes. Hence, quasi-
solid electrolytes are utilized to describe these electrolytes that
contain both liquid electrolytes and solid electrolytes. In an
early stage, liquid electrolytes were even intentionally intro-
duced into solid electrolytes to improve ionic conductivity at
room temperature, because ionic conductivity in solid-state
electrolytes is the most important parameter which determines
their application potential. Solid polymer electrolytes possess a
conductivity of 109–104 S cm1 due to the restricted motion
of the chain segment at room temperature. Currently, the
improvement of ionic conductivity largely relies on the mod-
ification of lithium salts and polymer matrices in solid polymer
electrolytes. Inorganic solid electrolytes including oxides, sul-
fides and halides have a conductivity of 106–102 S cm1,
which is mainly limited by bulk phase resistance and grain
boundary resistance. As for inorganic solid electrolytes, ionic
conductivity, air stability and electrochemical stability are
mainly optimized by element doping and lattice structure
adjustment. Considering the diverse properties of solid poly-
mer and inorganic solid electrolytes, they are compounded into
composite electrolytes to achieve the optimum performance by
replenishing their shortcomings. Thus, composite electrolytes
are expected to enable reliable operation of solid-state Li metal
batteries at room temperature. Subsequently, we will introduce
solid-state Li–sulfur, Li–organic and Li–air batteries in terms of
electrolyte materials. Since solvent residues are inevitable and
favorable in certain polymer electrolytes, some research on
quasi-solid electrolytes will be mentioned in the section on
polymer and composite electrolytes.

formation of SEI films, and LiTFSI was substituted by LiFSI to
form robust SEI films and increase coulombic efficiency.65
Considering the lower initial capacity in LiFSI/PEO based Li–
sulfur batteries, LiTFSI–LiFSI binary-salts were easy to imple-
ment and integrate advantages of both salts, and researchers
observed different failure mechanisms of two monosalt sys-

was conceived to merge the beneficial features of LiFSI and
LiTFSI, which is compatible with Li and sulfur simultaneously.
Anion regulation in lithium salts can also increase the ionic
conductivity of polymer electrolytes. As shown in Fig. 4(a), PEO
can form a hydrogen bond with a weakly acidic CF2H moiety

enhances Li+ conductivity.66 However, there is the shuttle effect

the limited control of the shuttle effect by lithium salt mod-
ification. Apart from perfluorinated lithium salts, lithium tri-
cyanomethanide (LiTCM) is a high-performance fluorine-free
salt which is able to maintain stability with polysulfides
(Fig. 4(b)).67 The new lithium salts are always introduced with
careful consideration of the dissociation degree and economic
feasibility, but only a small amount of additive is enough to
adjust the interface compatibility. Besides, additives can be
discovered by analysis of the critical components of robust SEI
films. Among various SEI compositions, Li3N possesses a
higher ionic conductivity of 6  103 S cm1 than Li2S
(1013 S cm1), LiF (1031 S cm1) and Li2CO3 (108 S cm1)
at 25 1C. Then lithium azide (LiN3) is proposed as an additive

prevents anode corrosion from the shuttle effect (Fig. 4(c)).68
Solid polymer electrolytes tend to dissolve and transport
polysulfides due to elevated operating temperature and motion
of polymer chain segments.65 It is diﬃcult to inhibit the shuttle
eﬀect of soluble polysulfides in solid polymer electrolytes.

in solid polymer electrolytes, indicating that the shuttle effect
of soluble polysulfides cannot be inhibited. Modification of
lithium salts and additives can only regulate deposition of
soluble polysulfides by tuning ionic interactions and interfacial
stability of polymer electrolytes. It is a facile and effective
strategy for immobilized anions by incorporating functional
interlayers in electrode–electrolyte interfaces. Recently, a class
of gel polymer electrolytes blocking liquid plasticizers by cross-
linking of polymers have been investigated, which can be
compatible with functional interlayers to suppress the migra-
tion of soluble polysulfides. As shown in Fig. 4(d), carbonyl
groups in polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) can impede the
migration of soluble polysulfides by chemical coordination. In
addition, polymethyl methacrylate is covered with acetylene
black which alleviates inferior conductivity and polysulfide
diffusion of sulfur.69 Meanwhile, crystalline porous materials
including zeolites, mesoporous silicon and metal–organic fra-
meworks (MOF) can modify gel polymer electrolytes based on
the Lewis acid–base effect or pore size effects. Concretely, the
gel polymer electrolyte modified by Mg-based MOF can fix
polysulfides and TFSI (Fig. 4(f)), and it achieves a higher Li+
transference number compared to the poly(vinylidene fluoride)
(PVDF) electrolyte.70 Thus, the foregoing research enabled us to
recognize that introducing polar functional groups into poly-
mer matrices can hinder the shuttle effect by electrostatic
repulsion or chemical coordination. Among various polymer
matrices, single-ion conductor polymers are the most represen-
tative research using the immobilization of anions. As shown in
Fig. 4(e), there is a solid–liquid conversion reaction because of
the dissolution of polysulfides in organic solvents, but the
nanochannels of electrolyte only allow Li+ transfer due to
Donnan exclusion.71 More interestingly, PVDF used as a binder
in the cathode can be an interlayer in PEO-based Li–sulfur
batteries (Fig. 4(g)), and researchers confirm that polysulfides
are insoluble in PVDF by density functional theory (DFT).72
Although there are limited experiments achieving the solid
conversion reaction of sulfur, the design of versatile interlayers
including novel polymers and nanoporous materials offers the
possibility for solid conversion reactions.

In Li–air batteries, the selection criteria of polymer electro-
lytes are distinctly different from those of the other two
systems, and they will be introduced exclusively in the next
paragraph. As for Li–sulfur batteries, it is difficult to inhibit the
shuttle effect of soluble polysulfides in solid polymer electro-
lytes, because the modification of lithium salts and additives
can only regulate deposition of soluble polysulfides by tuning
ionic interactions and interfacial stability of polymer electro-
lytes. However, the introduction of functional interlayers in gel
polymer electrolytes can suppress the migration of soluble
polysulfides. In Li–organic batteries, many organics have simi-
lar time-dependent capacity degradation driven by the diffu-
sion of active species. Therefore, polar groups in polymer solid
electrolytes can block the diffusion of active species by inter-
acting with organic molecules. Besides, single-ion conductive
polymers can also suppress the shuttle effect of active species.
Nevertheless, the Li+ transference number of single-ion con-
ductive polymers is not exactly equal to 1, and there are
indistinguishable diffusion and the shuttle effect of active
species in Li–organic batteries. Thus, solid-sate Li–organic
batteries should pay more attention to inorganic solid electro-
lytes in the future.
For polymer electrolytes in Li–air batteries, the incorpora-
tion of plasticizers to form gel polymer electrolytes enhances
the ionic conductivity and improves the densification, which
contributes to blocking the crossover of oxygen molecules. And
it is necessary to consider not only the air stability, but also
chemical stability in the nucleophilic environment formed by
the presence of Li2O2 as a discharge product in LABs. For
example, under the influence of strongly nucleophilic Li2O2,
PEO may cross-link, PAN containing electrophilic nitrile groups
is susceptible to nucleophilic attack, and halogenated polymers
(PVC, PVDF) undergo dehydrohalogenation reactions. In con-
trast, PTFE and perfluorosulfonic acid resin (Nafion) are more
chemically stable due to the lack of a-H or b-H in the vicinity of
the fluoride group. However, Li2O2 is not the only factor to
consider for chemical stability. PMMA with methyl and meth-
oxy groups has low reactivity with Li2O2, but the presence of
water molecules in the cell will promote the decomposition
reaction of PMMA. Therefore, the selection criteria of polymer
electrolytes in LABs require a combination of air stability and
chemical stability of discharge products.
Among the inorganic solid electrolytes, inorganic sulfides
have the merits of high conductivity, low interfacial impen-
dence and favorable compatibility with Li–In alloy anodes.
Sulfide solid electrolytes are compatible with sulfur and orga-
nosulfur cathodes owing to a mutually matched electrochemi-
cal window, but they are less applicable to Li–air batteries
owing to the susceptibility of humidity and O2. Besides, it is
difficult for sulfide solid electrolytes to relieve the interfacial
strain caused by sulfur conversion reactions, which will result
in deterioration of interfacial contact. Therefore, organosulfur
cathodes are more suitable for sulfide solid electrolytes due to
slight volume expansion of organics. And organosulfur cath-
odes are different from quinone based cathodes that are
subject to nucleophilic side reactions with sulfide solid electro-
lytes. Compared to sulfides, oxide solid electrolytes are more
stable to air and thus become the superior choice for solid-state
electrolytes in Li–air batteries, but they are less applicable to Li–
organic batteries due to their poor interfacial contact. Sulfur is
compatible with oxide solid electrolytes which can offer porous
sulfur frameworks for close interfacial contact. In addition,
appropriate interlayers should be introduced in the oxide solid
electrolytes (e.g., LATP) which are chemically incompatibility
with sulfur.
Due to the complementary strengths of diverse solid-state
electrolytes, composite electrolytes will be the most promising
SSEs for future Li–sulfur, Li–organic and Li–air batteries.
Beyond the merits for addressing specific issues, composite
electrolytes generally possess better interfacial compatibility
than inorganic solid electrolytes, and higher ionic conductivity
than polymer solid electrolytes. Naturally, there are different
advantages specifically for Li–sulfur, Li–organic and Li–air
batteries. In Li–sulfur and Li–organic batteries, appropriate
composite electrolytes have two important features of dissol-
ving and blocking active species, which can enable fast reaction
kinetics and cycling stability simultaneously. Thus, it is essen-
tial to harmonize the comprehensive performance by scientifi-
cally compounding electrolytes that dissolve active species and
ones that block active species. As for Li–air batteries, the air
stability, chemical stability as well as homogeneity of the
composite solid electrolytes should be considered. About the
crossover problem, a more consistent and conductive SEI layer
may be constructed to resist the crossover of air molecules in
the semi-open environment by doping composite solid electro-
lytes with ionic liquids. In summary, since uneven dispersion of
fillers will lower ionic conductivity, mechanical properties and
cost-effectiveness, it is vital to establish a reliable process in
composite electrolytes, ensuring economy, homogenization
and reproducibility. Although composite electrolytes make
numerous breakthroughs towards commercializing Li-metal
batteries, there is a long road ahead for the development of
composite electrolytes that inhibit crossover of active species.
4. Conclusions and perspectives
In lithium metal batteries, active species crossover not only
leads to rapid cathode capacity degradation due to dissolution
of the active species, but also due to lithium consumption or
passivation because of side reactions between the dissolved
active species and lithium. To solve the crossover problem,
solid-state electrolytes have attracted more attention, which can
avoid volatility, flammability and short circuits, and eﬀectively
inhibit crossover of active species. According to the character-
istics of diﬀerent cathode systems and the physical and
chemical properties of the active species, the selection criteria

highlighted and summarized:
(1) The safety risks caused by Li dendrite growth are an
urgent challenge to be addressed in LMBs. Although the
propagation of lithium dendrites is alleviated in solid electro-
lytes, they can still penetrate in grain boundaries, surface
defects and co-terminated pores. Drawing on the strategies of
Li anode protection, artificial SEI membranes, 3D hosts and
film-forming additives are developed for suppressing Li den-
drites. However, the thickness of artificial SEI membranes,
porosity of 3D hosts and sustainability of film-forming addi-
tives should reach the stringent standard to ensure the practi-
cality. Besides, strategies to inhibit the growth of Li dendrites
and reduce the interfacial impedance can be implemented by
substituting Li metal with Li alloys, which can eﬀectively
guarantee anode stability and volumetric energy density. Mean-
while, advanced characterization techniques and simulations
are required to understand the growth and destruction
mechanisms of lithium dendrites, guiding future interface
design between solid-state electrolytes and the lithium anode.
(2) The electrochemical performance degradation caused by
interfacial deterioration is the main hurdle for solid-state
LMBs. Because electrode/electrolyte interfaces involve electro-
chemical reactions, Li+ transportation and unfavorable side
reactions, building chemically and physically compatible inter-
faces is pivotal for solid-state LMBs. Especially for inorganic
solid electrolytes and conversion electrodes, there are chal-
lenges of poor contact, considerable strain and uneven Li+
migration at the interfaces. As for interfacial optimization, it
is imperative to introduce interlayers such as mixing ion/
electron conductors, Li+ conducting porous materials and gel
polymers for Li+ uniform distribution and mechanical strain
buﬀering. Furthermore, the selection of interlayers should
ensure their chemical stability with electrodes and solid-state
electrolytes, and they can be used as a functional component to
regulate chemical compatibility of electrodes and solid-state
electrolytes.
(3) The low area capacity and energy eﬃciency are jointly
caused by slow ionic mobility of solid-state electrolytes and
sluggish reaction kinetics of conversion cathodes, which hin-
ders practicality in solid-state LMBs. Among various solid-state
electrolytes, polymer solid electrolytes cannot be utilized at
room temperature due to inferior ionic conductivity. Never-
theless, the introduction of plasticizers, inorganic fillers and
ionic liquids into polymer solid electrolytes can improve their
ionic conductivity at room temperature. Considering the reac-
tion kinetics of conversion cathodes, the utilization of redox
mediators can eﬀectively decrease the overpotential, facilitate
reaction kinetics, and curb the side reactions. In the future
commercialization of solid-state Li–sulfur and Li–air batteries,
redox mediators can also play a vital role in facilitating the
rechargeability of the battery and improving energy efficiency.
(4) The specific capacity far below the theoretical value is
caused by low utilization of active materials. Particularly for
solid-state Li–organic batteries, there are serious defects in
active material utilization, electronic conductivity and tap
density, which will severely limit their commercial application.
Therefore, surface modification of conductive carbon is an
important research direction for ensuring uniform and close
contact between organic materials and conductive carbon.
Besides, the polymerization, salt formation and covalent graft-
ing of organic molecules can improve cycling stability and
utilization of active materials. However, there are challenges
of a low-loading cathode, low working voltage and complex
synthesis in organic cathodes. Thus, it is urgent to develop
novel organic cathodes compatible with solid-state electrolytes
by molecule design.
(5) The research progress in solid-state LMBs is slow and
stagnant due to the insuﬃcient mechanisms and kinetics
study. Although solid-state electrolytes play a role in inhibiting
active species crossover, there is a lack of in situ experimental
characterization in solid-state electrolytes. On the other hand,
the electrochemical mechanism of sulfur, organic and oxygen
cathodes in solid-state electrolytes is completely diﬀerent from
ones in nonaqueous electrolytes. And the same solid-state
electrolytes might play diﬀerent roles due to diverse physico-
chemical properties of active species. Therefore, the unclear
mechanisms of crossover suppression are eagerly required to
be explored. Advanced characterization techniques including
solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance, electron paramagnetic
