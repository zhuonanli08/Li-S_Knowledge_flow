ABSTRACT
All-solid-state Li–S and Na–S batteries (ASSSBs) employing highly conductive sulfide solid electrolytes combine high energy
density with improved safety by enforcing solid–solid sulfur conversion and eliminating polysulfide shuttling. However, sulfide
electrolytes possess narrow electrochemical stability windows, while sulfur’s low conductivity, sluggish redox kinetics, and ∼80%
volume change further challenge high-loading (>30 wt.% S, or > 3 mg cm−2) operation under practical stack pressures. This
perspective adopts an intrinsic chemical framework to dissect sulfur conversion in ASSSBs, emphasizing the coupled chemistry of
sulfur, conductive carbon, and sulfide electrolytes during both fabrication and cycling. Sulfur redox pathways in liquid versus solid-
state systems are compared, followed by an analysis of electrolyte stability, S/electrolyte interactions, and interphase evolution.
The differences in redox pathways for Li–S vs Na–S are discussed. Strategies to enhance sulfur reactivity and to tailor electrolyte
behavior are then evaluated, culminating in design principles for high-loading, low-pressure ASSSBs capable of meeting practical
performance targets.
1
Introduction
Sulfur (S) is an attractive cathode for rechargeable batteries
owing to its abundance, low cost, and high theoretical capacity
(1676 mAh g−1) [1–3]. In conventional Li–S and Na–S cells
with liquid electrolytes, sulfur undergoes a solid–liquid–solid
conversion via soluble polysulfides, which enables fast kinetics
but induces the shuttle effect, rapid capacity decay, and safety
concerns associated with flammable electrolytes [2–5]. Replacing
the liquid with a solid-state electrolyte enforces a solid–solid
conversion, suppressing polysulfide dissolution while improving
safety. Among solid-state electrolytes, sulfide-based solid state
electrolytes (SSEs) combine high ionic conductivity (surpassing
that of liquid electrolytes, LiSiCePSBr [6]: 32 mS cm−1, argyrodite
[7]: >10 mS cm−1) with good processability and mechanical com-
pliance, making them leading candidates for high-performance
solid-state sulfur batteries [8, 9].
Unlike in liquid systems, sulfur redox in solid-state cathodes is
intimately coupled to the electrochemical and chemical behavior
of the SSE. As shown in Figure 1, the conversion involves
three interacting components—sulfur, conductive carbon (C),
and the solid electrolyte—linked through reversible S ↔Li2S
transformations, electrolyte decomposition, and S/SSE interfacial
reactions. These processes occur across S–SSE, S–C, and SSE–
C contacts, as well as at S–SSE–C triple-phase boundaries.

Conductive carbon facilitates electron transport but can also
accelerate SSE redox. Importantly, these reactions are not only
initiated during electrochemical cycling but can be precondi-
tioned by electrode fabrication: high-energy ball milling [10] or
wet processing [11] may induce mechanochemical or solvation-
driven reactions, phase transformations, and interfacial recon-
struction prior to the first cycle. As a result, the initial chemical
state of the cathode can differ significantly from that of the
starting materials.
During operation, the narrow stability window of most sulfide
SSEs [15, 19–21] means that sulfur reduction/oxidation and elec-
trolyte decomposition proceed concurrently, generating evolving
interfaces enriched in decomposition products, radical or poly-
sulfide intermediates, and mechanical stresses. These interfacial
phases can act as ionic/electronic conductors/insulators [22–25],
or catalytic sites [26], thereby altering sulfur reaction pathways
and reversibility.
Recent reviews have already summarized the chemistry of poly-
sulfides [3], S/C composites [27], and the properties of solid state
electrolytes [20, 28]. They have also discussed the state-of-the-
art Li–S [5, 29–31] and Na–S [4] systems, offering perspectives
on their development from both chemical engineering [32]
and industrialization [2] viewpoints. However, the underlying
chemical mechanisms governing sulfur transformation in the
presence of sulfide SSEs, particularly the coupled chemistry
of sulfur, conductive carbon, and sulfide electrolytes during
manufacturing and cycling processes remain poorly understood.
Here, we adopt an intrinsic chemical perspective to dissect solid-
state sulfur conversion, focusing on i) solid-solid conversion of S;
ii) the (electro)chemical stability and decomposition chemistry of
sulfide electrolytes, iii) S–SSE interface transformations induced
during cathode fabrication, and iv) the coupled evolution of S–
SSE interfaces during cycling. Building on these insights, we
examine current Li–S and Na–S all-solid-state battery technolo-
gies and highlight opportunities for advancing their performance
and longevity.

Na2S2, Na2S4, and Na2S5 [34] (Figure 2c). Notably, many lithium

Electrochemical discharge is initiated when electrophilic alkali
cations (Li+, Na+, K+) attack electron-rich sulfur atoms, cleaving
S–S bonds to form high-order polysulfides(M2S8). Progressive
reduction shortens the sulfur chain length until monosulfide
(M2S) is obtained [3]. Despite involving the same reactants
and products, the phases observed during solid-liquid-solid con-
version differ from those in solid-solid conversion, revealing
fundamentally distinct sulfur conversion mechanisms in liquid
and solid states (Figure 2d,e).
In liquid Li–S batteries (Figure 2d), the conversion of S8 to
dissolved high-order polysulfides (Li2S8) produces the first volt-
age plateau [36], followed by a sloping potential during the
reduction to lower-order species (Li2S6, Li2S4) and a second
plateau upon Li2S2 precipitation. This solid-liquid-solid process
and the corresponding intermediates have been characterized by
Raman spectroscopy [37], UV–vis [36], XAS [42], and TEM [43,
44]. The final Li2S2 →Li2S step, hindered by the poor conductivity

insulating nature of Li2S, the transformation of Li2S2 to Li2S is
considered the rate-limiting step in liquid electrolyte batteries.
While dissolved polysulfides facilitate fast electron transfer across
multiple interfaces, their mobility causes the shuttle effect. In
parallel, Li2S6 ↔Li2S3∙reaction generates S3∙−free radicals,
which was identified [37] by the Raman peak at 531 cm−1. The
reaction pathway in Na–S liquid electrolyte batteries (Figure 2d)
is similar to that in Li–S batteries, involving a transition from S8
to soluble higher-order polysulfides (Na2S8, Na2S6, Na2S5, Na2S4)
and then to insoluble lower-order polysulfides (Na2S3, Na2S2,
Na2S) [4, 38].
In solid-state Li–S cells (Figure 2e), sulfur is reduced via solid–
solid pathways, producing broad, monotonic discharge profiles
without distinct liquid–solid nucleation plateaus [1, 45], consis-
tent with the Gibbs phase rule and phase-diagram predictions.
The lack of liquid-to-solid nucleation may contribute to faster
sulfur transformation, which is consistent with the unexpectedly
high-rate performance reported by Song et al., [46]. Transmission
Electron Microscopy (TEM) observations in carbon-coated S
electrodes with a native Li2O solid-electrolyte layer have revealed
direct S →Li2S conversion without detectable lithium polysul-
fides [13, 47]. However, in cells using sulfide SSEs (e.g., Li6PS5Cl,
Li10GeP2S12, Li7P3S11), Li2S2 is often identified as an intermediate
during both lithiation and delithiation [26, 45]. At elevated
temperatures (60◦C), an asymmetric redox pathway emerges:
lithiation proceeds via S8 →Li2S4 →Li2S, while delithiation
occurs directly from Li2S to S8 [48]. Density functional theory
(DFT) calculations predict that these SSEs are prone to decompo-
sition into Li3PS4, Li2S, and other phases such as LiCl [22, 49, 50],
and interfacial reactions between sulfur and SSEs have been
experimentally confirmed [11, 51, 52]. However, the impact of such
reactions on conversion kinetics and reversibility remains poorly
understood.
Solid-state Na–S cells (Figure 2e) operate under kinetic con-
trol [53]. At elevated temperatures and low current densities,
they often exhibit multiple discharge plateaus, indicative of
intermediate-phase participation. Na2S2 and Na2Sx (x = 4–5) are
typically observed prior to Na2S formation. In contrast, at low
temperatures or under high current densities, the Na2S2 step is
kinetically bypassed, leading to a single discharge plateau dom-
inated by direct Na2S formation [54]. When Na3PS4 is replaced
by Na4(B10H10)(B12H12) as the electrolyte, both crystalline and
amorphous sodium polysulfides were detected, including phases
from the Na–S phase diagram (Na2S5, Na2S4, Na2S2, Na2S), higher-
order species (Na2Sx, x = 6–8) and Na2S3 (appears only under
high-temperature or high-pressure conditions) [55]. Note the
use of the boride electrolytes facilitates the identification of the
polysulfides by avoiding the overlapping peaks from Na3PS4. The
formation of diverse intermediates underscores the critical role of
diffusion kinetics and pressure in dictating reaction pathways.
Due to the ubiquitous presence of carbon, S–C interactions
strongly influence sulfur and its solid–solid conversion. Given
the electronic insulating nature of sulfur, carbon has been intro-
duced to create S/C composites that exhibit enhanced electronic
conductivity and reactivity [56–65] through various methods,
including mechanical mixing [61], liquid-phase infusion (melting
sulfur at 150–160◦C into the micropores of carbon), and vapor-
phase deposition (sublimation of sulfur above 300◦C) [57, 63].
The fabrication process of S/C composites allows for the tailoring
of sulfur morphology based on the microstructure of the host
carbon, as molten or vaporized sulfur can diffuse into the carbon
framework, adsorb, and recrystallize [62, 64]. Classical molecular
dynamics simulations have predicted that this encapsulation of
sulfur induces the ring opening of S8 molecules, facilitating their
transformation into short-chain structures via interactions with
graphene edge sites [14, 66]. This process contributes to improved
reaction kinetics and sulfur utilization. Despite these advantages,
S/C interactions may also introduce potential trade-offs. Owing
to the mechanical mismatch between sulfur and carbon, the
large volume change of sulfur during cycling can induce stress
within S/C composites, potentially leading to microstructural
disruption and sulfur redistribution. Moreover, S@C composites
often employ high–surface-area carbons, which increase the
exposed carbon–electrolyte interfacial area and may accelerate
electrolyte decomposition. These considerations highlight the
need to balance electronic conductivity, sulfur reactivity and
confinement, and interfacial stability in the rational design of S/C
composite cathodes.
In liquid electrolyte systems, the effect of S/C composites on
sulfur conversion has been determined to be related with the
interaction between the carbon host and polysulfides [14, 27]. In
contrast, in solid-state configurations—where sulfur conversion
proceeds predominantly through kinetically controlled solid–
solid pathways—the specific role of S–C interactions remain less
well defined, in part due to the absence of thermodynamically
stable lithium polysulfide phases and the difficulty in resolving
structurally similar, poorly crystalline intermediates. Neverthe-
less, variations in S–C interactions provide an important oppor-
tunity to probe changes in solid-state sulfur conversion pathways
and to establish related structure–performance correlations. At
present, the effects of S–C interactions are most readily inferred
from apparent electrochemical metrics, such as sulfur utilization,
cycling stability, and the evolution of redox features in voltage
profiles, complemented by spectroscopic techniques including
X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS), Raman spectroscopy, and
X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The nature of S–C
interactions is highly dependent on carbon properties, including
carbon type (e.g., amorphous versus graphitic), morphology (e.g.,
dot, fibrous, or nanowire architectures), specific surface area,
defect density, and heteroatom doping. In addition, the S/C
ratio governs sulfur distribution on carbon surfaces and thereby
modulates interfacial interactions. It is acknowledged that high-
surface-area carbons, such as Ketjen black (KB) and acetylene
black (AB), were commonly used, as they exhibit higher reactivity
compared to vapor-grown carbon fibers (VGCF) [67], whereas
reports utilizing graphitic carbons remain scarce. Systematic
studies are still required to establish clear correlations among S-
C interactions and sulfur solid-state conversion behavior, which
will be essential for guiding the rational design of sulfur cathodes
in practical all-solid-state lithium–sulfur batteries.

In most SSEs, initial decomposition produces Li3PS4, Li2S, and
other phases, which may subsequently undergo further redox
[12, 16, 71]. Li2S serves as the main active species, explaining
the reversible capacity demonstrated in SSE–carbon composites
[21, 25]. In practice, decomposition is kinetically hindered
because both the SSEs and many of their products (Li2S, LiCl,
elemental P) are electronically insulating [22]. Early Li/SE/Pt
measurements suggested wide stability windows (0–5 V), but
this overestimated true stability due to limit interfacial contact
and negligible electron transport [69, 71]. Introducing conductive
carbon enlarges the electrochemically active interfacial area,
accelerating decomposition and making it detectable [69, 71].
This implies that redox primarily occurs at SSE–carbon interfaces
rather than uniformly in the bulk. As a result, in practical Li–
S and Na–S solid-state cells, carbon dispersion and connectivity
strongly influence measured capacity, which reflects contribu-
tions from both the nominal active material (S or Li2S) and
the redox-active SSE. Importantly, partial overlap between SSE
redox and sulfur redox complicates quantitative separation of
their individual electrochemical roles. Moreover, SSE redox is
intrinsically coupled with electrolyte degradation, leading to a
progressive decrease in effective ionic conductivity.
The contribution of SSE redox in sulfur composite cathodes is
commonly evaluated using SSE–C model electrodes. As sum-
marized in Supporting Table S1, typical sulfide SSEs exhibit
reversible capacities of ∼300 mAh g−1, corresponding to ∼150
mAh g−1 when incorporated into cathode composites containing
50 wt.% SSE. In S–SSE–C cathodes, differential capacity (dQ/dV)
and cyclic voltammetry (CV) analyses reveal characteristic SSE-
derived features, with oxidation typically observed at near ∼2.8 V
and a reduction feature near at ∼1.0 V.
Transport degradation associated with SSE–carbon interac-
tions can be quantified by transmission-line-model analysis of
impedance spectra [80] or by direct current polarization measure-
ments using electron-blocking electrodes [82]. SSE–C interac-
tions induce progressive electrolyte decomposition accompanied
by reduced effective ionic conductivity. Although reductive SSE
degradation can deliver an apparent high initial capacity, it gener-
ally exhibits poor capacity retention, consistent with irreversible
electrolyte breakdown.
The effect of SSE–C interactions in S–SSE–C cathodes further
depends on electrolyte composition and carbon characteristics.
While most sulfide SSEs decompose into electronically insulat-
ing products, SSEs containing transition metals such as Ti or
W may form ionic/electronic conductive sulfide phases (e.g.,
TiS2 [83] or WS2 [16]), which can enhance charge and mass
transport at the sulfur–SSE–carbon three-phase interface, thereby
improving sulfur reactivity and reversibility. Carbon morphology

4
S/Solid-State Electrolyte Interactions
4.1
During Cathode Fabrication
Mechanical milling (Figure 3a) is a common method for prepar-
ing composite sulfur cathodes with intimate contact between
S, SSE, and conductive carbon. The intense mechanical forces
generated during milling reduce particle size, expose fresh
surfaces, and can induce mechanochemical reactions [7, 87].
For sulfur, local temperature at the contact points may exceed
200◦C, promoting ring-opening reactions and the formation
of sulfur radicals [7, 61]. For sulfide SSEs, milling enhances
amorphization [88] and increases reactivity, which may accelerate
their decomposition during subsequent cycling [7, 88, 89].
Mechanochemical reactions between S and argyrodite-type SSEs
such as Li6PS5Cl have been reported, yielding polysulfidophos-
phate intermediates (Li3PS4+n, Figure 3b) [11, 52] and in situ–
segregated LiCl layers (Figure 3c) [12]. The formation of Li3PS4+n
improves interfacial contact between sulfur and the electrolyte
[11, 52], while LiCl layers enhance ionic transport and help
accommodate the volumetric changes of chalcogen cathodes [12].
Ball milling sulfur, AB, and Na3PS4 (NPS) induces interfacial
reactions between NPS and sulfur, although the specific reaction
products have not yet been conclusively identified [53]. Compared
with mild mixing, high-energy milling generally improves S
utilization and conversion efficiency [10, 51, 53, 58], but may
also introduce pre-existing interfacial phases that complicate the
electrochemical pathway.
Solution-assisted processing (Figure 3d) provides an alternative
route for forming S/SSE composites [90–92]. In lithium–sulfur
systems, S and Li2S can react in solvents at moderate temperatures
(50–70◦C) to form polysulfides, and S radicals can be stabi-
lized in high-donor-number solvents such as dimethyl sulfoxide
(DMSO) via S–S bond homolytic cleavage [3, 93]. In 1884, sodium
polysulfide was synthesized from sulfur and Na2S in a water-
ethanol environment [94]. Solvent-mediated mechanisms have
been exploited to synthesize sulfide SSEs (e.g., Li3PS4 [95],
Li7P3S11, LGPS, LPSCl [96]). Organic solvents such as tetrahydro-
furan (THF), acetonitrile (ACN), and dimethoxyethane (DME)
can promote the dissolution and reaction of precursors, offering
a versatile liquid-phase alternative to conventional high-energy
mechanical milling routes [97].

When applied to the preparation of sulfur composite cathodes,
solvent-mediated interactions not only promote uniform mixing
of components but also induce interfacial reactions between
sulfur and the electrolyte (Figure 3e,f). For example, S and
Li3PS4 can react in THF at room temperature to yield lithium
polysulfidophosphate after solvent removal [86], whereas wet
mixing S with LPSCl in weakly polar isopropyl acetate can trigger
beneficial interfacial chemistry and enhance the wettability of
the electrolyte toward S8 [11]. However, solvent molecules that
coordinate with the electrolyte cannot be completely removed
during the drying process [95].
4.2
During Electrochemical Process
As shown in Figure 4, during cycling, the interfacial chemistry
between sulfur and SSEs is governed by the co-evolution of
both components. Sulfur undergoes S–S bond cleavage, chain-
length shortening, and eventual conversion to Li2S (Na2S),
while the SSE can electrochemically decompose into Li3PS4
(Na3PS4), Li2S (Na2S), Li3P (Na3P), halides, and other secondary
phases. The in situ reaction of lithiated sulfur with P2S5 to
form Li3PS4 (Na3PS4) has already been demonstrated [76–79,
98]. These transformations often occur concurrently, with pre-
existing interfacial phases formed during electrode fabrication
further evolving under electrochemical bias, leading to dynam-
ically changing interfaces. The S reaction pathways are highly
sensitive to kinetic conditions—current density and temperature
can determine which radical or polysulfide intermediates emerge,
and these species may display reactivity distinct from both S8
and Li2S. Decomposition products of electrolytes such as Li2S,
LiCl, or P-rich phases may function as ionic or electronic insu-
lators, thereby modulating sulfur redox kinetics and reversibility.
Furthermore, large volume changes during (de)lithiation impose
localized interfacial stresses that can alter S reaction pathway
[55] and even extend the apparent electrochemical stability
window of the SSE [99–101]. Chemical modification of either
the sulfur or the SSE—such as halogen or metal-ion doping—
introduces additional variables, potentially tuning interphases
composition [102], or introducing catalytic activity [102–104],
ultimately shaping sulfur conversion kinetics and long-term
stability.
5
All-Solid-State Li–S and Na–S Batteries
Sulfur’s inherently low electronic conductivity and ∼80% volume
change upon (de)lithiation impose severe constraints on ASSSBs
[105]. Presently, as summarized in Supporting Table S2 and Sup-
porting Table S3, stable long-term cycling at high sulfur loading
(>30 wt.% S or > 3 mg cm−2) and low stack pressure remains
elusive. Most reported electrodes rely on excessive SE and con-
ductive carbon to improve S utilization, reducing electrode-level
capacities to <300 mAh g−1, with part of the capacity potentially
originating from SE decomposition. Moreover, stack pressures
exceeding 30 MPa—often >100 MPa—are required, precluding
practical application.
Building on previous analysis, advancing ASSLSBs requires
deliberate modifications of sulfur, SSE, and conductive additives.
Central to these efforts is tuning the intrinsic properties of sulfur
and the coupled S–C, SSE–C, and S–SSE interactions to rationally
engineer the S–SSE–C three-phase interface. Promising strategies
include: i) enhancing the intrinsic conductivity and reactivity
of sulfur, ii) tailoring carbon additives, iii) modifying SSE com-
positions, and iv) incorporating mixed conductive components.
Ultimately, progress may hinge on tailoring the interactions
between sulfur and the SSE.

For sulfur modification, as mentioned before, S/carbon com-
posites (Figure 5a) improve electron transport and reactivity
of S [56–65]. However, the influence of S–C interactions on
S–SSE interfacial chemistry remains largely unexplored. These
interactions are expected to depend strongly on the carbon
structure: ordered versus disordered domains exhibit distinct
affinities for sulfur, while sulfur deposition within micropores
enables nanostructuring, and defect sites can adsorb sulfur or
polysulfides. Moreover, heteroatom functionalization on carbon
surface, including single-atom Ni or Co sites with strong S affinity,
lowers activation barriers, and accelerates S conversion [104,
106]. Such interactions may, in principle, regulate the conversion
pathway and alter the distribution of polysulfide intermediates,
thereby influencing subsequent S–SSE interfacial reactions and
overall battery performance. Encapsulation of sulfur within car-
bon micropores not only nanostructures sulfur but also directly
modifies its contact with the electrolyte, tuning the extent and
nature of the S–SSE interaction.
Halogen doping (Figure 5b) provides a molecular-level conduc-
tivity boost: iodine-doped S9.3I crystals exhibit ∼5.9 × 10−7 S cm−1
at 25◦C (∼11 orders higher than S), promote reactive polysulfide
formation, and possess a low melting point (∼65◦C), enabling
thermal crack self-healing, thereby improving reversibility and
cycling stability. During electrochemical cycling, S9.3I generates
radical intermediates, which contribute to enhanced sulfur reac-
tivity and facilitate polysulfide conversion [105]. Incorporating
LiI (80Li2S–20LiI) or NaI (85Na2S–15NaI) increases the ionic con-
ductivity of Li2S or Na2S by more than two orders of magnitude,
enabling Li–S ASSBs to deliver 980 mAh g−1 at 2 C for 2000
cycles without capacity degradation [89], and Na–S ASSBs to
sustain stable cycling for over 100 cycles [107]. Mechanistically,
LiI-assisted catalytic activation favoring Li2S2 intermediate for-
mation, thereby improving reversibility and cycling stability [26].
While these findings highlight the catalytic role of halogens in
promoting sulfur conversion and free radical formation, their
impact on S–SSE interactions remain unclear and requires further
investigation. Transition metal sulfides (e.g., VS2 [108], TiS2
[102, 109], FeS2 [110–112], CuS, InS [113]) provide mixed ionic–
electronic conduction, contribute additional reversible capacity,
and buffer the large volume changes of sulfur. In most cases, they
form interphases between sulfur and the electrolyte, offering elec-
tronic conduction and thereby modulating S–SSE interactions,
though these effects remain to be further elucidated.

For SSE modification, the narrow electrochemical stability win-
dow of most sulfide electrolytes leads to decomposition during
cycling. Two main approaches have been pursued: i) Suppressing
decomposition (Figure 5d) – Li6PS5Cl has emerged as a bench-
mark SSE for sulfur cathodes due to its high ionic conductivity
and ready availability. Wide-bandgap carbon nitride–graphene
hosts have been introduced to restrict electronic transport at
the S/SE interface, suppressing decomposition and enabling a
capacity of ∼2.1 mAh cm−2 with 92% retention over 235 cycles
without impedance growth [23]. Comparing the electrochemical
behavior of modified electrodes (with suppressed decomposition)
to that of unmodified ones provides valuable insights into how
both pristine and decomposition-derived SE phases influence
sulfur conversion. However, since the referenced study did not
further characterize the sulfur conversion pathway, the pre-
cise role of SE decomposition in governing S–SSE interactions
remains to be elucidated. ii) Utilizing decomposition products
to tailor interphases – Tailored SE chemistries such as Li2S–
B2S3–P2S5–LiI (I2/I3
−-mediated ultrafast solid–solid sulfur redox
reaction, Figure 5c) [46], amorphous Li–Ti–P–S mixed ionic–
electronic conductors (expanded active interfacial area beyond
conventional triple-phase boundaries) [114], and Li6+xP1-xWxS5I
(WS2-contained ionic/electronic conductive interphases forma-
tion) [16] have been shown to enhance sulfur conversion kinetics.
By rationally tuning the electrolyte composition, the chemistry
of decomposition-derived interphases can be controlled, thereby
modulating S–SSE interactions and ultimately governing the
sulfur conversion pathway.
In summary, both sulfur and electrolyte modifications highlight
the central role of S–SSE interfacial chemistry in dictating
electrochemical performance. Strategies such as engineered S/C
composites, halogen doping, transition metal sulfide incorpora-
tion, and controlled SSE decomposition have all demonstrated the
potential to enhance conductivity, stabilize interfaces, and accel-
erate sulfur conversion. Nevertheless, these advances remain
fragmented, and the fundamental mechanisms governing S–SSE
interactions are still not fully resolved. This underscores the need
for an integrated framework that bridges materials design with
interfacial understanding.
6
Conclusion and Outlook
ASSSBs integrate high-capacity sulfur cathodes with highly con-
ductive sulfide solid electrolytes, offering a pathway toward high-
energy, safe, and sustainable electrochemical storage. However,
as revealed in this work, the coupled chemical–electrochemical
processes involving sulfur, conductive carbon, and sulfide elec-
trolytes fundamentally govern performance from electrode fab-
rication to long-term cycling. Sulfur’s intrinsically low conduc-
tivity, sluggish redox kinetics, and large volume change intersect
with the narrow electrochemical stability windows of sulfide
SSEs, producing complex and dynamic interphase chemistries
that dictate reversibility and rate capability.
Mechanistic insights obtained so far include:
Distinct solid–solid conversion pathways – In contrast to liquid
systems, solid-state cells exhibit direct S →Li2S/Na2S trans-
formations or polysulfides-mediated routes, strongly modulated
by temperature, current density, and pressure; the mechanistic
landscape remains incompletely resolved.
Concurrent SSE decomposition – Electrolyte breakdown accom-
panies sulfur redox, generating interfacial products, such as Li2S
(Na2S), LiCl, P-rich phases that may serve as additional active
materials, electronic/ionic conductors or isolators, catalytic sites,
or insulating barriers, thereby influencing reaction pathways and
reversibility.
Fabrication-induced preconditioning - High-energy milling and
solution processing can trigger interfacial reactions and phase
segregation before cycling, altering initial reaction pathways.
Dynamic interphase evolution – Electrochemical bias, mechan-
ical stress, and chemical reactivity jointly reshape interfaces
during cycling, with halide segregation and conductive decom-
position phases emerging as recurring motifs.
Avenues for deeper mechanistic exploration:
Development of advanced characterization – Because sulfur
is sensitive to high-vacuum environments, techniques such as
TEM are often challenging to apply directly [13, 37, 43]. In all-
solid-state Li–S batteries, sulfur ring-opening and polysulfide
formation typically yield poorly crystalline, glassy phases with
highly similar local structures, and no thermodynamically stable
lithium polysulfide phases exist. Moreover, many of the reaction
intermediates are likely transient in nature. These factors make
it difficult to unambiguously resolve the conversion process.
Progress will require characterization tools capable of probing
amorphous and metastable chalcogenide structures. Low-dose,
cryo-SEM/TEM [3], together with operando characterizations are
essential to unveil the real-time transition of these air-sensitive
materials and to decouple complex interactions. Time-resolved
X-ray/neutron scattering, spectro-microscopy, and strain map-
ping under realistic cycling conditions will be essential to
capture transient intermediates, monitor interphase evolution,
and correlate stress–reaction coupling in operando [19, 32, 55].
Deciphering chemo-mechanical coupling – The pronounced vol-
ume changes accompanying sulfur conversion (≈80% for S →Li2S
[105] and ≈160% for S →Na2S [4]), when accommodated within
solid electrolyte framework, gives rise to significant stress and
strain due to mechanical mismatch among sulfur, its conversion
products, and the SSE. The resulting stress evolution can pro-
gressively deteriorate solid–solid contact and transport pathways,
thereby influencing reaction kinetics, reversibility, and long-
term cell stability. Quantitative measurement of internal stress
evolution during cycling is therefore essential for disentangling
electrochemical and mechanical effects and for informing sulfur
cathode capable of maintaining stability under reduced external
pressure [115–117].
Using multiscale-modelling to bridge mechanics and chemistry
– Given that the exact crystal structures and intermediate phases
remain experimentally elusive, we turn to computational model-
ing and simulation. However, a critical gap exists between atomic-
scale Density Functional Theory (DFT) [118] and continuum-level
Finite Element Analysis (FEA). To address this, mesoscale mod-
eling (such as the Phase-Field Method) will be pivotal. It serves

Realizing practical ASSSBs—with high sulfur loading (>30 wt.%

(>1000 cycles), and minimal reliance on excess electrolyte or

Molecular-level
sulfur

–Modifying
S/Li2S
to
enhance intrinsic electronic conductivity and redox reactivity,
while elucidating the correlation between sulfur ring-opening
processes, the formation of radicals and polysulfides, and their
impact on electrochemical performance. Building on progress in
understanding solid-state sulfur conversion reactions, molecular
modification of S/Li2S to suppress volume expansion and
enable low-pressure operation is also critical for the practical
implementation of sulfur cathodes.
Chemical modification of electrolytes – SSEs provide the primary
Li+ transport pathways within sulfur cathodes, making their
chemical design critical for improving electrode kinetics and
stability. SSEs with high intrinsic ionic conductivity can signif-
icantly enhance the effective ionic conductivity of composite
cathodes. In addition, tuning electrolyte composition to modulate
SSE–sulfur interactions are essential for governing solid-state
sulfur conversion. The net effect of electrolyte decomposition is
governed by a delicate balance between enhanced local inter-
facial reactivity and compromised bulk ionic transport. While
electrolyte decomposition is typically associated with structural
degradation and a decline in ionic conductivity, its products can,
in some cases, play a non-negligible role in sulfur cathodes.
For example, the formation of S/Li2S may directly contribute
to the measured capacity, and the generation of mixed ionic–
electronic conducting phases can facilitate mass and charge
transport between sulfur and the electrolyte, thereby enhancing
sulfur solid-state conversion. From a mechanical perspective,
SSEs also act as structural frameworks that host sulfur and buffer
the stress–strain evolution associated with sulfur lithiation and
delithiation. Developing softer and more deformable electrolytes
is therefore essential to enhance cathode integrity and long-term
stability.
Optimizing electrode microstructure –Future microstructure
design must move beyond random agglomeration toward hierar-
chical architectures [120] that maximize the triple-phase interface
between active sulfur species, conductive carbon, and solid-
state electrolytes. Precise matching of particle sizes between
active materials and electrolytes is essential to shorten ion-
transport tortuosity, improve interfacial contact uniformity, and
enable homogeneous reaction propagation throughout the elec-
trode [52]. Meanwhile, mitigating the severe chemo-mechanical
degradation induced by sulfur’s large volume expansion requires
the integration of compliant, self-healing binders or void-
engineered frameworks capable of accommodating repeated
breathing stresses without disrupting ionic and electronic perco-
lation pathways or promoting parasitic side reactions.
By integrating intrinsic chemical understanding with S, sulfide
electrolyte and interactions between them, ASSSBs can transition
from laboratory demonstrations to commercially relevant sys-
tems, retaining the inherent safety and energy-density advantages
of the solid-state architecture while achieving the durability and
scalability required for grid and mobility applications.
